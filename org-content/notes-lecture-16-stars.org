* The four "fundamental" forces

Forces /defined/ by the second law of dynamics as the causes of changes
in momentum ($\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}=d\mathbf{p}/dt$), and they are a
central concept of classical and non-relativistic physics

*N.B.:* in quantum mechanics and relativity forces are mathematically
less easy to manipulate and a description of nature in the form of
conserved quantities such as energy, momentum, etc. is more
manageable).

We have already encountered several forces to construct physical
models of astronomical objects -- however not all forces are
"ontologically" the same!

** Apparent forces: manifestation of a non-inertial reference frame
Some forces, such as the centrifugal force or the Coriolis force are
/apparent/: they arise in non-inertial frames (e.g., a rotating frame)
as a consequence of the fact that the frame itself is accelerating.

*Example*: while you are going around a corner in your car, you may feel
pushed towards the outer-edge of the curve. This is because implicitly
you are considering the car as your reference frame, which is turning
\Leftrightarrow accelerating to change the velocity direction \Leftrightarrow non-inertial. From
the point of view of an inertial frame (e.g., a tree on the side of
the road), there is a centripetal force (the friction between the
wheels and the road, pointing towards the center of the curve) that is
forcing the car to turn, but that force does not act on you directly,
so you feel the car turning "under you" and perceive it as you being
pushed to the exterior.

Note that the fact these forces are /apparent/ just means we can find a
reference frame where they are not there (and usually the math
simplifies, so these are preferable), but it doesn't mean the /effect/
of these forces is not real: you do move around relative to your car
when you turn!

** "Non-fundamental" forces
These are forces that we introduce as a simplified model of something
more complex, that is they are an /effective model/ for something more
fundamental.

*Example*: The friction between the tires and the car! Friction is
caused by electromagnetic interactions between the atoms/molecules of
the tires and the asphalt, but we don't want to have to deal with an
extremely large number of molecules and calculate one-by-one all their
interactions: we can instead /define/ some parameter (the coefficient of
friction) and /calibrate/ it empirically to describe the change in
momentum of bodies "touching".

The concept of "touching", or in other words /contact forces/, itself is
an effective non-fundamental force: at microscopic level we are made
of atoms and molecules which are mostly empty. What we perceive as a
contact force is the electromagnetic repulsion between our mostly
empty component!

** "Fundamental" forces
The 19^{th} and 20^{th} century physics research was in large part
influenced by positivism, and searching for a "grand unification" of
all forces, that is a single theoretical framework that would explain
/all/ causes of changes of momentum.

*** Electromagnetic force
This started with [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell][James Clerk Maxwell]], who /unified/ electricity and
magnetism into /electromagnetism/, showing that one can be the source of
the other, and which you get depends on the reference frame used to
describe the phenomena (much like centrifugal vs. centripetal force
depend on the reference frame). The electromagnetic force is /mediated/
by photons: photons in classical mechanics are electromagnetic waves,
and exchange of photons allows for electromagnetic interactions
between charged particles.

*** Strong (nuclear) force
Another fundamental force is the /nuclear/ force, also called /strong/
force because it is much stronger than electromagnetism, although it
only works at very short distances (of the order of 10^{-15} m, the
typical size of a nucleus). This is what /enables nuclear reactions in
stars/, if $T$ is high enough that collisions bring nuclei close
enough, the electromagnetic repulsion loses to the nuclear force and
they can bind together. At particle-level it is mediated by /gluons/.

*** Gravity
Gravity is instead much weaker than electromagnetic forces, which
allows solids to bind together without collapsing under their own
weight. It is the first /fundamental/ force studied by Newton, and it
remains irreducible to other forces still today.

*N.B.:* The most modern theory of gravity that fits all the experimental
constraints that we have, General relativity, considers gravity a
manifestation of the intrinsic "shape" of space-time bent by masses!
We do not yet have a /quantum/ theory of gravity, so at particle level
there is a /theoretical/ particle, the /graviton/ which can be constructed
by analogy with other particles.

*N.B.:* The unification of gravity, for which the current best theory is
general relativity, which is a /classical/ (as opposed to quantum)
theory, with quantum mechanics is a capstone problem for modern
physics.

*** Weak force
The last "fundamental" force is the /electroweak/ force, or simply /weak/
force. This is /weak/ respect to the strong, electromagnetic, and
gravitational forces, and usually involves changes in /flavor/ of
leptonic particles (electrons, muon, and tau particles, differing in
mass but all with the same charge), which produce /neutrinos/.

All other forces that appear in physics are /effective models/ that can
be explained in terms of these 4 forces, and many people still work on
trying to finding a /unifying/ framework for all these fours to reduce
the number of fundamental forces.

*N.B.:* This is an example of a /reductionist/ approach, trying to explain
all phenomena with as few pieces as possible. 20^{th} century discoveries
such as deterministic chaos, emergent phenomena in complex system,
non-linear systems also have revealed that often complex physical
systems (e.g., the atmosphere of a planet, or a star, etc.) are not
just the sum of their parts and reductionist approaches risk missing
interesting and relevant phenomenology.


* Solar neutrinos

We have encountered neutrinos in discussing the Sun's energy
generation. These particles are the smoking gun of the /weak force/.
Neutrinos are subatomic particles first theoretically predicted by
Wolfgang Pauli in the 1930s based on the fact that in the decay of
neutrons $n\rightarrow p +e^{-}$ energy was apparently not conserved.

#+CAPTION: Free neutron decay as conceptualized before the idea of neutrinos
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[./images/Free_neutron_decay_no_neutrino.png]]

#+CAPTION: Energy distribution of the electron (\beta^{-} particle) from the decay of free neutrons. Instead of consistently get one energy equal to the transition energy, a distribution was observed. Pauli proposed that the transition energy was distributed between the electron and an unseen particle, the neutrino.
[[./images/Beta_decay_spectrum.png]]

*N.B.:* the half-life of a /free/ neutron $n$ is about 15 minutes, and
they decay into protons, electron and an anti-neutrino $\bar{\nu}$ to
conserve charge, energy, and leptonic number. This spontaneous decay
mediated by the weak force does not occur has easily for neutrons
bound in nuclei.

Instead of abandoning the principle of conservation energy, Pauli
proposed that the decay of the neutron produced not 2 but 3 particles:
$n\rightarrow p+e^{-} +\bar{\nu}$ with the third being hard to detect.

:Quote:
/I have done a terrible thing. I have postulated a particle that
cannot be detected/ - W. Pauli.
:end:

#+CAPTION: Free neutron decay applying conservation laws for number of nucleons (1 neutron becomes 1 proton), charge (1 proton and 1 electron cancel out), and leptonic number (1 electron and one anti-neutrino cancel out).
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[./images/Free_neutron_decay_complete.png]]



However, in the mid-50s, neutrinos produced by a nuclear power-plant
were [[https://icecube.wisc.edu/neutrino-history/1956/01/1956-first-discovery-of-the-neutrino-by-an-experiment/][detected by Cowan and Reines]]: the proximity to the source made
the neutrino flux sufficiently high (\sim10^{12}-10^{13} neutrinos s^{-1} cm^{-2}).
Since then, neutrinos from astrophysical sources (stars, stellar
explosions, and jets) and have been detected, at various energies, and
provide information complementary to photons since they hardly
interact with matter.

On Earth, the main source of neutrinos is the Sun: by measuring their
energy, we could even recently /directly/ confirm specific nuclear
reactions occurring in the Sun's interior, strongly and directly
confirming the picture of energy generation we have discussed [[./notes-lecture-15-sun.org][last
lecture]]. Neutrinos play also a crucial role in the late evolution of
massive stars, and their explosions, as we will see.

Typically, since neutrinos interact so little with matter, to detect
them one needs /a lot of matter/ to maximize the chances of an
interaction. Historically this was done by [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestake_experiment][filling underground
abandoned mines with detector material]]. In the late 60s, [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Davis_Jr.][Raymond Davis
Jr.]] detected the flux of neutrinos from the Sun, but it was 1/3 of the
predicted neutrino flux! This was dubbed the "missing solar neutrino
problem", and initially the community thought either the theoretical
prediction or the experimental apparatus were wrong.

*N.B.:* Because science is a collective enterprise, there is a sociology
of science and the community decides collectively what are the
important problems to work on. This per se is /not/ a scientific
(quantitative, based on empirical evidence) process, but it shapes the
way scientific communities work. To learn more about this aspect, I
highly recommend [[https://archive.org/details/structureofscie000kuhn]["History of Scientific Revolutions"]] by Thomas Kuhn.

For decades, this problem remained open: the experiment was repeated
and the result confirmed, the theoretical predictions were double
checked, with nothing pointing to errors. Ultimately, the solution
required understanding neutrinos /beyond/ the predictions of the
standard model! Astrophysics pushed us beyond the standard model of
particle physics.

The key are /neutrino oscillations/: in nature there are three species
of neutrinos (electron, lepton, and muon) corresponding to the three
leptons (electron, and its more massive "cousins" muon and tau
particles). Each of these 6 particles also has an antiparticle with
same mass, opposite charge and opposite "leptonic charge". The
neutrino-producing processes in a star like the Sun preferentially
produce electron-flavor neutrinos and anti-neutrinos (*N.B.:* "flavor"
is the real technical term!), which are also the easiest to detect,
and the predictions were based on this fact.

#+CAPTION: ChatGPT-generated artist impression of neutrino oscillations in between the Sun and the Earth, explaining the solution to the missing solar neutrino problem.
[[./images/neutrino_osc.png]]


However, it turns out that the standard model of particle physics
(built on quantum mechanics and decades of experiments in particle
accelerators) is missing a key piece: neutrinos can "change flavors"
as they travel from the Sun to Earth. By the time they arrive on
Earth, about 2/3 of the expected electron neutrinos are going to have
switched to muon or tau neutrinos, which the detectors then miss!
These /neutrino oscillations/ are the reason why the leptonic number is
/not/ a precisely conserved quantity (there is no exact symmetry of
nature corresponding, only an approximate gauge symmetry).

Several Nobel prizes have been awarded for the detection of neutrinos,
development of the theory on how they interact with matter, their
oscillations, and even the The [[https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2026/10/press-physicsprize2026.pdf][2026 Nobel prize in physics to Francis
Halzen]] was awarded for the development of neutrino detectors which
require 1 km^{3} of ice to work!

#+CAPTION: IceCube experiment in the south pole uses 1km^{3} of Ice to detect neutrinos, the Principal Investigator (PI) of this experiment F. Halzen was awarded the Nobel price in physics this month.
[[./images/icecube.jpg]]

Other experiments use large volumes of sea water, or huge tanks of
water polluted with elements that are more likely to react with
neutrinos deep in mines (e.g., at the famous Kamiokande mines in Japan).

* Stars other than the Sun

After this digression on forces in physics and solar neutrinos, let's
return to stars. We have discussed a bit about the Sun, but clearly
there are many other stars in the Universe, which can have different
properties than the Sun.

#+CAPTION: /Hubble space telescope/ image of the inner portion of the cluster \Omega Centauri.
[[./images/omega_cen.jpeg]]

These are much father than the Sun (recall that proxima centauri is $\sim
1\,\mathrm{pc}\simeq 4\,\mathrm{light\ years} away), and we only see them
as point-sources with very few nearby exceptions. How can we learn
about their properties?

** Luminosity $L$ vs. brightness $B$

We have seen that the luminosity $L$ of the Sun, that is its rate of
energy losses to photons at the surface is a crucial property, which
sets its lifetime. This is an intrinsic property of the star, but not
one we can directly empirically measure: we only measure photons in a
certain wavelength range, and we are at a given distance, so we don't
get the intrinsic rate of energy loss from a star, but just the
/brightness/ of the star seen from our position and in the limited range
of frequencies we observe (filter band pass).

How are these related? We can use /conservation of energy/ to find out:
the luminosity is an energy loss per unit time from the star. Once
this energy is lost, as light travels outward, energy has to stay
constant. As the distance from the star increases, the constant energy
is spread over a bigger surface! Therefore, in each time interval:

#+BEGIN_latex
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:brightness}
    B = \frac{L}{4\pi r^{2}} \ \ ,
  \end{equation}
#+END_latex

where $B$ is the brightness, $L$ is the intrinsic luminosity, and $r$
is the distance to the star. If we know $B$ (observed) and $r$, we can
derive the intrinsic luminosity $L$.

#+CAPTION: Energy conservation, spherical symmetry, and geometric constraints ($A=4\pi r^{2}$) lead to the $B\propto r^{-2}$ relation.
[[file:images/flux_cons.png]]

*N.B.:* The power of the distance appearing in Eq. \ref{eq:brightness}
is the same as for the Universal law of gravity, and for the
electromagnetic force: this is /not/ a coincidence, they all appear due
to the geometry of a sphere and conservation arguments.


** Distance

Clearly then, we need to know the distance to the stars to be able to
derive a fundamental intrinsic property: how luminous they are! Note
that the distance to the stars /remains/ a key problem in astrophysics
since the ancient Greek arguments about geocentrism due to the lack of
parallax in the stars, and it is still topical as measuring the
distance to stars is still the first step in measuring cosmological
distances!

We have already seen a /geometric/ method to measure stars, which was
known in antiquity, but unobservable by naked eye: the parallax
method. Galileo's discovery that stars not-visible by naked eye are
outnumbering naked-eye stars suggested that maybe stars are too far to
detect it, but with the improvements in telescope precision and
accuracy, we now routinely can detect stellar parallax!

Nowadays, we have developed two space-telescoped dedicated to
measuring parallax without interference from the atmosphere: [[https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Hipparcos_overview][Hypparcos]]
which flew in the 90s, and [[https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia_overview][Gaia]] which recently completed the data
acquisition phase, and will release the next batch of data in
December 2026.

At this point, people started to classify stars based on the
qualitative morphology of their spectra (which lines are present,
which are more dominant, etc.), and defining qualitative classes A, B,
C,...based on features such as the prominence of Balmer lines of
Hydrogen.

However, it was not yet known why different stars had different
spectra and belong to a certain class. As the number of observations
increased, the classes were reordered and merged, leaving the current
ones listed above after the work by [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_Jump_Cannon][Annie Jump Cannon]] together with
other "Harvard computers", a group of mostly women who analyzed and
made sense of stellar spectra in the Early 20^{th} century.

With the construction of the color-magnitude diagram it was realized
that these qualitative spectral classes were mostly determined by
temperature - from O for the hottest stars to M for the coolest (L and
T are classes introduced later for are classes introduced later for
Brown Dwarves). This is because the temperature of the gas determine
the electron populations in each ion/atom in the stellar atmosphere
and thus how many and which lines can be seen, and ultimately the
[[./notes-lecture-kappa.org][opacity]]. This is how this OBAFGKM(LTY) order came to be.

*** Relation with colors
On the /Gaia/ color-magnitude diagram, the color index and both the spectral
types and effective temperature are all on the x-axis (bottom and top,
respectively). How are these related?

Depending on T_{eff}, the maximum flux of a black body (i.e., of the
"continuum") shifts in wavelength, something known as "Wien's
displacement law": \lambda_{max}\prop 1/T, the higher the temperature the
shorter the wavelength of the maximum flux. This in turn means that
the change in T_{eff} translates in a change in the "color" of the star,
because the wavelengths around \lambda_{max} will dominate the perception of
the star (originally for naked eye observations, but this can be
generalized).

This can further be formalized by defining a "color index" as the
difference in flux between different photometric bands. Each band has
a transmission function T(\lambda) which is non-zero over a certain
wavelength range, for example these are the bands from /Gaia/'s
telescope used to make the diagram above:

** Why do 90% of stars fall on one line ? \Rightarrow one-parameter family

This is typically a very hard problem: as we have seen, on the HRD/CMD
there is a tight correlation between a star's magnitude and color.
This holds for the majority (>90%) of stars, and thus for the longest
phase of evolution. The linear relation between color and magnitude
(that is effective temperature and luminosity) suggests there is one
single parameter that determines the position on a star on this
diagram. However, how do we determine what this parameter is, having
only these observational data? It is generally not possible without
relying on models. However, binaries can help!

*** Binaries are common

The Sun is clearly not in a binary system. However, it is *not* uncommon
for stars to have gravitationally bound companion(s) orbiting around
them, which can be of great help to determine their properties.

In fact, in this case we have two stars presumably born at roughly the
same time, presumably with the same initial composition, and we can
derive physical properties comparing them to each other.

*N.B.:* At zeroth order of approximation (neglecting the impact of
irradiation), from the dynamical perspective, a planet+star system is
also a binary, and many of the classical techniques used to find
planets come from the study of binary systems!

Let's review some types of binaries.

**** Visual binaries

These are binaries that can be resolved (i.e., one can identify two
/distinct/ point sources) by naked eye. Typically, this means
astrophysically very wide binaries that are unlikely to interact
beyond the fact that they are gravitationally bound and they orbit
each other. Nevertheless, they have an important historical role,
since Hershel observation of their motion of the sky over decades
proved that Kepler's law are valid beyond the Solar system.

[[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1767RSPT...57..234M/abstract][Mitchell 1767]] also used statistical argument that all the visual
binaries visible at the time could not be just chance alignment, and
instead had to be somehow physically related to each other.

A classic example are Mizar & Alcor in the Big Dipper: the ability of
finding the "double star" in this asterism was used as an eyesight
test to become an astronomer in Hellenistic times. The name Alcor
comes from the arabic for "hard to see"! Nowadays with modern
telescopes we can see that Mizar is itself a quadruple system made of
two binaries orbiting each other, and Alcor may itself be a binary,
for a total of maybe 6 stars (e.g., [[https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0004-6256/139/3/919][Mamjek et al. 2010]])!

**** Astrometric binaries

Wide binaries where one of the two stars is too faint to be seen can
still be found by looking at the peculiar motion of the star that can
be seen on the sky. What matters is its /proper motion/ (not the
apparent motion due to the Earth rotation around its axis and
revolution around the Sun) with respect to background
stars too far to be moving. For a single star, this is usually a
straight line (because the timescale for it to turn around the
Galactic center is typically \sim hundreds of millions of years).
However, if the star is in a binary, what moves along a straight line
is the center of mass of the binary! The /photocenter/ (i.e. the point
from where we can detect light) will "wiggle" around this straight
line due to orbital motion (and on top of the parallax periodic
wiggle).

This was first detected for the binary Sirius A and Sirius B by Bessel
in 1844 (the detection of photons from Sirius B followed in 1862 by
Clark). Nowadays, this is a very timely because of the precise
astrometric measurements by /Gaia/:

- this has been used to find /Gaia/ BH1 ([[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.1057E/abstract][El Badry et al. 2023a]]), BH2
  ([[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.4323E/abstract][El Badry 2023b]]), and BH3 ([[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A%26A...686L...2G/abstract][Gaia Collaboration, Panuzzo et al. 2024]]):
  these systems have stars orbiting around an otherwise undetectable
  Black Hole! With the upcoming /Gaia/ DR4 data release, possibly 100s
  of astrometric binaries hosting a BH are expected (see e.g.,
  [[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..107C/abstract][Chawla et al. 2022]])
- it can also be used to find stars in wide orbits around NS or other
  faint stars, and possibly even massive planets!

***** SB1
In a spectrum, one star of the binary dominates, but we can see its
orbital motion as Doppler shifting of the spectral lines. Even if we
never see the less bright star, we can infer its presence from the
orbital motion of the brighter star.

*N.B.:* Radial velocity surveys that find massive and close-by planet
are effectively finding SB1 binaries made of a star and a planet!

***** SB2
These are spectroscopic binaries where both stars are contributing
light to the spectrum, meaning there will be two sets of spectral
lines that are periodically Doppler shifted in couter-phase.

Recently, "spectral disentanglement" has become an important technique
to reveal the presence of light from a companion in systems initially
interpreted as SB1 binaries, effectively turning SB1 binaries into an
SB2. The idea is to take a best-fit single-star spectrum for the
signal, and look for periodic variations in the residuals, indicating
contribution to the spectrum from a star /not/ fitted (see for example
[[https://github.com/TomerShenar/Disentangling_Shift_And_Add][this code]] and references listed there). This technique has been used
to "kill claimed BHs" in SB1 binaries revealing light coming from the
hypothesized BH, which usually has been reinterpreted as a star which
has lost its hydrogen-rich envelope to binary interactions.

**** Eclipsing binaries
These are binaries when at some point in the orbit one star passes in
front of the other. This blocks some of the light from one of the two
stars causing an eclipse and a dip in the light curve (i.e., the plot
of the luminosity of the system as a function of time).

This of course can only happen if the inclination of the orbital plane
of the binary w.r.t. the line of sight is not too large. Typically,
astronomers define the inclination of the orbital plane /i/ w.r.t. the
plane of the sky, therefore the line-of-sight (which is perpendicular
to the plane of the sky by definition) has an angle \varphi = \pi/2 - /i/ w.r.t.
the orbital plane. To have an eclipse, at least partial,

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
\sin(\varphi) \leq \frac{R_{1} + R_{2}}{a} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

where we use that sin(\varphi)=cos(i), and a \times cos(i) is the projected
binary separation a on the plane of the sky, which is smaller than R_{1}+R_{2}
with R_{i} stellar radii, leads to an eclipse. The eclipse will be total
if

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
\sin(\varphi) \leq \frac{R_{1} - R_{2}}{a}
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

Note that in either case, the occurrence of an eclipse puts an
upper limit on sin(\varphi), that is a direct observational constrain on the
inclination angle i.

The time duration of an eclipse can be used to determine the radii of
the two stars. Consider the following figure:

#+CAPTION: Light cuve (visual magnitude as function of time) for an eclipsing binary with i=\pi/2, figure 7.9 in Carrol & Ostie's book
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 100%
[[./images/EB.png]]


one can see that, labeling with 2 the geometrically smaller star
(darker gray) and assuming the orbit to be circular and the semi-major
axis to be a and the period P:

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
 \frac{t_{d} -t_{a}}{P} = \frac{2(R_{1}+R_{2})}{2\pi a} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

and

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
 \frac{t_{c} -t_{b}}{P}_{} = \frac{2(R_{1}-R_{2})}{2\pi a} \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

These equations allow to calculate R_{j}/a and if also the masses are
known also the individual radii R_{j} using Kepler's laws to get rid of
the orbital semi-major axis a.

*** Dynamical mass determination
**** Visual binaries
Using Kepler's laws, we can use binaries to measure observationally
masses without relying on hard, expensive, and uncertain stellar
models.

In particular the third law can be written as

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Kepler3}
   G(M_{1}+M_{2}) = n^{2} a^{3} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

where M_{i} are the masses of the stars, n= 2\pi/P is the angular velocity
(a.k.a. "true anomaly") and a is the semimajor axis of the orbit (of the
orbit of the reduced mass point \mu = M_{1}M_{2}/(M_{1}+M_{2}) around the center of
mass).

*N.B.:* Often for solar-system application M_{2} \ll M_{1} = M_{\odot}, so M_{2} can
be neglected (e.g., if M_{2} = M_{\oplus}). This is generally /not/ the case
with star+star binaries.

For a visual binary (see above) where we can measure the semi-major
axes a_{i} of both stars around the center of mass, we also have another
equation that follows from the definition of center of mass:

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:semimajor_axes}
 M_{1} a_{1} = M_{2} a_{2} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

where a_{1} + a_{2} = a. If we know the distance d to the binary (d \gg a, so
we can consider both stars at the same distance), then the angular
size of the ellipse that each stars traces on the orbit of the sky is
\alpha_{j} = a_{j}/d for j=1,2. In reality, the orbit is typically not on the
plane of the sky, but it has an inclination! This means what we can
see is not a_{j} but the projection of it on the plane of the sky a_{j}
sin(i). We can then solve these two equations for the individual
stellar masses (just having observations long enough to trace the
semi-major axes of wide visual binaries and knowing their distances,
for example because we have parallax measurements).

*N.B.:* even without knowing the distance d, using
Eq. \ref{eq:semimajor_axes} one can still determine the mass ratio q=M_{2}/M_{1}!

**** Spectroscopic (and eclipsing) binaries
In this case, we have access to more information thanks to the
variable "radial velocity/ies" (RV) of the stars, that is the
measurable Doppler shift of their spectral lines due to the orbital
motion.

However, what we can see though this is only the /projected/ orbital
motion along the line of sight (because we only get Doppler shift
along the line of sight), this is why the terminology is "radial
velocities": it's velocities along the radii of the celestial sphere
centered on the observer!

Following standard nomenclature, let's call z the direction of the
line of sight, using xy for the plane of the sky. By definition the
radial velocity of each star is dz/dt. \nu here is the true anomaly
(such that d\nu/dt = n), \omega is the argument of periastron - that is the
angle between the line of nodes (line where the plane of the sky,
assumed to contain the focus of the ellipse, and the plane of the
orbit intersect) and the direction of periastron.

#+CAPTION: Geometry of a binary orbit. This is Fig. 3.3 in Tauris & van den Heuvel 2023 book.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 100%
[[./images/bin_orbit.png]]


The one can calculate: z= r sin(i) sin(\nu+\omega) with r radius of the orbit
(which for an elliptical orbit is a function of time). The factor r
sin(\nu+\omega) gives the length of the segment OS in the figure (which lies
on the plane of the orbit which intersects the plane of the sky along
the direction AB), and the factor sin(i) projects it onto the line of
sight. The radial velocity is therefore:

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}\label{dzdt}
\frac{dz}{dt} = \left(r \cos(\nu+\omega)\frac{d\nu}{dt}+\frac{dr}{dt}\sin(\nu+\omega)\right)\sin(i) \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+end_latex
*N.B.:* For a Keplerian, non-precessing, unperturbed orbit, \omega is a constant.

Using Kepler's second law

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Kepler2}
\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\frac{d\nu}{dt} = \frac{\pi ab}{P} = \frac{\pi a^{2} \sqrt{1-e^2}}{P} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex
where P is the orbital period, a and b are semi-major and semi-minor
axes, and

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
e = \sqrt{1-b^{2}/a^{2}} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

is the eccentricity, and the relation between r\equiv r(\nu) for an ellipse:

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
r = \frac{a(1-e^{2})}{1+e\cos(\nu)} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

one can finally rewrite the radial velocity from Eq. \ref{dzdt} as

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vrad}
v_\mathrm{rad} = \frac{dz}{dt} = K (ecos(\omega)+cos(\omega+\nu)) \ \
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

where
#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
 K = \frac{2\pi a\sin(i)}{P\sqrt{1-e^{2}}} \ \,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex
is the so-called /semi-amplitude/ of the radial velocity, which apart
from the sin(i) factor depends only on observable quantities if one
can get spectra of many epochs (i.e., return to take spectra of the
same binary at many times, typically at least 7 spectra are required
for a decent fit to all these parameters):
 - P \rightarrow from the time interval between repeating identical spectra
 - e \rightarrow from the shape of the measured radial velocities as a function
   of time (i.e., radial velocity curve)
 - \omega \rightarrow similarly as e, it influences the shape of the radial velocity
   curve and can be deduced from it
 - $a\sin(i)$ \rightarrow if the binary is SB2, then we can measure $K_{1}\equiv K_{1}(a_{1})$
   and $K_{2 }\equiv K_{2} (a_{2})$ and use the relation between $a_{1}+a_{2} = a$ to work
   out asin(i)
 - \nu \rightarrow this is less directly obtained by performing an orbital fit
   using other orbital parameters (mean anomaly and eccentric anomaly)

*N.B.:* In a real observation, one also needs to remove the component
along the line of sight due to the motion of the Earth around the Sun,
and the peculiar motion of the Sun (i.e., $v_\mathrm{rad}$ from Eq. \ref{eq:vrad}
$\rightarrow v_\mathrm{rad}-\gamma$ with \gamma peculiar velocity of the observer).

Therefore, for an SB2 binary we can observe dz_{j}/dt = v_{rad, j} for
each j=1,2 star, and we can determine K_{1} and K_{2}. using Eq.
\ref{eq:Kepler3} multiplied by sin^{3}(i) to make the projection of
the semi-major axis of the orbit on the line of sight, a \times sin(i):

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
   G(M_{1}+M_{2})\sin^{3}(i) = n^{2} a^{3} \sin^{3}(i) \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

and using \ref{eq:semimajor_axes} rewritten as

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
  \frac{a_{1} \sin(i)}{a_{2} \sin(i)} = \frac{M_{2}}{M_{1}} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

we can solve for M_{j}sin^{3}(i), where except for the inclination angle of
the orbit, we have the masses! In the case of an /eclipsing SB2 binary/,
from the eclipses we can measure the inclination angle and obtain a
direct measurement of the masses. These are the most precise mass
measurements (so-called "dynamical masses", because they are based on
orbital dynamics). They are only possible thanks to the occurrence in
nature of stellar binaries!

*N.B.:* The highest stellar masses measured with these method are about
\sim80M_{\odot} in the eclipsing SB2 binary WR20a (e.g., [[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A%26A...420L...9R/abstract][Raauw 2004]],
[[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...611L..33B/abstract][Bonannos 2004]]).

In the case of an SB1 binary, when only one spectrum is visible, we
can write

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
a = a_{1} +a_{2} = a_{1}(1 + \frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}) = a_{1} (1 + \frac{M_{1}}{M_{2}}) = a_{1} \frac{(M_{1}+M_{2})}{M_{2}} \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

Thus, we can rewrite Eq. \ref{eq:Kepler3} \times sin^{3}(i) as

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
 (M_{1} +M_{2}) \sin^{3}(i) \frac{M_{2}^{3}}{(M_{1} +M_{2})^{3}} = \frac{n^{2}}{G}a_{1}^{3} \sin^{3}(i) \ \,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

where the r.h.s. depends only on observables, and thus this allow us
to derive observationally the mass function

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
f(M_{1}, M_{2}) = \frac{M_{2}^{3 }\sin^{3}(i)}{(M_{1} + M_{2})^{2}} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

which together with an estimate of the mass-ratio provides a limit on M_{2}.

sequence" stars, the one following also the L(T_{eff}) relatively tight
relation on the color-magnitude diagram: evolved stars are a different
problem!
