* Cosmology and the scientific method
Typically, empirically-grounded sciences rely on collective evidence with:
1. Lab experiments: controlled, repeatable setup.
2. Observations: possibly repeatable, uncontrolled (you take what you
   get). It is common to compensate for lack of control with large
   sample sizes, where a lot of "noise in the signal" averages to zero
   leaving cleaner information. Astrophysics often relies on
   astronomical numbers of objects!
What about Cosmology, the study of the Universe as a whole? It's not
exactly a lab we control. Is it observational, but the sample size is
1 Universe!

Things are not grim though, as the physical conditions that the
Universe passed through /as a whole/ at some point in life are similar
to conditions we can now find /locally/ in some places inside the
Universe (for example the temperature of the inside of a star, or the
interactions between particles at the energies of a particle
accelerator such as [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Hadron_Collider][LHC]]). We can leverage knowledge obtain in labs to
understand the physics.

Moreover, we have many independent ways to look at the one single
sample! Cosmology rests on galaxy observations earlier and earlier in
the Universe, how they are distributed across space time (e.g., [[file:notes-lecture-20-MW.org::*Measuring the mass of a galaxy: dark
 matter][Baryonic Acoustic Oscillations]]), and observations of the thermal
emission of the Universe's own /photosphere/ radiating black body
emission.

*N.B.:* Note how curiosity and organized collective methodological
skepticism work "around" the constraints of a problem if deemed
interesting!

* Big Bang Theory

Named such by [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Hoyle][Fred Hoyle]] in 1949 as a derogatory name for a theory he
found implausible.

If the Universe is expanding, in the past, it was smaller - more
compressed. Compressing a collection of particles (a gas) heats it up:
think of the cool sensation of air you breathe out, but in reverse!
So the Universe was hotter earlier

** Where was the big bang?
This is related to the eternal question of where is the center and are
we it that obsesses humans: the big bang corresponds to the emergence
of /spacetime/ itself (the [[file:notes-lecture-22-AGN.org::*The core idea of general relativity][general relativistic]] "object" that is bent by
masses), and where/when means a particular "point" or set of
coordinates in this "object" that acts as theater. There is no /where/
into which one can place the big bang: /all/ of space-time emerges at
once in a single event, with a total dimension that is small (smaller
than the Planck scale, see below), and started /expanding/. The
spacetime of he Universe as a whole, described by its /metric/ in GR
then /evolves/.

While this description may appear to many as /creationist/, cosmology is
not in the present day anymore concerned with the /why/ or /who/, and
physical theory stops being applicable at the Planck scale: we just
haven't yet developed the conceptual tools to address scientifically
that problem (and it is challenging to develop them without being able
to experiment at that scale!).

- Top Hat: Fundamental forces

The reality at the scale that our intuitive experience is based on,
there is a clear /separation of scales/ at which each [[file:notes-lecture-16-stars.org::*The four "fundamental" forces]["fundamental"
forces]] dominates:
- Gravity is $\propto G$ which is a "small" number: it only matters for
  large amount of mass, e.g., when talking about interactions with a
  whole planet.
- Electromagnetism is $\propto \alpha\simeq 1/137$, and it is the typically dominant
  force from planet-wide scale down to below atomic scale
  ($\sim10^{5}\,\mathrm{cm}$ to $\sim10^{-14}\,\mathrm{cm}$). For example,
  "touching" is feeling the electrostatic repulsion of molecules that
  all orient one particular way. EM also holds together crystalline
  solid structures, determines light-matter interactions, etc.
- At nuclear scale $\sim 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{cm}$ and below, the strong
  nuclear force takes over
- The weak force, is aptly named, and is typically not structurally
  important, but it plays a role in shaping the /composition/ of the
  Universe

However, how do thing works when the entire /space-time/ of the Universe
itself is at a scale where all force start to act at comparable
levels? The quest for a /grand unification/ theory looks to understand
physics in this conditions, expected in the very early Universe and
maybe at the center of BHs.

#+CAPTION: Going back in the evolution of the Universe, its entire scale is such that all forces work at comparable amount on matter!
[[file:images/forces_ratios.png]]

A quantum mechanical (QM) description of these interactions
(abandoning the Newtonian concept of force as "elementary") allows to
"unify" EM and weak force in a single mathematical framework which has
been tested in particle accelerators.

The strong force, while also described by quantum mechanics, cannot be
described with the same math (technically, it cannot be treated as a
/small perturbation/ causing a "wave" in a field). But we are sure from
nuclear physics that quantum mechanics is a useful description of
these interactions.

Gravity instead is /not/ described well by quantum mechanics (partly
because experimenting with gravity in a lab is challenging!). When
doing /gravitational/ problems one typically neglects the other forces,
and viceversa we neglect gravity when studying quantum mechanical
interactions. When /all/ the mass of the Universe was at a scale
governed by a mash of all the interactions we describe with QM
dominates, we do not know how the physics works. Studying quantum
gravity is a way to develop theoretical understanding for this regime.

*** Planck scale

Named after [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck][Max Plack]], who started QM with his theory of the thermal
emission of radiation by a body of finite temperature, it is the scale
at which /all forces/ contribute to determining the interactions in the
Universe.

We can estimate the size of the Universe when this was the case (or
equivalently "when" this was) from our understanding of the relative
"strength" of these forces:
#+BEGIN_latex
\begin{equation}
\ell =\sqrt{G\hbar}{c^{2}} \simeq 1.61\times10^{-35} \ \mathrm{m}$
\end{equation}
#+END_latex
Corresponding to a light crossing time $\ell/c\sim10^{-43}$ seconds.

Note however that we already know that most of the /mass/ in /matter/ of
the Universe (where matter means "stuff that feels gravity") is dark
matter, for which non-gravitational interactions may intervene at
small scales (we don't know how small). And there is also dark energy,
for which even less is known.

Nevertheless, our physical understanding is sufficient to understand
things pretty early on, and explain a lot of observed facts.
