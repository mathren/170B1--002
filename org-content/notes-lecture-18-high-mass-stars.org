** Main sequence evolution

Not only massive stars are more rare to form (cf. [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Initial mass function][Initial mass
function]] $IMF$), but their higher energy loss or radiative power
output $L$ - compared to lower mass stars - /requires/ an equally higher
$L_{\rm nuc}$ to sustain the gas pressure gradient against gravity.

#+CAPTION: Massive main sequence stars are rare (individual black dots vs. the yellow hue $\propto\rho_{\star}$) and occupy the top left corresponding to large $L$ and hot $T_{\rm eff}$
[[./images/Gaia_DR2_HRD_Gaia.png]]

We can empirically see from the color-magnitude diagram of the galaxy,
that massive stars are rare! They are also bluer and hotter.

Contributing to their rarity, a higher
$L_{\rm nuc}$ corresponds to a shorter lifetime: a star 10\times as massive as
the Sun only lives 20 million years (cf. \sim 10 billion for the Sun).

The bottom panel shows the deviations between the analytic fit and the numerical models.

[[./images/stellar_lifetimes.png]]

Because of the shorter lifespan, massive stars tend to live closer to
each other (while low mass stars have time to spread).

#+CAPTION: The [][30 Doradus region in the LMC] hosts about 1000 massive ($\gtrsim 10\,M_{\odot}$) stars and is a standard test bed for massive star evolution theory. This is where stars exceeding $100\,M_{\odot}$ are observed.
[[./images/30Dor_R136.png]]

However, stellar /explosions/ or gravitational slingshot
encounters in dense stellar clusters can accelerate stars in a galaxy,
even beyond the escape velocity from their own galaxy. These phenomena
preferentially accelerate massive stars, that can become /runaways/.

#+CAPTION: The nearest massive star to Earth is an O type star and travels through space at more than $\sim30\,\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}$, the runaway star  [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeta_Ophiuchi][$\zeta$ Ophiuchi]], which has a greek-letter name indicating it is visible by naked eye. The red shade is a bow shock in front of the star (supersonic analog of a bow wave in front of a boat moving faster than the sea wave speed).
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 75%
[[./images/zetaoph.jpg]]

These stars are hot at their surface, and their thermal radiation
peaks in the UV, but nevertheless so bright even in the optical, that
they outnumber the stars we can see the farther we go.

To sustain such high luminosities (dictated by the total mass $M$!),
they also need very hot cores which enables hydrogen burning through
the CNO cycle using carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen present from the
start as catalyst to make protons meet:

sensitive to the temperature: the burning region is more concentrated
than in lower mass stars.

to carry out the energy output by the CNO cycle without the help of
convection. But convection is bulk motion of matter which /refuels/
constantly the burning region.

** Prompt He ignition
At the end of the main-sequence though, we have a problem: H has been
consumed throughout the convective region, the outer layers of which
are way too cold to burn! Gravity temporarily takes over and the whole
star contracts: half of the gravitational potential energy released by
gas falling to the center goes into internal thermal energy of the gas
itself: electrons are too hot to "sit" in an energy level and fight
for the lowest energy seats and don't contribute to the pressure.

Instead, the center gets so hot that He ignites in the center
(smoothly, no [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Next fuel is hard to start: He Flash][He flash]]) /and/ the H shell ignites. The latter supports
the envelope, but Once again the shell is /not/ self-regulated: H burns
at the edge of the core, with the $T$ dictated there by the underlying
core mass and consequently structure. This results in an excess energy
production and thus the envelope expands: the star becomes a Red
Supergiant.

The nearest RSG to Earth is Betelgeuse, the shoulder of Orion:
#+CAPTION: Betelgeuse (left inset) is the nearest Red Supergiant, possibly in a binary system (based on its periodic variability unexplained otherwise), and far away from any other massive star: it is a runaway star, with a bow shock too! The other inset shows the star forming region Orion.
[[./images/orion3.jpeg]]

By counting the number of RSG and MS in a co-eval population (e.g., a
cluster), we can infer that RSG are \sim10% of massive stars: this
remains true across stellar masses and is dictated by the nuclear
structures of He and carbon C.


The expansion must be relatively fast (for stars, not for humans):
there are very few stars occupying the region of the CMD between the
main sequence and the red supergiants (RSG). However, looking where we
can see /more/ massive stars, for example LMC/SMC, there seem to be a
"Blue supergiant problem": we see many and don't fully understand why.

Possible ideas are:
- stellar mergers
- stars that have accreted mass from a companion
- stellar evolution is proceeding slightly differently in galaxies
  with different chemical composition than the Sun

** Onion structure
From now on, the fate of a single massive star is sealed: it will
consume each nuclear fuel available, each requiring a higher energy of
the collision between nuclei to enable quantum tunneling through the
electromagnetic barrier and fusing into new nuclei held together by
the nuclear force.

Most of the core energy loss since carbon core burning has been though
neutrinos produced by thermal processes (not from nuclear reactions):
massive evolved stars appear as RSG and are brighter in neutrinos than
photons!

#+CAPTION: Because of the higher $T$ requirement, each fuel burns in a smaller and more concentrated region, surrounded by a shell, leading to the characteristic "onion shell" structure.
[[./images/onion.png]]

For large but not so large $M\lesssim 15\,M_{\odot}$ the electrons will
sometimes contribute partially with their degeneracy pressure.

*N.B.:* Recent numerical simulations, and possible nucleosynthetic
signatures suggest that the "onion" picture could be an
oversimplification. Very late shell ignition in partially degenerate
environment can be relatively violent (akin to the He shell) and cause
"shell mergers". This could mix different nuclear fuels in a dynamical
event and allow the production of some elements like phosphorus
by ruining the nice layered onion.

The composition of the hashes of each fuel are (much like which fuel
is most efficient) determined by the nuclear structure of each element:

#+CAPTION: Nuclear energy per unit nucleon (proton or neutron) as a function of nuclear mass $A$. The structure of this plot is determined by the /strong/ (or nuclear) force.
[[./images/nuclear_binding.png]]

Nuclei that can be conceptualized as /bound states between groups of 2
protons and two neutrons/ tend to be more stable, meaning forming them
releases more energy: this is why nuclei with $A=4\times n$ with
$n=1,2,3,...$ correspond to peaks in the figure and are more common
elements in the Universe. Stars find it convenient to make them! Note
that these include Helium (whose nucleus is also referred to as $\alpha$
particle), Carbon 12 (3 $\alpha$ bound together), Oxygen 16 (4 $\alpha$),
Magnesium 24, etc...

*N.B.:* $\alpha$ nuclei are to nuclear physics what noble gases (He, Xe,
etc...) are to chemistry: tightly bound structures that release a lot
of energy (for their scale, 10 MeV for nuclear physics, eV for
chemistry) when formed, and are relatively stable.

But this game has an end: the curve in the figure has a "maximum".
There is a /most bound nucleus/, iron 56. All lighter /and/ heavier nuclei
are easier (takes less energy) to rob of one nucleon (proton or
neutron). As a consequence, trying to reshuffle the protons and
neutrons of $^{56}\mathrm{Fe}$ in another configuration always /costs/
energy (negative $\Delta y$ in the figure).

But the only reason there is nuclear fusion in /any/ star is that as gas
fights gravity it settles in a structure that is /hot/ in the center (to
sustain a pressure gradient), so /hot/ that gas taps into the nuclear
binding energy, compensating for the luminosity losses which enable
the collapse. In short: the burning occurs to compensate the losses.
If it costs energy to burn iron, it would only accelerate the collapse
and the gas has no further interest in doing it.

*N.B.:* it's a bit hard to track cause and effect because gravity, gas
structure, thermodynamics, and nuclear physics are coupled non-linearly.

*** Stellar winds & Wolf-Rayet stars
Massive stars are characterized by a very large $L$, this forces their
relatively short lifetimes (\sim 10 million years vs. 10 billion years
for a $1\,M_{\odot}$ star), and determines their burning mode through the CNO
cycle (as opposed to the pp chain dominating in lower mass stars).
A large luminosity also implies there are many photons that can
interact with the stellar atmosphere and /push the gas/. This can drive
a stellar wind through radiation pressure (as opposed to the stellar
wind which is gas-pressure driven).


#+CAPTION: Radiation can launch winds from the partially transparent stellar atmosphere.
[[file:images/winds.png]]

The stellar atmosphere which is /by definition/ partially transparent:
it is the layer of gas sitting above the location from where thermal
radiation from the stellar interior is starting to leak out almost
unimpeded by matter (/almost/!). So while photons still travel in any
random direction in the atmosphere, there is a net radial component of
the radiative energy flux towards the outside of the star.


In this layer, like in any else, most of the mass of the star is in H
and He, but most of the electron transitions are in metallic ions,
specifically Fe ions. Therefore, photons filtering through the
atmosphere are most likely interacting with a metal ion, which can
absorb a photon and get its momentum!

When the ion de-excites, the probability of de-excitation is isotropic
(the emitted photon is equally likely in any direction), so the ion
excitation takes some preferentially radial momentum in, and
de-excites in a random direction: the net result is the photons push
the metals out.

But everything is still ionized: the positive metal ions are
electromagnetically coupled to H and He and drag them away. This is
the fundamental picture of a line-driven wind. As the star evolves,
and grows in radius during shell burning phases, its atmosphere cools,
and more opaque matter can form (ions with more electrons, molecules,
dust): mass loss increases significantly for RSG.

While we saw that the solar wind is not removing much mass $\dot{M}_{\odot} \simeq
10^{-14}\,M_{\odot} \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, line-driven winds in massive stars caused by
their high $L$ can remove significant amount of mass, the more massive
a star is initially, the more luminous, the more mass it will lose!

A way to see this is to calculate a /timescale/ associated to mass-loss.
Using dimensional analysis for a star of mass $M$ and mass loss
$\dot{M}$ we can define:

#+BEGIN_LATEX
\begin{equation}
 \tau_{\rm ML} = \frac{M}{\dot{M}} = \begin{cases}
     \frac{1}{10^{-14}} = 10^{14} \gg 10^{10}\,\mathrm{yr} & \text{for the Sun}\\
     \frac{10}{10^{-8}} = 10^{9}\,\mathrm{yr} & \text{for a main-sequence massive star}\\
     \frac{10}{10^{-6}} = 10^{6}\,\mathrm{yr}  & \text{for a RSG}\\
    \end{cases} \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+END_LATEX

We see that $\tau_{\rm ML}$ is typically longer than the lifetimes of the
stars, for massive stars it can become comparable: radiatively driven
winds in massive stars can have a strong structural impact on the star
(and not only its rotation rate like low-mass-loss rates pressure
driven in the Sun).

#+CAPTION: Example of a Galactic WR star, WR124, as seen by /JWST/.
[[./images/WR124.jpg]]

In the most extreme cases, a massive star can self-strip: winds can
remove it's hydrogen-rich envelope! These stars, named after their
co-discoverers, are called Wold-Rayet (WR). They are a /spectroscopic class/
(like O, B, A, F, G, K, L, M defined based on the morphology of the
spectrum by the "Harvard computers" and then re-organized and
understood thanks to the understanding of radiative transfer and
atomic physics by Annie Cannon and Cecilia Payne Gaposhkin): a WR is a
hydrogen-poor (not necessarily hydrogen-free) star with such a thick
stellar wind that it blocks photons even outside the stellar
atmosphere and the strong stratification of the wind results in
emission lines.

Basically, for a WR we don't see the star, but only its wind. The most
massive stars will evolve into WR, the more massive, the higher $L$,
the stronger the wind, the sooner they self-strip.

#+CAPTION: Example of a WR spectrum showing annotated emission lines (source: [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf%E2%80%93Rayet_star][wikipedia]]).
[[./images/Wr137_spc.png]]

*** Super-massive stars

We have already seen that [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Highest mass limit: is there one?][highest possible mass for a star is not well
understood]]. Especially for the very first generation of stars, formed
from pristine, non-polluted material from the Big Bang (so just H and
He, nothing else), the masses of stars may have been much much larger,
exceeding $1000-10^{5}\,M_{\odot}$.

These are /theoretical/ objects: none of our telescopes can see these
stars. Nevertheless, it's pretty obvious that metals exist, so stars
at some point must have started appearing and processing the gas into
heavier nuclei.

The reason why these first stars may have been so big are twofold:
1. with only H and He, the gas has few electronic transition to use to
   emit photons and radiatively cool. This prevents fragmentation of
   the proto-stellar cloud and leads to a bigger star.
2. We see super-massive BHs at the center of galaxies that are too big
   to have grown swallowing mass: a possible solution is that gigantic
   stars collapsed into the /seeds/ for these BHs, reducing the need for
   accretion to increase their mass.

Super-massive stars above a certain limit may collapse because of
general relativity destabilizing their $P\equiv P(T)$ relationship, before
processing many nuclei into heavier elements: so the argument that
these stars must exists based on the composition of the universe also
requires that they not all collapse into super-massive BHs!


** Gravity's victory: core-collapse and compact object formation

Neev will cover this in detail after the exam: suffice to say here
that for $M\gtrsim 7.5-10\,M_{\odot}$ (the exact value depends on the details
of the chemical composition, rotation of the star, and maybe most
importantly the likelihood that it interacts with a binary companion
before its death), gravity ultimately wins: the iron core can only
lose energy and contract.

The collapse of the iron core brings it from
($\rho\sim10^{10}\mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}}$, about 10
billion times the density of liquid water) to densities exceeding
briefly the nuclear density: at that point the nuclear force stops
abruptly the infall, causing a shock wave: this can explode the star
in a supernova, dispersing the outer layers (closing the "galactic
enrichment loop" described [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Mass determines the structure and thus the evolution][here]]).

For example, the oxygen that we breath is synthesized in massive star
cores!

*N.B.:* You may have heard that plants make oxygen: that is true, but at
/molecular/ level: plants release O_{2} molecules as a byproduct of
photosynthesis. But plants aren't nuclear reactors: they are packing
together two existing O atoms through chemical processes at eV scale,
the nuclei already existed on Earth, where they come from the
primordial gas that formed the Sun and its solar system.

However, the heaviest elements (silicon, iron) end up locked in the
remnant neutron star or black hole. Plus the existence of nuclei of
anything heavier than iron that the stars would not synthesize remains
still unexplained.

* Origin of elements

#+CAPTION: Periodic table of chemical elements with the astrophysical origin of their nuclei. From https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/13873.
[[./images/PeriodicTableOrigins.jpg]]

Most heavy elements /cannot/ be produced by exoenergetic fusion
processes inside a star in hydrostatic equilibrium. Nevertheless, to
make their component nucleons come close enough together that the
strong force binds them, extremely high relative energies between the
incoming nuclei are needed. This leaves high-energy astrophysical
phenomena such as stellar explosions, mergers, and extreme transients
as promising sites.

** Neutron-capture processes
In most cases, the formation of heavier elements proceeds by sticking
neutrons onto a seed nucleus (e.g., iron), and then have the newly
formed neutron-rich nucleus decay into a stable element.

By using neutrons, we avoid dealing with the electromagnetic repulsion
in the nuclei, however, free neutrons are unstable and decay in 15min:
for these processes to happen we need a neutron source.

We distinguish two processes based on the amount of neutrons that can
be captured /before/ the decay of the neutron rich nuclei.

The two videos below show in the top inset the $T$, $\rho$, and energy
generation rate from a simulated thermodynamic trajectory of the gas.
The main panel shows the abundance (= fraction by mass) on a Z(N)
plane (number of protons vs. number of neutrons) and the bottom inset
gives the resulting yields from [[https://bitbucket.org/jlippuner/skynet/src/master/][skynet]], an open source
nuclear-reaction network developed by J. Lippuner.

*** S-process
"S" for /slow/: in this case the neutrons are captured one-by-one. One
neutron capture, followed by one decay, and so on.

This requires only moderate neutron fluxes, and never produces nuclei
more neutron-rich than by one neutron. We think it can occur in
thermally pulsating AGB where some neutron-rich carbon can be formed
in the intra-shell region and mixed outwards, where it ejects its
neutron and enables the s-process.

*** R-process
"R" stands for /rapid/: the neutron captures occur /faster/ than the
decays. This allows to build extremely neutron rich elements, before
they decade, flushing the "wrong nucleons" into the "right" type to
become a stable isotope.

This requires very high neutron fluxes, basically only realized in
extremely dense flows where electron captures on protons can generate
lots of neutrons, typically involving neutron stars (mergers with
something else, or ejecting part of their crust).

** Explosive stellar phenomena
Very often, binary interactions between stars are key to create these
phenomena!

#+CAPTION: Recall that the presence of stellar companions varies with mass/spectral type of the stars, and is extremely common at the high masses.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 75%
[[./images/bin-frac.png]]

In the following, I briefly describe some transients relevant to
nuclear astrophysics - a vast, and still active, topic of research
that started with the [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B2FH_paper][B^{2}FH paper]] in 1957 (led by Margaret
Burbidge).

*** SN Ia
We have seen that stars with initial mass lower than $\lesssim
7.5\,M_{\odot}$ end their life as stable WDs, which cool and contract
forever.

However, there is a maximum mass that a WD can have: what happens when
a binary companion dumps mass onto a WD? The outcome is very sensitive
to the mass-transfer /rate/ (how much mass per unit time is
transferred), but it can in some case result in a type of supernova
transient too, called SNIa.

These show in their spectra at peak no H and He (there is little to
none left in the WD), and shows strong silicon lines: the presence of
silicon in the ejecta suggest this doesn't come from the core of a
massive star (most of the silicon goes into the neutron star or black
hole remnant). Instead, that silicon is the product of an explosion in
the WD triggered by the accretion.

Exploding WDs produce most of the heavy elements of the silicon and
iron group!

These SNIa are also very important for /cosmology/, because three is a
standardizable relation between their peak luminosity (which if we
measure the brightness can tell us the distance) and decline rate in B
band (so called Phillips relation).

#+CAPTION: Collection of WD explosions on the peak $B$ vs. decline rate. While many "peculiar" types of explosions are observed, most follow the black line. The x-axis is easy to measure, the y-axis allows to measure the distance! Figure from [[https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00528][Taubenberger 2017]].
[[./images/SNIa_phillips.png]]

*** Novae
If the accretion rate on a WD is too high, the newly accreted gas will
explosively ignite H burning: this can produce some non-standard composition.

*** X-ray burst
We can consider the same process above, but with a neutron star
accreting. Because a typical neutron star radius is
$\sim10\,\mathrm{km}$, gas accreting on a NS has to fall much further
down than gas accreting onto a WD: this liberates more of its
gravitational energy, leading to higher $T$ and more explosive
burning.

X-ray bursts on a NS may be the primary pathway for certain heavy elements

*** long-GRBs: collapse of fast rotating magnetized stars
Rotation can help the gas in its fight against gravity: in the frame
of reference co-rotating with the star it introduces a centrifugal
force (apparent!) which partially fights gravity (the more the farther
from the rotation axis).

Rotation is also /stirring/ the star and mixing it: this can make the
cores bigger than they normally would.

When a fast rotating, magnetized stars collapses, some matter may have
too much rotation to fall onto the center directly (there is a
centrifugal barrier). This matter can start orbiting the center in a
disk (densities in a collapsing star are high), and the disk can
generate magnetic fields which can squeeze gas out in a jet.

The disk may be dense enough to neutronize and do r-process, although
this is highly debated presently: the conditions to neutronize and the
conditions for an outflow seem to be in tension.

These kind of transients produce /long/ \gamma-ray bursts (GRB): these
transients were first observed by satellites monitoring nuclear
experiments during the cold war, and they were finally associated to
dying stars with SN1998bw.

The video below shows a numerical simulation from [[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993L..54G/abstract][Gottlieb et al.
2025]] of a very massive long-GRB progenitor (yellow show
magnetization, blue shows gas density).
