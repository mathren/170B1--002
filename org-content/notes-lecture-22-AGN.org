# Top hat questions

* Recap and aims of this lecture

We have seen that [[https://science.nasa.gov/universe/galaxies/types/][galaxies come in many shapes]], they are typically out
of dynamical equilibrium because of the constant perturbations by
nearby galaxies in their host galaxy cluster.

Typically the vast majority of their mass is in the form of "dark
matter", which forms a (as far as we know) /spherical/ halo much bigger
than the galaxy itself.

We have seen that in the center of the MW (the galaxy that hosts the
solar system), there is a "supermassive BH" (SMBH) called Sag A^{*}, with
mass $M\simeq4\times10^{6}\,M_{\odot}$.

#+CAPTION: EHT radio-interferometry image of Sag A^{*}.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[./images/EHT_sagastar.jpg]]

This was first /inferred/ from the Keplerian motion of stars in the
innermost part of the galaxy, which constrain the total mass and the
maximum extent of this mass distribution. Later, the tenuous accretion
disk around it was resolved through radio-interferometry by EHT.

Sag A^{*} was actually the /second/ SMBH imaged by EHT, after they released
this image of the accretion disk and the shade of the BH in the galaxy
M87 (one of the 110 objects in the Messier catalog!):

#+CAPTION: Right: X-ray image of the center of the galaxy M87. Left: zoom-in with the EHT radio-interferometry.
[[./images/M87EHT.webp]]

These two SMBH and host galaxies (MW and M87) can both be imaged by
EHT: the resolution it can achieve is of \sim25 \mu arcsecond (recall [[file:notes-lecture-02-scale.org::*Angular distances and size][1
arcsec = 1/60 arcminutes and 1 arcminute = 1/60 degrees]]). In the MW,
Sag A^{*} has a mass of $4\times10^{6}\,M_{\odot}$ and distance of $D\sim 8\,\mathrm{kpc}$,
while M87 has a much more massive BH of $6\times10^{9}\,M_{\odot}$ and is at a
distance of $D\sim16\,000\,\mathrm{kpc}$.

The ratio $M_{\rm SMBH}/D\sim 0.5\times10^{3} M_{\odot} \mathrm{pc}^{-1}$ is the same for both
these objects! We will see below that the radius of a BH is
proportional to it's mass, so $M_{\rm SMBH}/D\propto R_{\rm SMBH}/D$: Sag A^{*} and
M87 SMBH have the same angular size on the sky, making them both
viable targets for EHT.

However, M87 is physically larger, so the timescale for its
variability is longer (it scales with size/$c$): M87 was easier to
observe than Sag A^{*}.

* What is a black hole?
Until the \sim late 1960s, BHs were thought to be a "mathematical
curiosity", a particular solution of Einstein's equations that
describe gravity beyond the Newtonian model, with general relativity
(GR).

** The core idea of general relativity
We cannot derive here GR, but we can focus on the key point that
conceptually updates Newton's law of gravity. In Newtonian gravity
/space-time/ is a fixed structure in which gravity can propagate at
infinite speed: if you remove the Sun, immediately the Earth lacks the
centripetal gravitational pull to stay on its orbit and is launched
along the tangent.

Einstein (in his "annus mirabilis" 1905) showed however that he could
derive all laws of electromagnetism in vacuum /postulating/ that the
speed of light in vacuum is the maximum speed for the propagation of
information (e.g., the info that the Sun is still there pulling the
Earth onto its orbit): this means Newtonian gravity is fundamentally
incompatible with /special relativity/, the theory that correctly
describes the dynamics of objects approaching the speed of light.

In 1916, Einstein (and shortly after Hilbert -- no lone geniuses, but
an active and debating community!) published his theory of /general
relativity/ that fixes this problem.

In this picture, gravity is actually an "apparent force" due to the
/curvature of space-time/ (much like the centrifugal force in a rotating
frame arising because the frame is not inertial): the Earth orbit's
the Sun not because of it's attraction working as a centripetal force,
but because the Sun's mass curves space time around it and the Earth
is trying to "go straight on a curved surface", like the interior of a
bowl!

#+CAPTION: Space-time represented as a 2D plane (green grid) deformed by the massive object (the Sun): the deformed "shape" of space-time causes straight lines to "bend" acting like if there were a centripetal force.
[[./images/space-time-curv.jpg]]

Like any good /new/ theory of something well understood empirically, GR
reduces for small masses and low velocities to Newtonian gravity, and
/extends/ it to a new framework for large masses and speeds close to the
speed of light. Einstein's field equation describes /how masses bend
space-time/ and at the same time /how space-time imposes to free-falling
masses to move/ (i.e., how masses only under the influence of gravity
move):

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}\label{eq:einstein_eq}
 \mathrm{Space-time\ curvature} \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{Mass\ distribution} \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

** Black holes
One week only after Einstein's publication, Karl Schwarzschild, from a
hospital bed during WWI, published solutions to Einstein's equation
for a spherically symmetric space time around a point mass.

*N.B.:* The assumption of spherical symmetry helps in the calculations!
Schwarzschild looked for the simplest solution possible first!

What he found is that there is a region of space around a mass with
radius $r_{g} = \frac{GM}{c^{2}}$ such that it would take infinite energy
for an object within this region to escape the gravity of the mass
$M$. In other word, the /escape velocity/ from within this region is
faster than $c$ and no object/information can reach it! This means not
even photons can escape this region, and thus the name "black hole".

Note that *any mass* compressed sufficiently (so that it is smaller than
its $r_{g}$) can be a BH! For instance, for the Sun, $r_{g}\sim
3\,\mathrm{km}$: if you were to compress it from $R_{\odot}\simeq7\times
10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm}$ below $r_{g}$ it would be a BH. *BHs can have any mass
in theory*.


*N.B.:* far away from this, $r\gg r_{g}$ the space-time of Schwarzschild's
solution becomes the "flat" spacetime of Newtonian gravity as
expected.

#+CAPTION: Infographic on a BHs.
[[./images/BHinfographic.jpg]]


So technically, /inside/ a BH matter (but $E=m\gamma c^{2}$, so all energy)
falls towards the center, where "space-time" is "punctured" in GR.
This singularity has $r\rightarrow0$ and $\rho\rightarrow+\infty$: the appearance of an infinite
density suggests that interactions other than gravity will start to
play a role: to treat the physics at the singularity we need a model
of /gravity/ (described by GR) *and* the other forces (described by
quantum mechanics, QM). These two are still separate field without a
synthesis between them: ultimately this suggests that $\rho\rightarrow+\infty$ is a
symptom that we cannot ignore QM anymore but we don't have a better
theory to use.

At the even of horizon though (the surface that has escape velocity
$c$) nothing physically special is happening: if you were passing
through the event of horizon, *you* wouldn't notice anything special (a
part from the fact that the gas around you is getting very hot, and
gravity is stretching you, these things were happening at $r_{g}+\varepsilon$ and
will continue at $r_{g}-\varepsilon$ for small distances $\varepsilon$).

*N.B.:* from 1916 (Einstein's first paper and Schwarzschild solution of
the equations), it took \sim40 years for Roy Kerr to find a second
solution, for a spacetime around a "rotating" point mass in 1963. Also
note how BHs are both a theoretical/mathematical playground to study
gravity in a simple curved space-time, /and/ astrophysical objects of
interest!

* How to detect a BH

Since there is /nothing/ to see for a BH, until the late 60s they
remained a theoretical "curiosity".

** High-energy emission from infalling gas
However, with the advent of rocket
powered X-ray observatories, sources of X-ray photons other than the
Sun started appearing. This required sending the telescopes above the
Earth's atmosphere, which blocks these short-wavelengths, high-energy
photons.

#+CAPTION: Position of Cyg X1 (red dot) in the constellation of Cygnus.
[[./images/Cygnus_const.webp]]

The first source detected was Cyg-X1, and [[https://scixplorer.org/abs/1972Natur.235...37W/abstract?fromADS=true&ads_compat=1][Webster & Murdin 1971]] showed
this X-ray source was coincident with a particular star suggesting a
physical association between the star (optical source) and the X-rays.
[[https://scixplorer.org/abs/1972Natur.235..271B/abstract][Bolton 1972]] used then Kepler laws and geometric arguments (same as for
the SMBH in the center of the MW 50 years later!) to show that the
star and X-ray source were indeed a binary system, made of a star, and
an object that had to be massive and compact (from Kepler laws). If
the X-ray source were a second star, it should be visible in the
optical too: this became evidence that it could not be a star and had
to be a BH.

#+CAPTION: Artist impression of an X-ray binary.
[[./images/XRB.png]]

The X-rays are produced by gas coming /from the star/ (either its wind,
or the star is too big to fit the volume of space dominated by its own
gravity and overflows) and falling /onto/ the BH.

Because this gas in the frame of the binary acquires angular momentum
(due to the Coriolis force), as it reaches the BH it forms an
accretion disk, and spirals downward (*N.B.:* if the star was made of DM
this would not work, because the infalling DM particles could not
torque each other and exchange energy as a collisional gas). The
release gravitational potential energy heats up the gas, and remember
that [[file:notes-lecture-07-light-matter.org::*Thermal radiation][thermal radiation]] peaks at shorter wavelengths/higher energy for
higher temperatures): the gas becomes so hot that its peak thermal
emission is in the X-rays.

X-ray binaries became the first astrophysical object /recognized/ as a
BH: the X-rays that we see come from matter /outside/ the event of
horizon, in the process of falling into the BH.

** Orbital motion of stars

#+CAPTION: Artist impression of VFTS243, the first X-ray quiet astrometric BH, the background is an optical image of the LMC, from [[https://scixplorer.org/abs/2022NatAs...6.1085S/abstract][Shenar et al. 2022]].
[[file:images/VFTS243_artist.png]]

In 2022, [[https://scixplorer.org/abs/2022NatAs...6.1085S/abstract][Shenar et al.]] used a large spectroscopic campaign to
characterize the binary orbits of stars in the 30 Doradus region of
the LMC, and found a X-ray quiet system which could also be
geometrically constrained to host a BH (much like Cyg X1), ushering
the era of /astrometric/ detection of BHs, that is not /directly/ seeing
gas falling in the BH, but inferring its presence from the orbital
motion of companion stars.

Since the advent of the /Gaia/ space-mission we have expanded this
method, to include wide orbits where the radial velocity shifts would
be too small, but that can be resolved by /Gaia/'s astrometric
precision. This has so far produced 3 stellar-mass BH, with hundreds
expected with /Gaia/ DR4 in December.

#+CAPTION: Example of orbit reconstruction for a star orbiting an unseen companion
[[./images/astrometric_orbit.jpg]]

** Orbital motion of gas
So far, we have talked about methods capable of measuring BHs in the
stellar-mass range ($M\sim 10-100\,M_{\odot}$). But if you have a much bigger
BH, $r_{g}\propto M_{\rm BH}$ grows, and if it is large, maybe mass doesn't fall
that far before getting in the BH, and doesn't heat up all the way to
producing X-rays!

In fact, the very first ideas of BH predate Einstein, and were
proposed by Mitchell and Laplace, who in the 18^{th} century proposed
objects so large that even light could not escape them.

#+CAPTION: Schematic representation of the regions of gas orbiting an SMBH from [[https://scixplorer.org/abs/2018ApJ...863....1C/abstract][Chilingarian et al. 2018]].
[[./images/BLR.jpg]]

As gas gets closer to the BH (shorter orbital separation $a$), its
orbital period has to decrease (faster orbit) by Kepler's laws: this
means taking a spectrum of the central region of a galaxy, which is
/unresolved/ we will see lines (typically in emission) that are getting
broader and broader (because of Doppler shifts along the /unresolved/
orbit) as we get close: from the width of spectral lines we can
measure BH masses in the center of galaxies!

These spectra were first detected as /quasars/ (= quasi star) and
interpreted as signatures of accreting SMBH.

** Gravitational-waves
Einstein himself derived another consequence of GR initially
interpreted as a non-physical mathematical curiosity: gravitational
waves.

If space-time is bent by matter, an oscillating matter distribution
can generate an oscillating space-time even far away from the
source (*N.B.:* because of conservation of momentum, this requires a
quadrupole moment), that is a "gravitational wave".

*N.B.:* Do /not/ confuse this with a /gravity/ wave which is a wave in a
fluid (e.q., water) where the restoring force is gravity.

In 1975, the discovery of the double pulsar by Hulse and Taylor,
showed an inspiral consistent with the emission of GWs /indirectly/
confirming their physical nature ([[https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1993/summary/][Nobel in 1993]]), and in 2015 we had
the first /direct/ detection of GW from a binary black-hole merger!

*** Merger of two stellar-mass BHs

#+CAPTION: Signal detection in two laboratories a speed-crossing-time apart confirming the first BBH GW merger.
[[./images/GW150914.png]]

GW150914 was the first BBH merger detected in GWs, which:
- proved the existence of $\sim30\,M_{\odot}$ BHs
- proved the existence of binary BHs (unknown until then!)
- proved the direct detectability of GWs

This discovery has the /huge/ implication that now GW provide a
completely /new/ way to explore the Universe, with the potential of
being as revolutionary as the invention of telescopes and their
astronomical application by Galileo had.

We can now not only "see" the Universe but also "hear it", since
ground-based GW detector operate in the same wavelength range as
audible sound (but GWs \neq sound waves! they propagate in vacuum unlike
sound for example).

*** Future of GWs: space-based GW and pulsar timing arrays for SMBH
In the 2040s, several space-based GW detectors, sensitive to much
longer wavelengths of GWs are planned.

#+HTML: <iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/GUyRKOex7rU?si=suaPbejT14zdwCNa" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

The most famous is probably [[https://lisa.nasa.gov/][LISA]], which will be sensitive to binary
SMBH (e.g., formed by galaxy mergers) and double WDs in the galaxy.
The first proof-of-concept of the 3 spacecrafts has already been
successfully launched.

At the same time, the /longest/ GW wavelength can be probed using /the
entire galaxy as a detector/ with pulsar timing arrays (PTA).

#+CAPTION: Pulsar timing allows to probe GW with $\lambda\sim\,\mathrm{kpc}$ by seeing them stretch and compress the space-time between us and a series of pulsars, i.e., NS spread all over the galaxy.

* BH mass distribution

Effectively, has you can infer from the /history/ of BH detection, the
game is to find objects too massive and compact in radius to /not/ be
visible.

With a variety of techniques, we are now probing the mass distribution
of BHs throughout the Universe:

This is /not/ complete: we also have astrometric BHs detected by /Gaia/, and soon Roman will reveal hundreds of BHs through lensing.
[[file:images/BH_mass_dist.png]]

Understanding this landscape is a primary ongoing goal of the
community.

** Stellar mass BHs
These are the end point of the evolution of massive stars, reaching up
to \sim100s of solar masses -- I have done some research on the
most-massive stellar-mass BHs, relevant to present GW detections.

In general, the question of stellar collapse and possible explosion,
and understanding the formation of these BHs is still open, despite a
lot of recent progress.

** Intermediate mass BHs
We barely see any BHs with masses between $\sim 10^{3}\,M_{\odot}$ and $\sim 10^{6}\,M_{\odot}$.
These are theorized "intermediate mass BHs".

There have been a few claims, e.g., the detection of ultra-luminous
X-ray sources. Of these, only one (HLX-1) still stands: for many other
ULXs /pulsations/ have been found, which implies the existence of a
solid surface oscillating, and thus /not/ an event of horizon (which is
not a physical boundary that can oscillate).

#+CAPTION: The yellow circle marks the location of HLX-1, the only possible X-ray source associated to an IMBH.
[[./images/HLX1.jpg]]


The reason for an apparent lack of detection of IMBHs is an open
puzzle, related to the formation of SMBHs (see below).

** Super-massive BHs

Super-massive ($\ge 10^{6}\,M_{\odot}$) BHs have been first discovered in the
center of galaxies as /quasars/:

#+CAPTION: /HST/ image of a quasar: it's an extremely bright point mass, the spikes are caused by diffraction in the telescope.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 75%
[[file:images/HST_quasar.png]]



#+CAPTION: Artist impression of a quasar spectrum.
[[./images/quasar.jpg]]

These are cosmological objects (distances of the order of the size of
the observable Universe!), and so their light has to filter through a
large swath of intergalactic clouds, galaxies with their interstellar
medium, etc, each leaving their absorption imprint on the spectrum.

We now know SMBH can be at the center of large galaxies, and we have
seen that they probably co-evolve: the SMBH mass and mass of the
galaxy are tightly correlated across many many orders of magnitude.

#+CAPTION: $M_{\rm SMBH}(\sigma)$ empirical relation between the mass of the supermassive BH in galaxy centers and the "velocity dispersion" $\sigma$, that is the width of the velocity distribution of stars in a galaxy.
[[./images/Msigma.jpg]]

The tentative explanation for this correlation is that the BH at the
center goes through phases of accretion (e.g., when a cloud of
baryonic mass falls into a galaxy from the IGM), and this accretion
gives powerful feedback throughout the galaxy influencing its
evolution (see also below).

*** Active Galactic Nuclei
Many central regions of galaxies are very "active", earning them the
name AGNs. These regions contain a SMBH orbited by a disk of gas: the
amount of mass available is enormous, and lots of things (many of
unknown nature) may and do happen in AGNs.

[[https://spt3g.ncsa.illinois.edu/datasets/spt_agn_lightcurves/][Here]] you can find examples of light curves $L(t)$ of some AGNs: as you
can see, they have many "flares" (these have /nothing/ to do with
stellar flares, despite the name), many of unexplained causes.

#+CAPTION: Cartoon sketch of stars and stellar-mass BH/NS that could be embedded in the AGN disk.
[[./images/immortal_stars.png]]

Stars could form inside the disk (and explode as SNe possibly causing
flares), or they can be "captured" in the disk because of friction.
These stars embedded in dense gas may evolve differently than normal
stars, and have many chances to interact with other stars, the AGN
disk, or compact objects embedded in it: AGN disks are a great
playground for theorist, because they show a rich and unexplained
phenomenology, and theorist can hide all sorts of engines in these
disks.

Recently, the detection of Quasi Periodic Eruptions from AGNs, that is
quasi-periodic X-ray flares has been interpreted as the signal of a
star orbiting the SMBH at an inclination w.r.t. the AGN disk and
periodically smashing into the disk (although this is still very new
and debated):

#+CAPTION: Example light curves in X-rays of QPE from AGN sources.
[[./images/QPE.png]]

AGNs can also launch jets (remember that accreting objects receive
energy from the gravitational potential released by accreted matter,
we encountered this for [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Star
formation][young stellar objects]] already!)

These massive jets provide a direct way for the BH to influence its
host galaxy far beyond $r_{g}$ (*N.B.:* $r_{g}\simeq 10^{-8}$ pc only for a $10^{6}\,M_{\odot}$
and to kpc scale!)

#+CAPTION: /HST/ image of M87, we have seen the jet for a long time, EHT confirms this is launched by an SMBH.
[[./images/M87.jpg]]

*** Little red dots (?)
These are one of the most exciting discoveries by /JWST/ so far: compact
sources showing broad H\alpha lines, that appear as "little red dots"
(LRDs). Their current interpretation is very debated.

#+CAPTION: Sample of LRDs from /JWST/ from [[https://scixplorer.org/abs/2025ApJ...986..126K][Kocevski et al. 2025]]: in their rest frame they are blue, but cosmological redshift makes them appear red to us.
[[file:images/LRD.png]]

These are /not/ AGNs: they show no X-rays and little variability. One
promising candidate interpretation is that they are accreting SMBH
embedded in a spherical cocoon of gas -- akin to a "quasi-star"
powered by accretion on the BH (release of gravitational potential)
and not nuclear reactions.
