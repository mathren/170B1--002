* Galaxies other than the MW

#+CAPTION: /JWST/ deep field showing a field of galaxies
[[./images/JWST_HST_deepfield.webp]]

A decade ago, the Hubble space telescope (named after the person who
measured the distance to M31 using Leavitt's law for Cepheids) was
pointed for a very long time (11.3 days of continuous exposure) to
/nothing/, a dark area of the sky with no known sources. This crazy
observational program revealed faint and far galaxies, creating the
"/HST/ deep field" images. Since then, this experiment has been
repeated, increasing exposures and thus depth of the observations, and
re-done with /JWST/, which was in part designed with the scientific goal
of studying galaxies in the early Universe.

*N.B.:* /JWST/ has a bigger field of view, and more sensitive IR cameras
with a bigger mirror, so it can outcompete /HST/ optical and UV sensors
in a shorter exposure time.

These /deep fields/ of photometric space-based observations revealed a
plethora of faint galaxies! The area in the tilted square in the image
above is of size comparable to the projection of your thumb at arm
length onto the celestial sphere!


** Galaxy density

Based on observations like these (but typically performed on a wider
area of the sky, with a trade-off of going less deep), we can estimate
the /number density of galaxies/ in the Universe.

However, we already know that not all galaxies have the same mass (the
MW is much larger than both LMC /and/ SM for example!), so we may want
to look not just at the number density, but the number density as a
function of galaxy mass.

Because the mass-budget is /dominated/ by DM, which we cannot directly
/see/, we can use the /stellar mass/ $M_{\star}$ as a proxy, knowing that for
example for the MW $M_{\rm tot} \simeq M_{\star} \times 1000$. In the figure below, from
[[https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2025/03/aa52570-24/aa52570-24.html][Shuntov et al. 2025]], each color the number of galaxies per (10^{6}pc)^{3}
elements of volume and as a function of their $M_{\star}$ on the x-axis for
one spherical shell of the Universe centered around us:

#+CAPTION: /JWST/ galaxy mass function for different slices of the Universe (each color correspond to a certain spherical shell of the Universe, labeled by the redshift $z$). Note the log-log scale.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 75%
[[file:images/gal_density.png]]

Black curves are closer to us: anywhere in the Universe, small
galaxies outnumber large galaxies (which you would expect in the
inside out repeated mergers picture of galaxy evolution) -- the curves
slope downwards!

Moreover, earlier in the Universe (lighter colors corresponding to
farther distances and thus galaxies seen earlier in the Universe's
evolution) there are fewer galaxies.

Since its launch, /JWST/ has been revealing very large galaxies very
early in the Universe, possibly challenging key elements of the
paradigm for galaxy evolution and cosmology.

*N.B.:* We can use the number of galaxies per unit volume in a given
mass-bin to convert estimates of events /per galaxy/ into an intrinsic
rate density per unit time:

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{R} = \frac{\# \ \mathrm{events}}{\mathrm{Galaxy}\times \mathrm{time}}\times \frac{\# \mathrm{Galaxies}}{\mathrm{Volume}} \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+end_latex


* Galaxy zoo
Galaxies exhibit a diverse set of morphological properties, in terms
of their shape, size, and colors, as you can see from the collection
of NASA images classified by galaxy type [[https://science.nasa.gov/universe/galaxies/types/#intro][here]].

The [[https://zoo4.galaxyzoo.org/][Galaxy zoo]] project is an ongoing citizen-scientist effort to crowd
source the visual classification of galaxies: this was used to label
training set for machine learning classifications, but also provided
unexpected discoveries, such as green pea galaxies.

#+CAPTION: "Green pea" galaxies are spherical in shape, green in color, and usually very isolated: the current thinking is that these may be neaby analogs of the galaxies that caused the reionizatin of the Universe.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 75%
[[./images/GP.jpg]]

This shows two things:
- we have so many observational data that professional astronomers
  /need/ the help of amateurs to parse through them (which is useful to
  design algorithms to then have computers do what thousands of
  amateurs can collectively do, but faster)
- there is so much unknown out there that dedicated amateur
  astronomers and citizen scientists can contribute crucial insights
  and even find and name entire new /classes/ of astronomical objects,
  green pea galaxies being just one example.

** Spiral

We have already encountered spiral galaxies, the MW is /one/ of them.
These are big galaxies, with disks containing most ongoing star
formation, spiral arms that compress the gas (driving the stars
together until they can trigger the formation of new stars from the
gas), an extended halo composed of highly inclined and eccentric
orbits of mostly old stars dynamically perturbed.

#+CAPTION: Composite image of the "[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_arm#/media/File:Messier51_sRGB.jpg][whirpool galaxy]]" or Messier 51 (M51) another example of spiral galaxy, seen face on (galaxies are randomly oriented in the sky).
[[./images/whirpool.jpg]]

NASA maintains [[https://science.nasa.gov/category/universe/galaxies/spiral-galaxies/][here]] a catalog of composite images of spiral galaxies,
which shows the diversity of features that can occur in a spiral
galaxy. Recall that galaxies are /never/ in equilibrium because of
flybys and mergers within a galaxy cluster! Therefore, by sampling the
diversity of (spiral) galaxies in the Universe we understand which
morphologies are easily realized and occur often in nature (ergodicity
= studying distributions across space to understand distribution in
time).

Spiral galaxies usually are blue and rich in new stars forming in
their disks. They are also the typical hosts of core-collapse SNe (the
death of short-lived massive stars).

** Elliptical

Elliptical galaxies appear red, so their are rich in old, evolved, low
mass stars, and don't have much active star formation. They are
usually more massive and sit deeper in the DM halo of a galaxy cluster.

#+CAPTION: NGC2865 is an example of elliptical galaxy.
[[./images/NGC2865.jpeg]]

Their "round" shape is because all the orbits in a hypothetical
initial disk are perturbed onto high inclination, high eccentricity
orbits, their larger mass and older age means they had more time for
galaxy mergers to generate perturbations, and many stellar orbits.

They can host SNIa (WD explosions), but rarely (if at all) host
transient astrophysical sources related to massive stars.

** Lenticular
This is an intermediate class between spiral and elliptical: they are
disk-y galaxies, but with oversized bulges and no spiral arms. They
tend to be dust rich and may be a transitional class.

#+CAPTION: Example of lenticular galaxy NGC4886.
[[./images/NGC4886.jpeg]]

** Irregular
Many (most) small galaxies have irregular shapes: they are never given
the time to settle on equilibrium because of the interaction with
other galaxies.

We have already seen irregular dwarf galaxies, for example the LMC/SMC
pair.

[[./images/SMC.jpg]]

"New Galaxy Catalog", a modern version of the Messier catalog of

* Hubble Galaxy classification scheme

- Top Hat Q3

Based on the apparent morphology in the optical range, Galaxies can be
classified as:
- Elliptical (E) +  number characterizing the oblateness of the galaxy (S0
  = spherical)
- Spiral (S) + letter for prominence of the bulge
- Barred spiral (Sb) + letter for the prominence of the bulge

#+CAPTION: Schematic representation of the Galaxy classification scheme designed by Hubble.
[[file:images/hubble_classification.png]]

This was originally thought to be an evolutionary sequence, but /it is not/!
Galaxies are constantly re-shaped by the dynamical perturbations of
nearby galaxies, mergers, and churning. The inflow and outflows to the
IGM are regulated by feedback processes by objects within the galaxy.

Typically the most important feedback sources are:
- massive star's explosions (in aggregate)
- SMBH in the galactic centers (which can accrete matter and power
  violent kpc+ scale outflows in the form of jets)

While this makes sense from a theory perspective (as these are the
common and energetic phenomena occurring in a galaxy), we also have
empirical evidence for this in the form of relatively tight
correlations between galaxy and SMBH properties.

#+CAPTION: $M_{\rm SMBH}(\sigma)$ empirical relation between the mass of the supermassive BH in galaxy centers and the "velocity dispersion" $\sigma$, that is the width of the velocity distribution of stars in a galaxy.
[[./images/Msigma.jpg]]

*N.B.:* Note that the $M_{\rm SMBH}(\sigma)$ is plotted on a log-log scale.

* Distance ladder

After using Leavitt's laws to measure the distance to Andromeda (M31)
and resolving the great-debate on the size of the Universe in favor of
Curtis and the existence of "isolated island universes" which we refer
to as galaxies, Hubble started measuring the distances to all nebulae
he could find.

The Cepheids used in M31 could not really help for larger distances,
and Hubble had to come up with experimental methods, which today have
converged in the modern "distance ladder". Each rung at the bottom
relies on more precise techniques but limited in the distances they
can measure. Each successive rung is calibrated on the previous,
allowing to extend the distance measurements:

#+CAPTION: representation of the "Cosmic distance ladder" to measure the longest distances across the Universe.
[[./images/distance_ladder.jpeg]]

Hubble only had the second and third rung, and he made lots of wrong
hypothesis, mis-identifying distant extragalactic clusters for
individual stars, etc., but with these, he measured (typically
underestimated) several distances to galaxies (D). By the early 1920s,
it was already possible to obtain spectra and recognize red-shifts of
known patterns corresponding to specific elements, which probe the
bulk velocity of the entire galaxy along the line of sight to the
observer.

*N.B.:* To know more about the distance ladder, I recommend [[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YdOXS_9_P4U&pp=ygULM2IxYiBsYWRkZXI%3D][this video]]
by the youtube channel 3b1b (three blue one brown) with guest host the
mathematician Terrence Tao. In general this channel is a good resource
for math, physics, and science outreach.

*** Redshift
This is a /descriptive/ term of what happens to the central frequency of
an atomic/ionic transition (whose energy is fixed, and so also
frequency \nu of the photons produced at de-excitation/absorbed at
excitation), and formally it is defined as:

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
z = \frac{\lambda_{\rm obs} - \lambda_{\rm rest}}{\lambda_{\rm rest}} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

where $\lambda_{\rm obs}$ is the wavelength /observed/ for a known transition
which in a lab has wavelength $\lambda_{\rm rest}$. Often $z$ is used instead
of $t$ or distances in extragalactic and cosmological studies, since
it is a direct spectroscopic observable, while the others need a model
of cosmology to be obtained from the observables. See for examples the
colors in Fig. 2 above.

Note that typically all the lines from a given source will have the
same redshift: one can use more than one specific transition of known
$\lambda_{\rm rest}$ to measure $z$:

** Hubble-Lemaitre's law

#+CAPTION: Original diagram from [[https://scixplorer.org/abs/1929PNAS...15..168H/abstract][Hubble 1929]]. The x-axis is actually wrong and underestimated, and the fit (line calculated based on the point) is not very good, but this still made the "Hubble law" famous. It was later confirmed (changing the value of the constant $H$) with better data and data analysis.
[[file:images/hubble_law.png]]

Several people (including at Lowell observatory) had started noticing
a tight correlation between distances and velocities measured through
redshifts. Specifically George Lemaitre in a paper published in French
on a Belgian journal had stated what is now known as the Hubble (or
Hubble-Lemaitre) law:
#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hubble_law}
 v = H_{0}D
\end{equation}
#+end_latex
which states that the velocity $v$ of a galaxy relative to the MW is
proportional to its distance $D$, and the proportionality constant $H$
is *the same in every direction*.

*N.B.:* Because $v$ is measured
spectroscopically through Doppler shifts, it is /only/ the component of
the velocity vector away/towards the observer!

This is a strong hint that the universe is /homogeneous/ on large
scales: we are like one raisin in the raisin cake which sees the
farthest raising moving faster!

In practice, today, we typically measure $v$ (e.g., from a /JWST/
spectrum!) and use Eq. \ref{eq:hubble_law} to determine the distance
$D$. Complication arise because galaxies are not only "dragged" by the
cosmological expansion of the Universe, but they also have "local"
motion within their galaxy cluster that add to it: they don't follow
perfectly the relation of Eq. \ref{eq:hubble_law}!

Moreover, in the past 100 years, more than one method to measure $H_{0}$
have been used, and there is a long-standing discrepancy which causes
uncertainty in the correct present-day value of this constant.

*** Age of the Universe

Moreover, this also allows to work back in time to give an age to the
Universe as a whole! $v=L/t=HD$ so taking $L=D$, that is looking at
the velocity it takes for the universe to get to its size today, we
get $H=1/t$ related to the age of the Universe - which now must have a
*beginning* $1/H$ time units ago, and an *evolution*.

** Naming of constants and giving credit in the scientific literature

The constant $H_{0}$ was called "Hubble constant" to credit his 1929
paper. However, we have seen already cases where similar ideas
germinate in multiple people's head at roughly the same time ([[file:notes-lecture-14-planetary-atmospheres.org::*Greenhouse gas molecules][e.g.,
the discovery that CO_{2} has a high IR opacity]], or Hilbert and
Einstein publishing a "geometric theory of gravity" -- general
relativity -- one week apart).

The very same thing happened for the measurements of the expansion of
the Universe: Hubble used data already collected before at Lowell
Observatory (in northern Arizona) by Slipher, which George Lemaitre
also used to derive the same equation Eq. \ref{eq:hubble_law} in a
publication in French in 1927. Lemaitre himself translated his work in
English, but did not bother to translate the paragraph about Eq.
\ref{eq:hubble_law}, suggesting he did not consider this a crucial
contribution. Friedmann in 1922 had already derived theoretical models
for an expanding Universe: that this was a possibility was "hanging in
the air" in the community at the time, and data being collected slowly
grew the empirical evidence supporting it. This is a very typical
process in science.

Despite this clear evidence that credit should never be attributed to
individuals, because ideas come from the fertile ground of discussions
among members of a community, in 2019 the [[https://web.archive.org/web/20190330010355/https://www.iau.org/news/pressreleases/detail/iau1812/?lang][IAU had a whole discussion]]
on whether we should rename $H$ as the Hubble-Lemaitre constant.
Personally, I think this was a missed opportunity to give a
/descriptive/ name untied to the personalities -- which is always
preferable.

* Cosmological principle

The set of observations made famous by Hubble's 1929 paper were the
first indication that the Universe as a whole is expanding. We have
already briefly mentioned this [[file:notes-lecture-01-intro.org::*Expansion of the Universe][very early on]], and this typically leads
to the question: "expanding into what?"


#+CAPTION: Raisin cake analogy for the Hubble flow. The analogy breaks down because there is no external oven for the Universe to grow /into/ filling a "pre-existing" space.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 75%
[[file:images/raisin_cake.png]]

Another possible analogy is the surface of a balloon inflating (not
the volume inside the balloon, just the skin):


#+CAPTION: The "skin" of a balloon as it is inflated can be a useful analogy to understand why Hubble's law is evidence for an expanding homogeneous universe.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/baloon_cosmology.png]]


This is /not/ a well posed question: the Universe /is/ the whole
space-time continuum, it is /not contained/ in anything! While the
visible part of the Universe is finite (and has radius $\sim cH_{0$}), but
there is no indication that the actual Universe (beyond what we can
see) has an edge or a center. Unlike the cake, there is no edge!

The idea that on very large scales (much larger than the cosmic web
along which galaxy clusters form) is homogeneous is called
/Cosmological Principle/ -- which is a working hypothesis in cosmology.

In general $H_{0} = H(t=\mathrm{today})$ is the value of the
proportionality constant /today/ but it could be a function of time, but
/not/ space by the Cosmological principle (*N.B.:* however, space and time
are not really separated entities in general relativity). This means
at any time $t$ in the age of the Universe, we could define a
reference frame "moving with the Hubble flow": this cosmological
reference frame is /special/ w.r.t. all other possible reference frames
because it is moving with the Hubble flow. This idea breaks the
Galilean invariance principle that all frames are equivalent, but this
frame is possibly /not/ an inertial frame (especially if the
cosmological expansion of the Universe can accelerate).
