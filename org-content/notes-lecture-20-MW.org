* What is a galaxy?

We now know that these are "galaxies". The word /galaxy/ comes straight
from the greek "milky"+"way". These 3 galaxies are part of the larger
[[file:notes-lecture-01-intro.org::*Extra-galactic][Local group and Virgo cluster]].


#+CAPTION: Optical all-sky image oriented disk edge on of the Milkyway galaxy.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 100%
[[./images/Gaia.jpg]]

In the modern definition, *a galaxy is a /gravitationally bound/ system
of stars* (including their remnants and planets), *gas, dust, and dark
matter*.

The "Milky way" is the projection on the sky of its disk (see also
below)!

#+CAPTION: We are /within/ the galaxy, so we cannot see it from outside! This is a composite image of the "[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_arm#/media/File:Messier51_sRGB.jpg][whirpool galaxy]]" or Messier 51 (M51) another example of spiral galaxy, seen face on (galaxies are randomly oriented in the sky) and qualitatively similar to what we think our galaxy is.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[./images/whirpool.jpg]]

In common astrophysical jargon we use Milkyway (MW) to refer to the
entire Galaxy hosting the Solar system, and not just the visible arm
appearing like a white-ish glow in a dark night: the name has /drifted/
from referring to the purely empirical phenomenology observed by naked
eye to a /theory-bearing/ statement that embeds the idea of "isolated
island Universes" as galaxies. But this understanding that is far from
trivial! Figuring out how stars are distributed and what is the place
of the Solar system in this took a long path.

The textbook analogy is that it is like trying to understand the shape
of the house sitting within the kitchen. You can see some outside from
a window, but there are thick walls everywhere blocking your view!

* Timeline
Let's discuss a brief and partial timeline of the shaping of the idea
of "island Universes" or galaxies:
- In the Aristotelian tradition, the question of the distribution of
  stars in the sky did not really exist: they occupied the outermost
  celestial sphere, and the apparent clumping was of no concern. Their
  lack of detectable annual parallax (by naked eye) was considered
  evidence for the lack of orbital motion of the Earth in support of
  geocentrism.
  #+CAPTION: Medieval illustration of an Aristotelian geocentric Universe
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/geocentrism.png]]
- In the 1600s, Galileo's pointing of telescopes to the night sky
  showed that the Milky way, and extended bright clumps were actually
  made of many stars. He did not /solve/ the parallax issue existing since
  ancient Greece but made it less of an issue.
  #+CAPTION: Galileo sketches of the Orion region (left) and Pleiadis (right): his telescope allowed him to distinguish an unprecedented number of stars.
  #+ATTR_hTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/galileo_orion_pleiadis.jpg]]

- Siblings William and Carolina Hershel tried to map the shape of the
  Milky way from star counts and determined it was a flattened disk,
  so not a uniform distribution.
  #+CAPTION: Drawing by Hershel in 1785
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/WCHershel.jpg]]

- [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Star formation][Recall]] Messier catalogs: extended "nebular sources" could be
  mistaken for comets within the Solar system. If was unclear whether
  these constituted /local/ sources (for some meaning of /local/), or far
  away unresolved sources. *N.B.:* Galileo had already shown once that
  extended nebulae such as Orion where actually many many stars
  unresolved without a telescope, same "flavor" of question! This
  catalog contains 110 objects that he knew were /not/ comets that
  Messier wanted to /avoid/. But a part from /not being comets/, they are
  a mixed bag astrophysically (from galactic planetary nebulae, to
  clusters, to external galaxies). The Messier catalog remains a
  classic target for amateur astronomers in the Northern hemisphere.
  [[https://cosmos-book.github.io/catalogue/messier/index.html][Here you can see images of all of them]].
  #+CAPTION: 110 objects in the Messier catalog of /not/ comets.
  [[./images/messier.jpg]]

- The study of the distribution of these nebular sources became a way
  to probe the structure of the "Universe" (at a time with no
  distinction between Universe/Galaxy). Kapteyn, Pannekoek, and other
  dutch astronomers started a program to measure their distances and
  started finding puzzling large numbers -- expanding once again the
  Universe since Galileo had shown that stars were more numerous than
  then eye could see.

- In a replay of the geocentrism vs. heliocentrism debate, the
  position of the Solar system in the Galaxy was unclear, and either
  assumptions were considered. Harlow Shapley in the 1920 showed that
  a particular set of nebular sources (the [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Star formation][globular clusters]]) were
  distributed not centered around the Sun, but a /different/ center did exist.
  #+CAPTION: Reproduction by F. Timmes of H. Shapley spatial distribution of globular clusters.
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/shapleyGC.jpg]]

- Curtis & Shapley [[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M4w-hwiKxOE][debate]]: the American National Academy of Science
  held on 1920 a "Great debate" between two Californian astronomers on
  the question of the shape and size of the Universe.

  Shapley's position was that the Universe was much bigger than
  previously thought (1000s of light years!) and the solar system was
  not in its center. He interpreted all nebulae (such as M31) as
  objects /part of the same "Universe"/ (in today's language he meant
  /galaxy/), much like globular clusters were.

  Curtis position was that the Universe was made of these large
  "island Universes" separated by large swaths of space without stars,
  and the Solar system was in one of these "island Universes"
  (galaxies) together with /some/ nebulae (like the GCs), but some /other/
  nebulae are other farther "island Universes".
  #+CAPTION: M31 (Andromeda galaxy, part of the Messier catalog and visible by naked eye in good conditions) was a key object in the debate on the scale of the Universe
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/M31.jpg]]


- Hubble, using the stellar physics insight by Henrietta Swan Leavitt
  measured the distance to nebular objects, revealing that M31 was
  \ge750 kpc \gg 10 kpc radius of the stellar disk of the MW.
  #+CAPTION: Cepheids are pulsating stars: measuring the /time/ for pulsations is easy, and Henrietta Leavitt had discovered that the period of pulsation gives the /luminosity/. From this, Hubble, measuring the /brightness/ of Cepheids and their period could calculate the distance to M31.
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/cepheids.jpg]]


** The Cepheids period-luminosity relation as distance measurement

[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt][Henrietta Swan Leavitt]] was one of the Harvard computers in the early
20^{th} century, and was studying /Cepheid/ stars, which change
periodically in brightness. Using nearby stars, for which she could
get a parallax and thus a distance, she found a proportionality
between the period and visual-bang brightness.

#+CAPTION: Correlation between Period (on the x-axis in days) and brightness (in V-band magnitude) of Cepheids in a paper by H. Leavitt 1912.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[./images/leavitt.png]]

Both Shapley and Hubble used observations of the same kind of stars in
GCs and M31 respectively, and using their measured periods determined
their intrinsic luminosity and thus distances. Hubble's distance to
M31 was so great that one had to conclude it was a separate
"island Universe" or galaxy.

*N.B.:* The understanding of stars underpins the entire galaxy evolution
field, and even cosmology: Cepheids are still the first rung in the
cosmological "distance ladder".

* Size of the Milky Way and its neighborhood

- Top Hat Q3: appearance of young stars


#+CAPTION: Schematic representation of the Galatic structure
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/MWschematic.png]]

The MW has a diameter of about $R=10\mathrm{kpc}$, with most of the /young/ stars in
a thin disk of 300pc of thickness, and a /halo/ of older stars roughly
spherical.

The Sun is part of the Galactic disk, with a galactocentric radius of about
8kpc: we are indeed in the outskirts as revealed by Shapley.

*N.B.:* Stars and solar systems rotate around the Galaxy, but also can feel
(e.g., during fly-bys) their own gravity: the /total/ angular momentum
is distributed through gravitational torques. Stars behave like the
particles of a gas that can "bump into each other", but instead of
"bumping" (electromagnetic interactions), they communicate through
gravity. This is why (some) galaxies have /disks/!

The nearest galaxies are the LMC and SMC, at about
$D=50\,\mathrm{kpc}$ from the Galaxy.

*N.B.*: The size of a Galaxy and its distance to the nearest galaxies
 are typically comparable ($R/D\sim1$, only $\sim 0.2$ for the MW-LMC/SMC),
 therefore the tides exerted by a galaxy on the next are significant,
 and medium-size galaxies are not in dynamical equilibrium!

*N.B.:* Tides are due to the difference in gravity between points of an
  extended body (like a Galaxy)

That is however only the /visible/ part of the galaxy: effectively,
where the /stars/ are. By trying to measure the /total mass/ of the
galaxy, we found that this is only a small fraction (\sim20%) of a
galaxy!

** Measuring the mass of a galaxy: dark matter

Measuring distances is hard, that's why Leavitt's relation between
luminosity and period of the cepheids was important. Similarly,
measuring masses in space is hard: for stars we could find binaries,
and use Kepler's laws to derive masses.

Binary galaxies exist too (in a sense the MW-LMC-SMC is a triple
system of Galaxies), but their orbits are:
1. not stable and Keplerian, but the out-of-equilibrium product of the
   constant tug and pull of flybys by other galaxies
2. take hundreds of millions of years (vs. sub-hour to centuries for
   stars)
This means we cannot use the same /astronomical/ trick! However, we can
still use the physics /described/ by the Kepler laws (and /explained/ by
the Universal law of gravitation by Newton): not /among/ galaxies, but
by measuring the orbit of stars /around/ the galaxy.

*N.B.:* The mass of a star is so small compared to the mass of a galaxy,
that we can use stars as "test particle" that probe the local
gravitational field of the galaxy without changing it significantly.

The Sun's orbit takes about 250 million years (meaning we barely have
moved since the genus homo appeared on Earth \sim 3 million years ago).

We can also play this game in other galaxies, and Vera Rubin
discovered "excess mass" that is /not/ made of shiny stuff in any of the
electromagnetic wavelengths we look.

#+CAPTION: Galaxy rotational curves show the /projected/ velocity of galactocentric motion through Doppler shifts, and velocities are higher than they should if all the mass was where the light is from.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[./images/galaxy_rotational_curve.jpg]]

Galaxy rotation curves are empirical evidence that the /gravitational
mass/ of a galaxy is /not/ distributed like the light sources. The
easiest hypothesis is that there is "dark matter" (DM): the only thing we
know is it does not interact significantly in electromagnetic
wavelengths.

The idea of dark matter was originally proposed by Zwicky in the
1930s, but he was a scientist famous for shelling out many half-baked
ideas: Vera Rubin's work provided the first evidence for it.

Since then, many other concordant and independent line of evidence
have been found for DM at various scales:

- Cosmological: we can measure the equivalent of /sound waves/
  propagating across the distribution of /galaxies/ (as in galaxies are
  the molecules of the air carrying the sound) requires DM
  #+CAPTION: Animated gif of baryonic acoustic oscillations, sound waves generated early in the Universe which are frozen in the "gas of galaxies"
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/BAO.gif]]
- Strong lensing: In general relativity (GR), mass can bend space-time
  causing gravity (this, as any new scientific theory, englobes and
  reduces to Newtonian gravity for scales large compared to $GM/c^{2}$).
  Since in this framework gravity is not a /force/ acting on a /mass/ but
  rather the effect of the /shape/ of space-time, light is also affected
  by gravity. A mass sitting along the line of sight can act as a
  magnifying glass:l ight from background galaxies can be "lensed" and
  arrive stretched, which provides another means to measure mass, and
  we find DM around galaxies.
  #+CAPTION: "Einstein ring" produced by gravitational lensing by the yellow galaxy of a background blue galaxy.
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/einstein_ring.JPG]]
- [[https://science.nasa.gov/missions/webb/nasa-webb-pierces-bullet-cluster-refines-its-mass/][Bullet cluster]]: shows two merging galaxy clusters with most of the
  mass (blue IR) ahead of the stars and gas (pink). The mass location
  is confirmed by lensing of background galaxies: this shows how most
  of the mass is not slowed down by the EM interactions in the
  collision and goes straight past each other /because/ it is DM
  #+CAPTION: Composite image of the bullet cluster. The pink is X-rays tracing the hot gas (heated by the collision between these clusters of galaxies), the blue is IR from /JWST/. The gravitational lensing suggests that most of the mass is in the blue area, /not/ where most of the gas is (pink): this is because DM is unaffected by the collisional energy injection and overshoots the gas which instead slams into the gas of the other galaxy, slows down and heats up.
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 75%
  [[./images/bullet_cluster.jpg]]

All these point to about \sim 5/6 of the mass of the Universe being /dark
matter/ and \sim1/6 of the mass is /baryonic/ meaning, interacts with
electromagnetic forces and thus can /shine/. The evidence for DM is
strong and based on multiple coherent astrophysical measurements at
various scales.

Despite this, we don't have any evidence at /particle/ level of what DM
is: particle accelerators like Fermilab in Illinois and CERN in
Switzerland continue experimental searches, and theorist still come up
with DM particle models or theory of gravity that try to do away with
DM (which usually struggle with the /diversity/ of evidence for DM).

The mass of the MW is about $10^{12} M_{\odot}$ /in stars/ and which is only
0.1-1% of the total mass, of $10^{15}M_{\odot}$.

* Galactic structure

Let's briefly consider the observable /structure/ of the MW galaxy: as
we will discuss below, the features are a product of the stellar and
gas kinematic within the galaxy observed at a specific snapshot in
time (since the timescales involved are often 100 of millions of
years, the entire history of humanity is still just a snapshot in time
from the galactic point of view).

** Halo

*** DM component
The DM halo's structure is still a topic of research (e.g., using the
motions of stars as test particles in the DM gravitational potential
-- much like rolling a ball on a surface to find the hills and
valleys).

All we know about DM is that it is /by definition/ something that
behaves according to the laws of Newtonian gravity (regulating the
motion of stars in Galaxies), but /not/ interact electromagnetically.

This means DM particles cannot /collide/ and exchange energy (remember:
"touching" is EM repulsions between your electrons and the electrons
of what you touch!), or angular momentum.

While a collapsing cloud of ordinary baryonic matter, if it has any
minimal rotation, tends to form a disk, DM should continue to zoom
around in a spherical cloud of orbits randomly oriented around the
common center of mass that cross each other with no collisions: a
uniform distribution of matter is also what is required to fix
rotational curves.

For the MW this halo is probably extending 100 of kpc ($\gtrsim 10\times$
the radius of the stellar component of the Galaxy).

*** Stellar Halo
This is a roughly spherical distribution of old and faint stars with
radius about 10 kpc. These stars have been scattered /from their
original birth location in the disk/ onto high eccentricity and high
inclination orbits by say encountering a dense molecular cloud, or any
other gravitational perturbation. By Kepler's laws, they spend most
time of their galactocentric orbit at periastron, far in the halo!

Halo stars tend to be older and less rich in \alpha elements (remember that
stars enrich the gas: the older stars are less "rich"!), corroborating
there was a lot of time to perturb their orbits.

Some globular clusters also orbit the Galactic center spending most of
their time in the Halo.

*N.B.:* An alternative possibility is that halo stars have been
"captured" from another galaxy, see below. Either way they are on
highly inclined and eccentric orbits around the center of mass of the
Galaxy.

** Galactic disk

*** \alpha-rich/thin disk
We have already encountered this as the most prominent feature in
optical-band observations: most young stars are forming in the
Galactic thin disk (100pc thick), where also there is a lot of
interstellar dust blocking the view and causing darker patches. These
stars are also \alpha-rich, that is they have many elements with $A=4n$ and
$n=1,2,3,...$, that is elements that are bound states of \alpha particles,
those produced preferentially by the massive stars.

*** \alpha-poor/thick disk
Around the thin disk of young stars, there is a thicker disk, maybe
3-500pc thick of stars slightly older (dynamically heated onto more
inclined galactocentric orbits) and less enriched in elements like
oxygen, the thick \alpha-poor disk.

*N.B.:* Original definitions of thin and thick disk were only based on
the position of the stars (vertical height above the midplane of the
galaxy), but that is ill-defined: over millions of years (short time
compared to the galaxy lifetime), that height changes. Today we use a
mixture of kinematic and chemical signatures to assign a star to the
thin or thick disk.

*** Spiral arms

The rotation of the galaxy is fast enough (in terms of comparing the
kinetic rotational energy with the gravitational potential of the
galaxy as a whole) that hydrodynamical instabilities can develop. Much
like a traffic jam is a pile up of cars, the spiral arms of a Galaxy
are pile ups in the orbit of stars in the disk!

#+CAPTION: Visualization of a two-arm spiral in a collection of elliptical orbits around a common center of mass.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/spiral_arm_graphic.png]]

In these /stellar traffic jams/ the gas of the galaxy is compressed, and
there are more "occasions" for perturbations to trigger star
formation: most young stars are in the spiral arms of the thin disk!

** Bulge

:Question:
- Where is the largest /density/ of stars in the Galaxy?
:end:

The majority of stars are towards the Galactic center, the Bulge of
the galaxy. There the metallicity (amount of mass that is not H and
He) is higher, suggesting also a larger churn of stellar generations:
this suggests an inside out formation of galaxies, with the inner
portion containing the stars that have materials recycled the most
times.

Much like the halo is composed of stars /dynamically heated/ onto very
inclined and eccentric orbits, the bulge is also thicker than the disk
because of this reason.

** Galactic center

The Galactic center is in the southern hemisphere and, according to
Kepler's laws, it has stars with the shortest period orbits! To see
these stars in this densest region of the galaxy, full of
astrophysical dust, we need IR (the dust absorbs short wavelength
photons and re-emits at longer wavelengths).

#+CAPTION: Multi-epoch IR campaign to trace the orbits of the stars closest to the Galactic center resulted in the [[https://www.nobelprize.org/all-nobel-prizes-2020/][2020 Nobel Prize]]: those orbit undeniably show the presence of a large and invisible mass packed in a very small volume that can only be a super-massive BH.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/S2.png]]


By tracing the orbits of the innermost stars of the Galaxy, the one
with the shortest separation from the Galactic center, we can
constrain the amount of mass /at/ the center.

Not only it's more than we can see (like what causes the idea of DM),
but also it has to be within these short orbits! So it has to be
extremely dense: only a supermassive black hole (SMBH) can be there,
with $M\gtrsim 4\times10^{6}\,M_{\odot}$.

Large galaxies like the MW host SMBHs! How these form remains a
mystery between cosmology and accretion physics (the study of how
matters falls into BHs). Whether smaller galaxies like the LMC host an
SMBH or not remains to be seen.

* Galactic dynamics

As stars move within a galaxy, subject to the gravity from all bodies
contained in the galaxy (other stars, gas, e.g., molecular clouds, and
DM), and as galaxies interact with each other within a cluster, a lot
of significant dynamics occurs.

Studying galactic dynamics is interesting from the point of view of
complex non-linear N-body systems, and the morphology of galaxies
(which never settles onto a static equilibrium configuration) needs to
be explained as an outcome of the dynamics.


** Dynamics within the galaxy

#+CAPTION: Stars orbit the center of the Galaxy, but it is /not/ a point mass: DM is spread uniformely
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/galdyn.png]]

Can stars collide: /not/ really in general! The density of stars is too
low, we can estimate it (in reality it's not uniform!):

#+BEGIN_latex
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\star} \simeq \frac{10^{12}\,\mathrm{stars}}{\mathrm{Vol}}=\frac{10^{12} \,
\mathrm{stars}}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}} \simeq 0.2 \mathrm{pc}^{-3} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+END_latex
assuming $R=10\,\mathrm{kpc}$. Stars are almost never coming close
enough to each other. To have a stellar merger by collision, usually
dense stellar environments such as globular clusters (GC) are needed.

Also, recall that most of the mass appears to be roughly uniformly
distributed DM which determines to zeroth order the potential of the
galaxy.

As an example, the gif below from [[https://www.tomwagg.com/][Dr. Tom Wagg]] shows a binary system
moving through a galaxy under the influence of the total gravitational
potential of the galaxy. When a SN occurs in the binary, the explosion
breaks the binary and sends the two stars (one of which is now a NS)
on separate paths:

#+CAPTION: Simulation of the galactic dynamics of a binary system in a galactic potential with [[https://cogsworth.readthedocs.io/en/latest/][cogsworth]].
[[./images/one_bin_dyn.gif]]

But, as we will see in a second, over billions of years, a galaxy is
/not/ isolated, fly-bys within the galaxy cluster, and mergers occur,
bringing in new mass and perturbing the orbits of long-lived low
mass-stars. The longer the life of a star, the more chances of
something perturbing it onto a high-inclination high-eccentricity
orbit spending a lot more time (because of the second Kepler law) in
the halo! This explains why the halo population is on average older
(it takes time for a star to be sent on a halo-like orbit by
perturbations), and why older galaxies tend to be more spherical.

*N.B.:* this is also a reason for old galaxies to be elliptical and
puffy instead of "disky" and flat.

*The collection of all the orbits of stars determines the /evolving/
shape of a galaxy*.

*** Hypervelocity stars

If a binary system gets too close to the SMBH, one star can be
"swallowed" (maybe in a [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_disruption_event][tidal disruption event]]), and the other flung
out with all the recoil (the mass of the star is very small compared
to the SMBH), becoming an hypervelocity star and escaping the galaxy!

#+CAPTION: The SMBH in the center of galaxies can (very rarely) fling out stars from a binary accelerating them beyond the galaxy's escape velocity.

** Dynamics among galaxies

orbiting each other and the MW. About 150-200 million years ago, [[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988...79R/abstract][LMC

Even when two galaxies merge, they basically go through each other
with barely any stellar system touching, but galaxy mergers can
compress interstellar diffuse gas driving the formation of new stars:

* Galactic Evolution

** Gas cycling: stars in the ISM and galaxies in the IGM

We have [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Mass determines the structure and thus the evolution][already seen]] that stars take the gas of the interstellar
material and process it forming new elements, and return it at their
deaths to the galaxy. This is a big part of the /chemical evolution/ of
a galaxy, and since stars keep forming from new gas, their chemical
composition allows to constrain /when/ they formed.

#+CAPTION: Galaxies cycle the gas of galaxies

** Galaxy inside-out assembly
The dominant cosmological model to date, \Lambda CDM, which we will discuss
later in the course, predicts the formation of DM halos along the
cosmic web, which pull gravitationally baryonic gas in forming
galaxies in cluster.

Within a cluster, galaxies are always out of dynamical equilibrium
(unlike stars, which are hydrostatic for the most), and as they zoom
past they can merge: bigger galaxies assemble inside out swallowing
dwarf galaxies (the MW ultimately will the LMC and SMC), and merging
with each other (much more rarely since big galaxies are rare!), like
the MW and Andromeda/M31 will.

*** Stellar streams

The picture of inside-out galaxy formation and evolution is /directly/
supported by stellar streams within the MW. These are groups of
kinematically and chemically related stars (i.e., they are made of the
same composition and move along orbits with similar properties) which
trace the tidally disrupted dwarf galaxies and clusters that fell into
the Galaxy and were "spaghettified" by the gravitational field of the
galaxy. Finding these streams is often a task referred to as Galactic
archaeology since they are "fossil remnants" of the galaxies eat by
the MW.
