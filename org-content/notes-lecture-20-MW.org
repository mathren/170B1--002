In the southern hemisphere, there are also two bright "patches", now
unfortunately known as the Large and Small Magellanic Cloud (LMC & SMC).

Magellan was not even the first to report them in the limited context
of western Europe, and proposals to change the name to retain the
acronym have been discussed (e.g., Magellan \rightarrow Milky, [[https://physics.aps.org/articles/v16/152][APS]]).

Note however that the nature of these extended sources was unclear,
and the clouds are /not/ the only extended sources (there are others in
both hemispheres), complicating their interpretation.

* What is a galaxy?

We now know that these are "galaxies".

#+CAPTION: Optical all-sky image oriented disk edge on of the Milkyway galaxy.
[[file:images/opticalMWbook.png]]


In the modern definition, a galaxy is a /gravitationally bound/ system
of stars (including their remnants and planets), gas, dust, and dark
matter.

The "Milky way" is the projection on the sky of one of its spiral
arms!

#+CAPTION: We are /within/ the galaxy, so we cannot see it from outside! This is a composite image of the "[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_arm#/media/File:Messier51_sRGB.jpg][whirpool galaxy]]" or Messier 51 (M51) another example of spiral galaxy, seen face on (galaxies are randomly oriented in the sky) and qualitatively similar to what we think our galaxy is.
[[./images/whirpool.jpg]]

In common astrophysical jargon we use Milkyway (MW) to refer to the
entire Galaxy hosting the Solar system, and not just the visible arm
appearing like a whiteish glow in a dark night. But this embeds an
understanding that is far from trivial! Figuring out how stars are
distributed and what is the place of the Solar system in this took a
long path.

The textbook analogy is that it is like trying to understand the shape
of the house sitting within the kitchen. You can see some outside from
a window, but there are thick walls everywhere blocking your view!

* Timeline
A brief and partial timeline of the shaping of the idea of "island
Universes" or galaxies:
- In the Aristotelian tradition, the question of the distribution of
  stars in the sky did not really exist: they occupied the outermost
  celestial sphere, and the apparent clumping was of no concern. Their
  lack of detectable annual parallax (by naked eye) was considered
  evidence for the lack of orbital motion of the Earth.
  #+CAPTION: Medieval illustration of an Aristotelian geocentric Universe
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/geocentrism.png]]
- In the 1600s, Galileo's pointing of telescopes to the night sky
  showed that the Milky way, and extended bright clumps were actually
  made of many stars. He did not /solve/ the parallax issue existing since
  ancient Greece but made it less of an issue.
  #+CAPTION: Galileo sketches of the Orion region (left) and Pleiadis (right): his telescope allowed him to distinguish an unprecedented number of stars.
  #+ATTR_hTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/galileo_orion_pleiadis.jpg]]

- Siblings William and Carolina Hershel tried to map the shape of the
  Milky way from star counts and determined it was a flattened disk,
  so not a uniform distribution.
  #+CAPTION: Drawing by Hershel in 1785
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/WCHershel.jpg]]

- [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Star formation][Recall]] Messier catalogs: extended "nebular sources" could be
  mistaken for comets within the Solar system. If was unclear whether
  these constituted /local/ sources (for some meaning of /local/), or far
  away unresolved sources. *N.B.:* Galileo had already shown once that
  extended nebulae such as Orion where actually many many stars
  unresolved without a telescope, same "flavor" of question! This
  catalog contains 101 objects that he knew were /not/ comets, but are a
  mixed bag astrophysically, and a classic target for amateur
  astronomers in the Northern hemisphere. [[https://cosmos-book.github.io/catalogue/messier/index.html][Here you can see images of all of them]].
  #+CAPTION: 101 objects in the Messier catalog of /not/ comets.
  [[./images/messier.jpg]]

- The study of the distribution of these nebular sources became a way
  to probe the structure of the "Universe" (at a time with no
  distinction between Universe/Galaxy). Kapteyn, Pannekoek, and other
  dutch astronomers started a program to measure their distances and
  started finding puzzling large numbers -- expanding once again the
  Universe since Galileo had shown that stars were more numerous than
  then eye could see.

- In a replay of the geocentrism vs. heliocentrism debate, the
  position of the Solar system in the Galaxy was unclear, and either
  assumptions were considered. Harlow Shapley in the 1920 showed that
  a particular set of nebular sources (the [[file:notes-lecture-17-stellar-evo.org::*Star formation][globular clusters]]) were
  distributed not centered around the Sun
  #+CAPTION: Reproduction by F. Timmes of H. Shapley spatial distribution of globular clusters.
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/shapleyGC.jpg]]

- Curtis & Shapley [[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M4w-hwiKxOE][debate]]: the American National Academy of Science
  held on 1920 a "Great debate" between two Californian astronomers on
  the question of the shape of the Universe. Shapley's position was
  that the Universe was much bigger than previously thought (1000s of
  light years!) and the solar system was not in its center. He
  interpreted all nebulae (such as M31) as objects /part of the same
  "Universe"/ (in today's language he meant /galaxy/), much like globular
  clusters were.

  Curtis position was that the universe was made of these large
  "island Universes" separated by large swaths of space without stars,
  and the Solar system was in one of these "island Universes"
  (galaxies) together with /some/ nebulae (like the GCs), but some /other/
  nebulae are other farther "island Universes".
  #+CAPTION: M31 (Andromeda galaxy) was a key object in the debate on the scale of the Universe
  #+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
  [[./images/M31.jpg]]


- Hubble, using the stellar physics insight by Henrietta Swan Leavitt
  measured the distance to nebular objects.
  #+CAPTION: Cepheids are pulsating stars: measuring the /time/ for pulsations is easy, and Henrietta Leavitt had discovered that the period of pulsation gives the /luminosity/. From this, Hubble, measuring the /brightness/ of Cepheids and their period could calculate the distance to M31.
  [[./images/cepheids.jpg]]



[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt][Henrietta Swan Leavitt]] was one of the Harvard computers in the early
20^{th} century, and was studying /Cepheid/ stars, which change
periodically in brightness. Using nearby stars, for which she could
get a parallax and thus a distance, she found a proportionality
between the period and visual-bang brightness.

#+CAPTION: Correlation between Period and brightness of Cepheids in a paper by H. Leavitt 1912.
[[./images/leavitt.png]]

Both Shapley and Hubble used observations of the same kind of stars in
GCs and M31 respectively, and using their measured periods determined
their intrinsic luminosity and thus distances. Hubble's distance to
M31 was so great that one had to conclude it was a separate
"island Universe" or galaxy.

** Dynamics among galaxies
The MW is a spiral galaxy, and the LMC and SMC are dwarf galaxies
orbiting each other and the MW. About 150-200 million years ago, [[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988...79R/abstract][LMC
and SMC collided]].

*N.B.*: The size of a Galaxy and its distance to the nearest galaxies
 are typically comparable ($R/D\sim1$), therefore the tides exerted by a
 galaxy on the next are significant, and medium-size galaxies are not
 in dynamical equilibrium!

*N.B.:* Tides are due to the difference in gravity between points of an
  extended body (like a Galaxy)
