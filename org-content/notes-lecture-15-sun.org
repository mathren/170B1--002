* What is a star?

We have already seen that until the Copernican revolution and the
abandonment of a geocentric theory of the Universe, the Sun (and the
Moon) were counted as "planets" because of their non-regular motion on
the night sky. However, we now know that the apparent motion on the
sky of the Sun is due to the fact that we orbit it, and it physically
is a /star/ and not a /planet/ in the modern sense of the term.

In the modern sense, a star is a /self-gravitating mass/ capable of
sustaining itself by /producing its own internal energy through nuclear
physics processes/. The objective of today is unpack this definition
specifically for the Sun.

*N.B.:* The understanding of nuclear physics is a 20^{th} century
achievement, which started with the discovery of radioactivity by
[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie][Marie and Pierre Curie]], and required the development of quantum
mechanics! This definition of star is only $\lesssim 100$ years old!

Because its not-subtle influence on Earth's surface, every human
culture has observed the Sun, and developed mythologies to explain its
existence and behavior. However, it is only in the recent past that we
have started understanding the /interior/ and /evolution/ of the Sun and
stars in general.

* Basic properties of the Sun and how we know them

Presumably the Sun, much like planets, is in global hydrostatic
equilibrium: if it were not, we would see it change on a dynamical
timescale. Using the forces that determine hydrostatic equilibrium
(gravity, pressure gradient, and to a smaller extent, rotation), we
can calculate the typical timescale for dynamical phenomena to be of
order of \sim hours in the Sun: since it comes back every day reliably,
we know it must /not/ be out of dynamical equilibrium.

[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin][Cecilia Payne-Gaposhkin]]'s PhD thesis in 1925 (only about 100 years
 ago) realized that the /bulk/ of the mass of stars is made of Hydrogen
 and Helium. The key insight was that /it doesn't take much of an
 element with many lines for it to imprint a strong signal in the
 spectrum/: the most prominent lines do /not/ indicate the elements that
 are most present by mass, but just those that have more transitions
 available in the visible spectrum. Instead, most of the mass is in
 light elements, unlike the rocky planets.

At the time, initially, famous astrophysicists (e.g., Russell!)
rejected this idea: dominant paradigm was that there is no particular
composition difference between the Earth and the Sun (something that
reads absurd now after discussing [[./notes-lecture-12-planet-formation.org][solar system formation]]). We now know
this is true not only for stars, but for the Universe as a whole too!

** Mass

Measuring masses in space is difficult, much more than measuring time!
We can leverage Kepler's laws to measure the mass of the Sun from the
measure of the year and the knowledge of the Sun-to-Earth distance!

Because the Earth is much less massive than the Sun, $M_{\rm Earth}+M_{\odot}\simeq M_{\odot}$,
the 3^{rd} Kepler law gives:

#+BEGIN_latex
\begin{equation}
M_{\odot} = \frac{4\pi^{2}}{P^{2}}\frac{a^{3}}{G} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+END_latex

where except for the constants, we only need to know the Earth's
period and the semimajor axis $a$. We can even refine this estimate
using more than one planet.

*N.B.:* Measuring time is always easier than measuring distances in
astrophysics! Methods that allow to convert time measurements into
distance measurements are important tools in every sub-field of
astrophysics.

The result is $M_{\odot}\simeq 2\times 10^{33}\,\mathrm{g}$, which is often used as a unit
of mass (instead of kg or pounds) for astrophysical objects.

** Age

The age of the Sun was the object of a great multi-disciplinary debate
in the late 19^{th}/early 20^{th} century, involving biologists, geologists,
and astrophysicists.

Biology had just been revolutionized by Darwinian theory of evolution,
based on random mutations + natural selection to explain the diversity
of life on Earth and the variety of environmental adaptations
observed. This process however is very slow (because of the random
nature of mutations), and requires a large age for the Earth.

Geology was also developing, and requiring $\gtrsim$ billion years old
planet to explain through erosion and tectonics the features observed
on Earth's surface.

Presumably, the Earth is not older than the Sun it orbits, so these
arguments putting a lower limit on the age of the Earth also put a
lower limit on the age of the Sun.

*** The need for stellar *evolution*
However, the Sun obviously shines: this means it loses (internal
thermal) energy to radiation at a measured rate of $L_{\odot}\simeq
4\times10^{33}\,\mathrm{erg\ s^{-1}}$. Something comes out: at equilibrium, this
means something must /go in/ to have balance! In other words: the fact
the Sun is /losing energy/ requires an /energy source/ and sets a
timescale for the life of the Sun given by the total energy available
divided the energy loss rate, which is its luminosity:

#+BEGIN_LATEX
\begin{equation}
 \tau = \frac{E_{\rm tot}}{L}
\end{equation}
#+END_LATEX

Unless there are sources of energy to "refill" $E_{\rm tot}$, this sets a
maximum lifetime of the star: once all the energy is gone, there is no
energy to be lost in the form of a luminosity $L$, and the star should
turn "off"!

In the late 19^{th}/20^{th} century, with a /positivism/-dominated culture,
physicists thought (very wrongly) that they knew almost everything,
and would not consider the possibility of /refilling/ $E_{\rm tot}$ for a
star and Kelvin and Helmholtz calculated that $\tau\sim 15\,\mathrm{Myr}$,
much shorter than the Earth's lifetime required by biology and
geology. Spoiler: they were /very/ wrong.

This timescale of millions of years is now called the
"Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale" or "global thermal timescale", and it is
/by definition/ how long a star would live losing all its internal
energy at a constant luminosity.

This discrepancy in this multi-disciplinary debate remained unsolved
until Hans Bethe in the 1930s figured out that stars /can/ refill their
own internal thermal energy (in other words, they can produce their
own "heat") tapping in the nuclear energy of the matter in their core!

This elongates the lifetime of the Sun from the \sim 10s of millions of
years predicted by physicists to the billion of years required by
geological, fossil, and biological evidence!

However, this process of energy /generation/ requires a slow change in
composition in the Sun core: /stars must evolve/ and are not static and
immutable objects!
