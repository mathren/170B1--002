* What is a star?

[[file:notes-lecture-03-history_and_philosophy_of_science.org::*From Ancient Greece, through Hellenistic and Muslim world, to modern ideas][We have already seen]] that until the Copernican revolution and the
abandonment of a geocentric theory of the Universe, the Sun (and the
Moon) were counted as "planets" because of their irregular (compared
to star) motion on the night sky. However, we now know that the
apparent motion on the sky of the Sun is due to the fact that we orbit
it, and it physically is a /star/ and not a /planet/ in the modern sense
of the term.

In the modern sense, a star is a /self-gravitating mass/ capable of
sustaining itself by /producing its own internal energy through nuclear
physics processes/. The objective of today is unpack this definition
specifically for the Sun.

*N.B.:* The understanding of nuclear physics is a 20^{th} century
achievement, which started with the discovery of radioactivity by
[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie][Marie and Pierre Curie]], and required the development of quantum
mechanics! This definition of star is only $\lesssim 100$ years old!

Because its not-subtle influence on Earth's surface, every human
culture has observed the Sun, and developed mythologies to explain its
existence and behavior. However, it is only in the recent past that we
have started understanding the /interior/ and /evolution/ of the Sun and
stars in general.

* Basic properties of the Sun and how we know them

Presumably the Sun, much like planets, is in global hydrostatic
equilibrium: if it were not, we would see it change on a dynamical
timescale. Using the forces that determine hydrostatic equilibrium
(gravity, pressure gradient, and to a smaller extent, rotation), we
can calculate the typical timescale for dynamical phenomena to be of
order of \sim hours in the Sun: since it comes back every day reliably,
we know it must /not/ be out of dynamical equilibrium.

*N.B.:* Exactly like we discussed for planets, this implies there will
be an internal /structure/ of the star, determined by the dynamic
balance necessary for a static equilibrium, and energy transport
throughout the star.

We know the surface $T\simeq 5800\,\mathrm{K}$ of the Sun from its
spectrum: its overall shape follows a black-body ideal emitter
spectrum (except for the absorption lines), whose peak frequency only
depends on $T$. Since energy has to flow from the bulk of the volume
to the surface, and (thermal) energy flows from hotter to colder
regions, we can reasonably assume that the Sun's interior is going to
be hotter.

At these $T$ the collisions between molecules/atoms due to their
thermal motion are energetic enough to break them: most matter is
going to be in the form of an ionized /plasma/.

The Sun's surface, much like the atmosphere of planets, does not end
with a /sharp edge/: its radius is ill-defined, and depends on the
wavelength of light used to look at the Sun $R\equiv R(\lambda)$. Photons of
different wavelength/frequencies can escape from different depth in the
atmosphere, meaning different radii. However, in the layers between
different radii caused by this effect there is very little mass.

Using geometric arguments and visible light, we can constrain the
radius that contains the bulk of the mass to be $R\simeq
6.99\times10^{10}\,mathrm{cm}$.





** Composition

#+CAPTION: From spectroscopy, we can see the fingerprint of elements in the solar atmosphere.
[[./images/solar-spectrum.jpg]]

[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin][Cecilia Payne-Gaposhkin]]'s PhD thesis in 1925 (only about 100 years
ago) realized that the /bulk/ of the mass of stars is made of Hydrogen
and Helium. The key insight was that /it doesn't take much of an
element with many lines for it to imprint a strong signal in the
spectrum/: the most prominent lines do /not/ indicate the elements that
are most present by mass, but just those that have more transitions
available in the visible spectrum. Instead, most of the mass is in
light elements, unlike the rocky planets.

At the time, initially, famous astrophysicists (e.g., Russell!)
rejected this idea: dominant paradigm was that there is no particular
composition difference between the Earth and the Sun (something that
reads absurd now after discussing [[./notes-lecture-12-planet-formation.org][solar system formation]]). We now know
this is true not only for stars, but for the Universe as a whole too!

** Mass

Measuring masses in space is difficult, much more than measuring time!
We can leverage Kepler's laws to measure the mass of the Sun from the
measure of the year and the knowledge of the Sun-to-Earth distance!

Because the Earth is much less massive than the Sun, $M_{\rm Earth}+M_{\odot}\simeq M_{\odot}$,
the 3^{rd} Kepler law gives:

#+BEGIN_latex
\begin{equation}
M_{\odot} = \frac{4\pi^{2}}{P^{2}}\frac{a^{3}}{G} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+END_latex

where except for the constants, we only need to know the Earth's
period and the semimajor axis $a$. We can even refine this estimate
using more than one planet.

*N.B.:* Measuring time is always easier than measuring distances in
astrophysics! Methods that allow to convert time measurements into
distance measurements are important tools in every sub-field of
astrophysics.

The result is $M_{\odot}\simeq 2\times 10^{33}\,\mathrm{g}$, which is often used as a unit
of mass (instead of kg or pounds) for astrophysical objects.

** Age

The age of the Sun was the object of a great multi-disciplinary debate
in the late 19^{th}/early 20^{th} century, involving biologists, geologists,
and astrophysicists.

Biology had just been revolutionized by Darwinian theory of evolution,
based on random mutations + natural selection to explain the diversity
of life on Earth and the variety of environmental adaptations
observed. This process however is very slow (because of the random
nature of mutations), and requires a large age for the Earth.

Geology was also developing, and requiring $\gtrsim$ billion years old
planet to explain through erosion and tectonics the features observed
on Earth's surface.

Presumably, the Earth is not older than the Sun it orbits, so these
arguments putting a lower limit on the age of the Earth also put a
lower limit on the age of the Sun.

*** The need for stellar *evolution*
However, the Sun obviously shines: this means it loses (internal
thermal) energy to radiation at a measured rate of $L_{\odot}\simeq
4\times10^{33}\,\mathrm{erg\ s^{-1}}$. Something comes out: at equilibrium, this
means something must /go in/ to have balance! In other words: the fact
the Sun is /losing energy/ requires an /energy source/ and sets a
timescale for the life of the Sun given by the total energy available
divided the energy loss rate, which is its luminosity:

#+BEGIN_LATEX
\begin{equation}
 \tau = \frac{E_{\rm tot}}{L}
\end{equation}
#+END_LATEX

Unless there are sources of energy to "refill" $E_{\rm tot}$, this sets a
maximum lifetime of the star: once all the energy is gone, there is no
energy to be lost in the form of a luminosity $L$, and the star should
turn "off"!

In the late 19^{th}/20^{th} century, with a /positivism/-dominated culture,
physicists thought (very wrongly) that they knew almost everything,
and there was no viable theory to explain a hypothetical /refilling/
$E_{\rm tot}$ for a star. JJ Thompson (lord Kelvin after which the
temperature scale is named) and Helmholtz calculated that $\tau\sim
15\,\mathrm{Myr}$, much shorter than the Earth's lifetime required by
biology and geology. Spoiler: they were /very/ wrong, because the
theoretical framework of classical (as opposed to quantum) physics was
very incomplete, as the first 3 decades of the 1900s showed
progressively.

This timescale of millions of years is now called the
"Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale" or "global thermal timescale", and it is
/by definition/ how long a star would live losing all its internal
energy at a constant luminosity.

This discrepancy in this multi-disciplinary debate remained unsolved
until Hans Bethe in the 1930s figured out that stars /can/ refill their
own internal thermal energy (in other words, they can produce their
own "heat") tapping in the nuclear energy of the matter in their core!

This elongates the lifetime of the Sun from the \sim 10s of millions of
years predicted by physicists to the billion of years required by
geological, fossil, and biological evidence!

However, this process of energy /generation/ requires a slow change in
composition in the Sun core: /stars must evolve/ and are not static and
immutable objects!

Another key insight is that the /evolution/ is /driven/ by the need to
compensate for the surface losses to the luminosity: in other words
/stars evolve because they are losing energy/. This was a paradigmatic
change: because stellar evolution timescales are extremely long
compared to human timescales, most of humanities had conceptualized
stars as something immobile and eternal!

*N.B.:* They lose energy necessarily to thermal radiation /because/ they
have a finite temperature $T$.

*N.B.:* Using historical records, we can go back a few thousands of
years, and some nearby stars (e.g., Betelgeuse) seem to have changed
colors in this time frame: humans have observed stars changing and
evolving but not any single individual!


* Energy sources

Stars obviously shine. Everything with a finite temperature $T$ does,
stars happen to be hot enough to shine in optical wavelength (some
even peak at shorter wavelengths, higher energy: the stars I focus on
in my research often peak in the UV!).

A star does not need an energy source to shine: just to not be too
cold! However, the timescale arguments above demonstrated that for the
Sun to be sufficiently long-lived it needed an energy source.

This means that /the Sun does not shine because it has an energy
source/: it's the opposite! /Stars need an energy source because they
shine/, and something has to compensate the losses!

** Radioactivity
To be completely fair to late 19^{th} century physicists, they did try to
consider all possible sources of energy /known/ at the time. Radioactive
decay, which is a way to tap into the nuclear energy, was considered
as soon as identified: however, the composition of the Sun does not
contain enough radioactive material for this to be significant, and it
only results in a minor increase of the energy budget.

** Chemical reaction: is the Sun on fire?
Chemical reactions are a common energy source on Earth: for example,
the chemical energy stored in bonds among the electrons of molecules
is what powers a fire!

#+CAPTION: Flames shine because heat ionizes partially burned compounds, and then electrons recombine with the molecules producing photons. However, the energy that heats up things starting the process comes from re-adjusting chemical bounds at molecular level.
[[./images/fire.jpg]]

Chemical reactions re-arrange electrons into molecules and tap into
the energy stored in this bond, which means the typical energy scale
is of electron-volts or less. The number of reactions that would be
needed per unit time to power the Sun's luminosity $L$ is
unrealistically high and suggests that chemical reactions have an
insufficient energy budget to power stars.

*N.B.:* $1,\mathrm{erg} = 6\times 10^11,\mathrm{eV}$, so to get to
$L\simeq10^{33}\,\mathrm{erg\ s^{-1}}$ from chemical reactions at eV scale (\sim10^{-11}^{}
erg), we would need \sim 10^{44} reactions per second!

** Nuclear reactions

As mentioned above, it took the development of nuclear physics and
understanding of the nuclear structure to finally find a reasonable
source of energy capable of refilling $E_{\rm tot}$ at a rate comparable
to the losses from the surface $L$: $L_{\rm nuc}= L$.

Nuclear reactions are of two types:
- /fission/: nuclear energy is obtained by breaking into pieces larger
  nuclei. This is the mechanism that generates electricity in a
  nuclear power plant, where Uranium breaks down in pieces leading to
  release of energy.
- /fusion/: we can extract nuclear energy from light nuclei by smashing
  them together in new heavier nuclei.

The plot below shows on the y-axis the average nuclear binding energy
per nucleon (proton or neutron) for various nuclei. Reactions that
release energy (that is /exoenergetic/) are those that move towards the
peak (most bound nucleus, $^{56}\mathrm{Fe}$), the difference between the
final end-product of the reaction and the initial reacting nuclei
times the number of protons/neutrons involved (i.e, $A$) is the energy
released by the reaction. Because we are now thinking at /nuclear/
scale, the relevant energy scale is the binding energy of protons and
neutrons in a nucleus, 10^{6}\times larger than chemical reactions, in the MeV
range!

#+CAPTION: Nuclear energy per unit nucleon (proton or neutron) as a function of nuclear mass $A$.
[[./images/nuclear_binding.png]]

Because stars are primarily made of light nuclei (H and He), fusion is
the dominant energy production mechanism during long-lived phases of
stellar evolution.

Specifically, in the Sun's interior, matter is fully ionized, so
hydrogen is in the form of loose protons and electrons. The Sun can
turn 4 protons into one nucleus of He, but $m_{\rm He}< 4\times m_{p}$: the mass
/lost/ in the conversion becomes thermal energy! The fraction of mass
converted in energy is:

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_defect}
\varphi = \frac{4m_{p} - m(^{4}\mathrm{He})}{4m_{p}} = \frac{2.85 \times 10^{-2} m_{u} c^{2}}{4m_{p}} \simeq 0.007 \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+end_latex


However: how often can 4 protons, all charged positively and thus
repelling each other electromagnetically, meet in one point? While the
back-of-the-envelope calculation above shows that nuclear reactions
would be an energetically viable to explain the long lifetime of the
Earth (from geology and biology) but it remained physically hard to
explain how these nuclear reactions could occur at a sufficient rate.

The key was progressively worked out through the 1920s and 1930s and
it is that 4 protons /never/ meet each other at once! Instead, the
reaction occurs through /intermediate/ steps that involve catalyst
elements (e.g., isotopes of He missing one neutron, Lithium,
Beryllium).

#+CAPTION: The pp chain in the Sun allows for the overall conversion of 4 protons into a nucleus of Helium through intermediate steps.
[[file:images/ppchain.png]]

Even using catalysts and intermediate steps, the first step is always
to smash 2 protons together, so they need to have sufficient energy to
overcome the electric repulsion they feel. If they come close enough
together, the nuclear force kicks in and can bind them together
winning over the electromagnetic repulsion.

This energy comes from the thermal energy of the protons in the
stellar plasma \Rightarrow nuclear reactions require high $T$ \Rightarrow high average
random kinetic energy of the protons. Even then, without quantum
mechanics effects (i.e., without accounting for the fact that protons
are little wave packets in a quantum field, and they can thus "tunnel
through" their repulsion), the Sun would not be hot enough to perform
nuclear reactions.


#+CAPTION: The relative energy of two protons need to be sufficient to overcome the electromagnetic repulsion.
[[file:images/hot-nuclear-burning.png]]


There is another problem: 4 protons (or equivalently nuclei of
Hydrogen) have charge $4e$, and their turn into $^{4}\mathrm{He}$ that
has only charge $2e$ because it is composed of two charged protons and
two neutrons. Where does the charge go?

Charge is another /conserved/ quantity (cf. [[file:notes-lecture-06-gravity-motion.org::*Conservation laws in physics][Conservation laws in
physics]]), the associated symmetry of nature is less intuitive than for
conservation of energy (time-translation invariance), momentum
(spatial-translation invariance), or angular momentum (invariance
under rotation), but it exists: it's called /gauge/ symmetry. So charge
needs to be conserved, and the nuclear reaction /cannot/ be just
$4p\rightarrow^{4}\mathrm{He}$.

Applying conservation laws, we can reconstruct the entire overall
reaction and thus its energy budget:

#+begin_latex
\begin{equation}\label{eq:overall_h_burn}
4^{1}\mathrm{H}\rightarrow^{4}\mathrm{He}+2e^{+} + 2\nu_{e} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+end_latex

where we need to add:
- 2 positrons ($e^{+}$): these are the anti-particle of electrons, same
  mass opposite charge, and they take away the 2 missing charges.
- 2 electron neutrinos (\nu_{e}): this took a bit longer to be added, but
  it turns out that in nature the /leptonic number/ (i.e., the number of
  particles like $e^{\pm}$) is also /approximately/ conserved, and so if you
  produce two anti-particles of leptonic nature (each carrying -1 unit
  of "leptonic number"), like the two positrons we need to add to
  conserve charge, then we need to add two neutrinos to balance out
  the leptonic number.

The positrons released in the core will quickly smash into an electron
(the plasma is ionized!) and annihilate into \gamma-photons, which will
immediately interact with the remaining electron: ultimately the
positrons deposit thermal energy.

*N.B.:* given that the annihilation of an electron-positron pair
releases at least the rest energy $mc^{2}$ of both particles, neglecting
any other energy they carry, we know the minimum energy of the photons
produced by the annihilation is $\varepsilon = 2mc^{2}$ ($e^{+}$ and $e^{-}$ have the same
mass) so we can calculate their frequency $f= \varepsilon/h = 2mc^{2}/h$ and
knowing the electron mass is $m_{e}c^{2}\simeq0.511keV$ we obtain high-energy $\gamma$
ray photons which are immediately thermalizing (i.e., being absorbed
and depositing their energy) within the stellar plasma.

*** Stars don't shine because they burn, Stars burn because they shine!

When we say "burn" what we mean is /not/ a chemical reaction like
in a fire, but /nuclear/ burning, rearranging protons and neutrons in a
lower energy configuration to extract from their configuration energy
to balance the losses at the surface.

Since the nuclear burning is a /consequence/ of the energy loss at the
surface (and not at all its cause: planets with no nuclear reactions
also shine in the IR!), stars are self-regulated thermonuclear
reactors!

Lose too much energy (e.g., increasing $L$), and the star will
contract (\simeq fall on itself), some of the gravitational potential
energy will go into thermal energy (i.e., increase the temperature
$T$), and thus increase the energy release by the nuclear burning
(increasing the chances of reactions) until it again matches the
energy losses.

Viceversa, decrease the energy losses (e.g., decrease $L$), then a
temporary excess energy release by nuclear burning in the core will
result in expanding the star (like heating up a balloon makes the air
expand: that is how we can fly air ballons). But expansion without
heat exchange leads to /adiabatic cooling/ (blow on your hand and move
it away from your mouth: farther the gas has expanded more and its
colder): the temperature $T$ would decrease and so the energy
generation rate.

#+CAPTION: Energy generation by nuclear burning is a /consequence/ of the energy losses at the surface and not its cause. This is what makes the energy generation process /self-regulated/ through a negative feedback loop.
[[file:images/self-regulated-burning.png]]


* Structure of the Sun

So we have seen that for the Sun to exist as long as the Earth, it
needs an energy source, which turned out to be nuclear energy. To
harvest it, the star needs very hot central temperatures, much higher
than its surface: nuclear burning happens only in the innermost parts,
and energy needs to be transported from the central inner regions to
the surface where it can leak out.

#+CAPTION: The interior structure of the Sun needs to be studied /indirectly/, through signals other than photons (e.g., neutrinos), or secondary signals imprinted on the photons (asteroseismology/helioseismology), and/or via computer models of the stars.
[[./images/physics.jpg]]


We have already seen the energy transport mechanism in planetary
context, and they remain the same in stellar context:
- conduction, which is usually subdominant in stars like the Sun
- radiation, which dominates in the core of the Sun
- convection: the outer layers of the Sun are convective. Unlike a pot
  of boiling water that is convective because of the fast heating at
  the bottom, the convection in the Sun's envelope it's because at the
  top of the Sun the temperature $T$ is low enough that molecules and
  even H^{-} ions form, and these are very good at stopping photons (the
  process is exactly the same as we discussed for the greenhouse gases
  in planetary atmospheres!).

The movie below shows real observations of the Sun: we see at the
surface the granularity due to convection:

#+HTML: <iframe width="600" height="400" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/CCzl0quTDHw?si=1h3tkmpwsi4w9uRz" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

The size of the convective cells is very large (the smallest are
comparable to several times the area of say Texas).

Remember that the thermal radiation depends only on $T$, and hotter
$\Leftrightarrow$ brighter: we can /see/ the convective flow with net zero mass flux
and non-zero energy flux!

* Interior of the Sun

We can only see the outer /atmosphere/ of the Sun, through thermal
photons produced in the interior that filters through the (by
definition) partially transparent outer layers. How can we know about
the interior structure?

*** Helioseismology

Much like Earthquakes probe the interior of the Earth (the propagation
of waves through the interior produces signals in seismometers all
over the globe that together tell us something on the interior
structure), we can use "star quakes" to study the interior of stars.
This was (as many stellar-physics things) first done with the Sun
which is close-by and can be /resolved/ (i.e., it occupies more than one
pixel on a camera! or to say it better, it's image on the focal plane
is much larger than the typical point-spread-function of a
telescope!), in that case we typically talk about /helio/-seismology.

"Star-quakes" can be cause by different kind of waves, depending on
the structure of the star. In stars with convective envelopes like the
Sun, the dominant waves making it to the surface are /sound waves/ (the
restoring force is the gas pressure):
#+CAPTION: Waves propagating through stars can make them "wobble". This animation shows waves of different "order" (i.e. where the oscillations have a different number of zero-points on the surface), exagerated in amplitude for intuition.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[./images/pulsation-modes.gif]]

Capturing the subtle variations in the appearance of the Sun due to
these waves is a direct constrain on the internal structure.

*** Neutrinos

The neutrinos produced in the star will leave the star unimpeded:
unlike photons, neutrinos interact extremely weekly with matter.

These particles are the smoking gun for the last fundamental force we
have not yet encountered, besides electromagnetic forces, gravity, and
nuclear forces: the weak force. As the name suggest, this one is very
weak compared to the others, and in this weakness lies the difficulty
of detecting the neutrinos!

Through nuclear reactions and thermal processes the Sun produces a
flux of neutrinos such than on Earth there is about a billion of them
per second per cm^{2}: in other words, every second, about a billion
neutrinos cross one of your fingernails. Most traverse you (and the
whole Earth) without /any/ interactions. Matter is almost /fully/
transparent to neutrinos (not quite: we /can/ detect them, but it's
extremely hard!). Catching them is hard, but possible, and if we do,
we can learn something directly about the stellar interior.

* Solar neutrinos

Neutrinos are subatomic particles first theoretically predicted by
Wolfgang Pauli in the 1930s based on the fact that in the decay of
neutrons $n\rightarrow p +e^{-}$ energy was apparently not conserved. Instead of
abandoning the principle of conservation energy, Pauli proposed that
the decay of the neutron produced not 2 but 3 particles: $n\rightarrow p+e^{-} +\nu$
with the third being hard to detect.

:Quote:
/I have done a terrible thing. I have postulated a particle that
cannot be detected/ - W. Pauli.
:end:

However, in the mid-50s, neutrinos produced by a nuclear power-plant
were [[https://icecube.wisc.edu/neutrino-history/1956/01/1956-first-discovery-of-the-neutrino-by-an-experiment/][detected by Cowan and Reines]]: the proximity to the source made
the neutrino flux sufficiently high (\sim10^{12}-10^{13} neutrinos s^{-1} cm^{-2}).
Since then, neutrinos from astrophysical sources (stars, stellar
explosions, and jets) and have been detected, at various energies, and
provide information complementary to photons since they hardly
interact with matter.

On Earth, the main source of neutrinos is the Sun: by measuring their
energy, we could even recently /directly/ confirm specific nuclear
reactions occurring in the Sun's interior, strongly and directly
confirming the picture of energy generation we have discussed above.

Typically, since neutrinos interact so little with matter, to detect
them one needs /a lot of matter/. Historically this was done by [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestake_experiment][filling
underground abandoned mines]]. In the late 60s, Davis detected the flux
of neutrinos from the Sun, but it was 1/3 of the predicted neutrino
flux! This was dubbed the "missing solar neutrino problem", and
initially the community thought either the theoretical prediction or
the experimental apparatus were wrong.

*N.B.:* Because science is a collective enterprise, there is a sociology
of science and the community decides collectively what are the
important problems to work on. This per se is /not/ a scientific
(quantitative, based on empirical evidence) process, but it shapes the
way scientific communities work. To learn more about this aspect, I
highly recommend [[https://archive.org/details/structureofscie000kuhn]["History of Scientific Revolutions"]] by Thomas Kuhn.

For decades, this problem remained open: the experiment was repeated
and the result confirmed, the theoretical predictions were double
checked, with nothing pointing to errors. Ultimately, the solution
required understanding neutrinos /beyond/ the predictions of the
standard model! Astrophysics pushed us beyond the standard model of
particle physics.

The key are /neutrino oscillations/: in nature there are three species
of neutrinos (electron, lepton, and muon) corresponding to the three
leptons (electron, and its more massive "cousins" muon and tau
particles). The neutrino-producing processes in a star like the Sun
preferentially produce electron-flavor neutrinos (*N.B.:* "flavor" is
the real technical term!), which are also the easiest to detect, and
the predictions were based on this fact.

However, it turns out that the standard model of particle physics
(built on quantum mechanics and decades of experiments in particle
accelerators) is missing a key piece: neutrinos can "change flavors"
as they travel from the Sun to Earth. By the time they arrive on
Earth, about 2/3 of the expected electron neutrinos are going to have
switched to muon or tau neutrinos, which the detectors then miss!

Several Nobel prizes have been awarded for the detection of neutrinos,
development of the theory on how they interact with matter, their
oscillations, and even the The [[https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2026/10/press-physicsprize2026.pdf][2026 Nobel prize in physics to Francis
Halzen]] was awarded for the development of neutrino detectors which
require 1 km^{3} of ice to work!

#+CAPTION: IceCube experiment in the south pole uses 1km^{3} of Ice to detect neutrinos, the Principal Investigator (PI) of this experiment F. Halzen was awarded the Nobel price in physics this month.
[[./images/icecube.jpg]]

Other experiments use large volumes of sea water, or huge tanks of
water polluted with elements that are more likely to react with
neutrinos deep in mines (e.g., at the famous Kamiokande mines in Japan).

* Solar activity

Since the first telescope observations of the Sun, it has been very
clear that it is far from the ideal sphere postulated by Aristotle.

As we have already discussed, the outer envelope is convective: there
is plasma (meaning free charges) moving around, creating currents and
magnetic fields! These magnetic fields in turn shape the motion of the
charges, leading to the complex phenomenology we observe.

** Solar wind
#+CAPTION: The solar wind has a latitudinal structure too.
[[./images/solar_wind.jpg]]

We have already encountered this when discussing planets in the Solar
system being invested by particles (typically protons).

These come from the surface of the Sun, where very recently it was
worked out that magnetically driven waves deposit energy in the
outermost layer (so called "corona") that heats up to \sim10^{6} K \gg 5700 K
at the surface emitting thermal radiation. This very hot layer around
the star corresponds to a high pressure pushing material out.

The amount of mass lost to the stellar wind is very small \sim10^{-14}M_{\odot}
yr^{-1}. The age of the Sun is about 4.5\times10^{9} yr so it has not lost very
much mass. However, this stellar wind is crucial to the angular
momentum budget of the Sun: the wind acts like the arms of a dancer
widening, slowing down the rotation! The magnetic field play a crucial
role in keeping the wind connected to the star for it to be relevant
in terms of angular momentum.

** Sun spots
In the 1600s, with the invention of the telescope, the presence of Sun
spots became impossible to ignore.

#+CAPTION: Drawings of Sun spots by Galileo.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/sunspots-galileo.png]]


These appear darker because they are /colder/ regions of the surface:
they can afford to be cold without lacking pressure to support the
gas, because of the intervention of magnetic fields which compensate
the cold gas with magnetic pressure.

The number of Sun spots and their position are /not/ fixed in time. This
is literally /weather/ on the Sun, but driven by magnetic phenomena.
Roughly speaking there is a cycle of \sim 11 years between maximum
activity.

Individual spots form at mid-latitudes (\sim\pm 30 degrees from the
equator), and move towards the equator before disappearing.

** Rotation

By using Sun spots as tracers, Galileo suggested that the Sun rotates,
and on top of this it rotates in a non-uniform fashion. The Sun is the
only star close enough that we can measure its rotation rate as a
function of latitude $\omega(\theta)$, and surprisingly the equator rotates
/faster/ than the poles! This as far as I know has not yet been
explained clearly from a stellar physics perspective.


#+CAPTION: The solar surface rotation can be directly observed and is faster at the equator.

** Flares and coronal mass ejections

Magnetic activity at the Sun's surface can cause brightening (flares)
and even eruptions (coronal mass ejections). [[https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/gallery/sdoproduced-videos/][NASA SDO gallery of
videos of the Sun]] shows several years worth of Sun's images showing
these phenomena. The magnetic field stores some energy, and when its
/topology/ changes this energy can be released.

In most cases flares do not cause ejection of mass, but only the
production of high-energy photons. The surface temperature would be
too low for these photons to be produced thermally, but the
non-thermal magnetic process can and do produce them.

In some cases, the reconfiguration of the magnetic field can cause an
ejection of mass: this can be a temporary significant enhancement of
the solar wind density.
