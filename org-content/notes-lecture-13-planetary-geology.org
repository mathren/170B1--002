/Planetary science/ is the study of planets, moons, and minor-bodies in
the Solar system (including Earth) and/or exoplanets (and exomoons!).
These studies allow us to understand the physical processes that shape
a planet (formation and evolution) and probing them in conditions
different than the Earth, for example:
- different chemistry
- different irradiation from the Sun/host star
- different mass and thus thermodynamic conditions (pressure,
  temperature, etc)

By performing /comparative studies/, planetary science can teach us a
lot about our own planet that we cannot know otherwise.

* Sources of energy for a planet

Planets receive on their "day-side" energy in the form of radiation
from the Sun (or their host star for exoplanets). However, depending
on the properties of the planet, this energy does not penetrate very
deep (maybe 100s of km at best). While it is crucial in driving both
weather (you know it's colder at night!) on the surface, irradiation
from the host star is not the main source of energy in the inside of a
planet. Nevertheless, on Earth /geological activity/ (volcanoes,
earthquakes, etc) is direct evidence that there is energy (the ability
to "move things") stored there!


** 1. Residual thermal energy from formation
As planets accreted matter from the protoplanetary disk, typically
through a disk because matter interacts with itself as it falls and
conserves the total angular momentum, it also accretes thermal energy
(the source is the gravitational potential energy of the infalling
matter). Part of this residual /accretion/ heat is still trapped in planets and
acts as an energy source.

** 2. Differentiation

#+HTML: <iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/sV0SxwaVKN8?si=vJNr3lMCQPUTamJ-" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

Imagine to pour oil in a glass of water, in a messy way, and then let
it sit for a long time. The oil would progressively rise at the top,
because it is less dense -- there is a buoyancy pushing it up. This
process releases some energy (it was effectively excess gravitational
potential energy of the water temporarily above the oil).

Similar things can occur in a planet (or a star!), although the time
to wait is literally /geological/ (100s of millions to billions of
years, as old as the Solar system!). This process of rearrangement is
called /differentiation/, and is also a source of energy in a planet

** 3. Radioactive decay
Add the radioactive decay of material the planet is made of (tapping
into the nuclear energy stored in the structure of those nuclei), and
you have all three major sources of heat in a planets /interior/.


* Energy transport
*N.B.:* The concept we introduce here to discuss the structure of
planets are /exactly the same/ to discuss the structure of stars.

The total energy in the interior of a planet discussed above
ultimately produces the thermal emission of the planets, typically
peaking in the infrared (the details depend on the temperature of the
last layer of the planet from where photons can escape).

This means planets lose energy (or cool) from the surface: energy must
be moving from the interior outwards. Roughly speaking, energy in a
blob of matter can move in three main ways:

** Conduction (electrons carry the energy)

- TOP HAT Q2

Matter, especially if solid, doesn't have many moving parts to carry
energy. The most movable are the electrons in the outskirts of
molecules/atoms bound in crystals.

Energy transport by motion of electrons is called /conduction/. Metals
are good at conducing heat because their electrons are typically quite
"loose" and this is why they heat up evenly even if only one part is
left in the Sun.

** Radiation (photons carry the energy)
In a medium in which light can pass (remember that light has many
wavelengths!), energy can be carried by photons. This is typically
more important in stars and outer layers of planets than their
interior.

** Convection (bulk flow of matter carries the energy)
This is when energy is carried by bulk motion of matter: convection
implies there s some stuff going up and some stuff going down, with
one of the two sides carrying more (thermal) energy even if the mass
cancels out. Bulk motion of matter, no net mass flux, but with an
energy flux.

This is an /instability/ meaning convection kicks in only if the other
possible energy transport are insufficient.


#+CAPTION: You can see convection in the Earth's atmosphere, for example cumulonimbus clouds form with a column of rising humid hot air, which cools and condensates into the cloud as it rises.
[[./images/cumulonimbus.jpg]]

A common misconception is that water boiling is exhibiting convection:
that is not exactly correct. Water boiling is /by definition/ a phase
transition from liquid to gas, however, shortly before the phase
transition occurs, it is true that conduction in the water is
typically insufficient to carry the energy release at the bottom, and
convective motion can be spotted as a small simmering of the water
breaking down into small waves at the surface.


It's maybe easy to picture convection in gas (atmosphere) and liquid
(water "simmering" just before it starts boiling with large bubbles),
but in planetary interiors, even solid can participate in convection!
This is a very slow motion (compared to for example the thermal
velocities), but unstoppable!

Geological timescales are comparable to the age of the planets, that
is the age of the Solar system, up to \sim4.5 Billion years. Many
processes can take hundreds of millions of years to shape a planet.

The fact that even rock over long timescales behaves like a fluid! We
can thus study the /evolution/ of the surface of a planet to learn about
their formation, evolution, and outer conditions!


* Why are planets spheres?

If we wait geological times, in a planet, which by definition is /self
gravitating/, fluid rock will shape itself to minimize the
gravitational potential it has. In other words it will "fall on
itself at the lowest possible height".

Gravity is a central force, only depends on $r$
#+BEGIN_LATEX
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F_{1}} = \frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\mathbf{\hat{r}} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+END_LATEX

so the shape that minimizes the energy is a sphere, the region of
space equidistant from a point, the center.

For non self-gravitating bodies, e.g., small asteroids, the shape is
determined by the detailed properties of the matter they are made of.

#+CAPTION: The shape of smaller bodies can be arbitrary because mass is not strongly pulled on itself by its own gravity. As size grows, dwarf planets become spherical which is an indication that they are massive enough to be self-gravitating.
[[file:images/asteroids_shapes.png]]

Small, non-self-gravitating, asteroid are effectively "piles of
rubble" loosely held together by gravity and shaped by how the parts
lock into each other, without gravity crushing down the parts into a
nice spherical shape.

* Different planets \rightarrow different structures \rightarrow different surfaces

#+CAPTION: The properties of planets determine the agents shaping their surfaces.
[[file:images/planet_sizes_effects.png]]

Since heat storing scales with volume, but heat losses scale with
surface:

#+BEGIN_latex
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Energy\ loss} \propto \mathrm{Surface} = 4 \pi r^{2}\\
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Amount\ of\ energy \propto Volume} = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+END_latex

The larger r the smaller the Surface/Volume \propto $r^{-1}$ is, therefore
larger planets hold on to more of their internal energy.

Small planets also have smaller mass and thus gravity: it is harder to
hold on to their ever-evaporating atmosphere for long!

For each star, there is a range of distances defined as /habitable/ if
liquid water is possible at the surface of the planet. Too close and
the surface will be too hot, too far from the star it would be too
cold. In the Solar system, Earth, Mars and Venus are all in this
region, and yet because of their surface evolution, only Earth does
have liquid water at the surface presently. Geological and atmospheric
process compound with the distance from the host star to determine the
chemical and physical conditions at the surface!

* Shaping the surfaces

** Tectonics
Tectonic plates are slabs of solid rock "floating" over the mantle of
a planet, and slowly drifting, bumping in each other, sinking, and rising.
This is driven from below by convection in the mantle!

#+CAPTION: Tectonic plates on Earth.
[[./images/tectonic.png]]

The boundaries between the plates are often areas of relative
instability, with outflows in the form of volcanoes (great pacific
ring of fire), earthquakes (notoriously, California or Nepal).

Whether tectonics occurs on bodies other than Earth can be studied in
the Solar System. For instance, Moon missions have left radio
detectors, which allow to measure its size very precisely and detect
its shaking: the Moon being small, it has no tectonic (anymore)
because it has already lost most of its formation residual energy.

** Impact cratering

We have seen how gravitational dynamics between N>3 bodies plays a
chaotic role in the solar system evolution. Minor bodies in the solar
system are often perturbed and knocked off their long-held mildly
elliptical orbit, to become space projectiles on very eccentric
ellipses, or even parabolae/hyperbolae. Occasionally these can hit
something else.

Earth's atmosphere acts as a shield: most of these objects heat up due
to friction with the air, and consume themselves becoming a "shooting
star". Some hit the ground as meteors. When this occurs this can leave
a crater!

#+CAPTION: Meteor crater natural landmark in Arizona is the impact crater from a [[https://news.arizona.edu/news/did-meteor-impact-trigger-landslide-grand-canyon][collision roughly 50000 years ago]].
[[./images/meteor-crater.webp]]

 - TOP HAT Q3

The Moon is small so it managed to lose its internal heat, and let its
atmosphere escape, so more craters form there and they don't weather
down very much. The same argument applies to Mercury!

#+CAPTION: Example of impact craters on the Moon. The lack of atmosphere prevents any slowing down or consumption of incoming bodies.
[[./images/moon_crater.jpg]]

From the way craters degrade in time, we can age date them, and
looking at areas of the moon that are rarely hit (dominated by
old-looking craters) we can determine when most of these were formed.

It turns out this is likely roughly when the gas was cleared from the
protoplanetary disk by the Sun, resulting in a decrease in the mass of
the disk, and a re-arrangement of many orbits, causing also migration
of the gas giants and including many comets from the Oort cloud. The
decreased total mass and changes in position of the biggest objects
(excluding the Sun) cause a re-arrangement of the orbits of the minor
bodies, and put many of those in route towards the inner solar system.
And we know from looking at the distribution of age of craters on the
Moon!

Major impacts are rare today, the solar system is mature and most of
the violent orbital perturbations have ceased (because they mostly ran
out of stuff to push in the Sun or out of the solar system). A recent
spectacular example was the comet [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_Shoemaker%E2%80%93Levy_9][Shoemaker–Levy 9]] impact with Jupiter
in 1992:

#+CAPTION: Hubble space telescope ultraviolet image of Jupiter showing multiple impact sites from the Shoemaker-Levy 9 comet pieces falling on Jupiter.
[[./images/shoemaker_levy.png]]

Despite the spectacular impact, because Jupiter is a gas giant, its
"surface" is not solid (it's defined by the maximum depth at which
photons can penetrate, but there is no solid material there, the gas
blocks the photons), so we don't expect it to have formed craters.

*** The role of collisions in Earth's history
Earth has also been subject to "bombardment" from minor bodies earlier
in the Solar system evolution. We know of craters on Earth (one of the
most recent one is in Arizona!), and we have already seen some
consequences of the bombardment for the history of Earth:
- major collision with Mars-sized body \rightarrow creation of the Moon,
  peculiarly large, moving outwards, and with a \sim half-earth-like and
  \sim half non-earth-like composition
- how most of the water arrived on Earth: it condensed far from the
  /snow line/ in the outer solar system, and was brought to us by comets
  "panicked" by the change in the disk and thrown into orbits crossing
  the inner solar system.
- extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs

The last one however is often misunderstood in popular culture. About
66 million years ago, at the transition between Cretaceous and
Paleogene, about 75% of life forms on Earth went rapidly extinct. But
/rapidly/ has to be understood on ecological and geological terms: it is
unlikely that this means minutes/hours/days! In fact, the hypothesis
that this mass-extinction event comes from an impact was first
suggested based on finding a layer of minerals (iridium) /all over the
surface of the world/. This mineral forms often in violent impacts
between rocks (which provide the energy to reshuffle the crystal
structures at molecular/atomic level), and the fact we find it all
over the surface of the Earth suggests that the hashes from such
collision spread /globally/ on the surface of the Earth.

This suggests that the impact lifted so much material to cause a
change in the global climate, resulting in an overall cooling of the
Earth, massive death of plants, which disrupted the entire food chain
leading to the mass extinction especially of large animals.


#+CAPTION: Chicxulub crater: Magnetic and gravitational anomalies (colors) allowed to identify the site of a giant impact in the Yucatan peninsula, likely the start of the extinction event at the end of the Cretaceous period. The impact likely melted iron rocks, allowing to re-arange their magnetic fields according to the melted flow caused by the impact and causing the anomalies.

* Magnetosphere

/What about magnetic-fields?/ This question is almost a running joke
among astrophysicists, because magnetic fields are ubiquitous when
discussing ionized gas (i.e., freely moving charged particles), but
typically complicate significantly the dynamics of the system.


Much like /velocity/ is defined in a [[file:notes-lecture-05-gravity-motion.org::*Galilean invariance][reference frame]], and depending on
the observer's relative velocity to it an object can be moving or
static, /magnetic fields/ and /electric fields/ are related like motion
and not moving depending on the observer. The bottom line is that
magnetic fields in planetary context are generated by /electric
currents/.

Thus, for a planet to have a magnetic field it requires a source of
energy, and a mechanism to generate currents, for example fast
rotation! This is not enough, there should be something that can /carry/
the current, like liquid metal.

As we have seen the residual heat from formation (gravitational
collapse), and to a smaller degree the radioactive decay of trapped
heavy unstable elements act as source of heat in planets. This causes
convection (transport of heat by macroscopic motion). If rotation is
fast and the planet is not "too stiff", meaning there is a liquid
layer, rotation "twists" the convection generating currents which make
a magnetic field.

The Earth meets these conditions, its magnetic field roughly has a
North-South direction (that's because Earth's rotation is involved in
generating it!), so it can be used to navigate by compass.


#+CAPTION: Visualization of the density of plasma around the Earth (color) and Earth's magnetic field lines. The reddish "rings" are the van Allen bands, where the Earth magnetic fields traps some of the solar wind.
[[file:images/Earth_B.png]]


On a planetary scale, it protects the Earth from the solar wind: this
is a stream of high energy protons investing us. Protons are charged
and a moving electric charge is deflected by magnetic fiels (Lorentz
force). [[https://cosmic-watch.com/magnetosphere/][This visualization]] shows the pressure around the Earth as a
function of the impinging wind (mostly protons) constantly escaping
from the Sun.

*N.B.:* We can literally /see/ what was happening at the surface of the
Sun 8 light-minutes ago, because that is the time it takes a /photon/
traveling at the speed of light to cover the Sun-Earth distance (1
au). The protons of the solar wind are more slower than photons, so we
can use this to help predicting some of the solar wind variability.

*N.B.:* Mars, slightly smaller than Earth, has lost enough of its
formation heat and has no molten core anymore: this means it has lost
the ability of carrying currents and generating a significant magnetic
field. This makes its surface more exposed to stellar winds, and
probably contributed to the evaporation of its atmosphere.

The magnetic field of a planet can accelerate particles from the solar
wind (typically protons) and make them smash into molecules in their
atmosphere, causing aurorae. [[https://www.spaceweather.gov/communities/aurora-dashboard-experimental][This website]] shows forecast predictions
on the probability of aurorae based on the Sun's activity.

#+CAPTION: Aurora Borealis showing two colors, because protons of different energy coming from the solar wind and deflected by the magnetic field of the Earth hit different molecules (likely nitrogen and oxygen).
[[./images/aurora.jpg]]


Any planet with a magnetic field can have aurorae! In Jupiter case,
the aurorae interact with the (close by) Moon Io, which is volcanic
and its volcanoes can eject charged particles which can be channeled
by Jupiter's magnetic field towards the magnetic poles where aurorae
most likely form.
