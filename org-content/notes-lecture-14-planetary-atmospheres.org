Following the spirit of [[./notes-lecture-13-planetary-geology.org][last lecture]], we will focus today on the
/physical processes that shape the atmosphere of planets/. Once again,
the physics is the same everywhere, but the specific conditions
(composition, distance to the star, etc.) of each body in the Solar
system - and in exoplanetary systems! - determine the variety of
atmospheric conditions we can find.

And also in analogy with planetary geology, /comparative/ planetary
atmosphere studies can teach us about our own atmosphere, and the
massive damage that \sim 200 years of industrial extraction of fossil
fuels is doing to it. So while it may seem wasteful to study the
atmospheres of other planets, it's actually one of the best possible
ways to understand the physics of the complex and chaotic atmosphere
on Earth, which we cannot replicate at scale in a lab.

* Atmosphere vs. envelope

- *Atmosphere*: partially transparent to some light (photons coming from
  this layer have at most one interaction with the gas before
  free-streaming outwards). The definition of atmosphere is thus
  related to light-matter interactions.

- *Envelope*: [[./notes-lecture-12-planet-formation.org][we have discussed]] planet formation as a bottom-up process
  mediated by disks, where a solid core is formed first, and then
  accretes gravitationally attracted mass. One can think of everything
  outside the core as an envelope, which however can be solid (e.g.,
  Earth's mantle) and this statement doesn't say anything about its
  radiative properties (no transparency requirement)

** The Earth

#+CAPTION: Earth's atmosphere is a thin layer, roughly exponentially stratified (no sharp edge), with the bulk of the mass below an altitude of $\sim100\,\mathrm{km}$.
[[./images/atm.webp]]

The Earth's atmosphere is a thin layer of gas, cold enough to be in
the form of molecules:
- Nitrogen N_{2} makes the majority of it
- Oxygen in the form of O_{2} molecules is \sim 16%
- Other molecules, such as CO_{2}, H_{2}O, CO, CH_{4}

The gas is held by the planet's gravity and has a roughly
exponentially decaying density: there is no sharp boundary, although
above about $100\,\mathrm{km}$ the density becomes so low that
collisions between molecules are rare. This is conventionally defined
as the boundary between the atmosphere and space. Compared to the
radius of $\sim 6000\,\mathrm{km}$ of the planet, the atmosphere is a very
thin layer.

It is transparent to what is /by definition/ the visible range of the
electromagnetic spectrum, but blocks large portions of the high-energy
photons produced by the Sun.

- Top Hat Q2: why is the sky blue?

Throughout the \sim 4.5 billion-year-long history of the planet, the
composition of the atmosphere has evolved: early bombardments by
comets depositing large amounts of H_{2}O, long periods of volcanic
eruptions releasing CO_{2} and other gases trapped in the interior,
biological processes (cyanobacteria releasing molecular O_{2}), etc. The
modifications these processes introduced in the chemical composition
of the atmosphere, and consequently the physical processes that
determine the status of the atmosphere. We have multiple geological
and fossil evidence (including "carrots" from the ice caps at the
poles, which literally froze in time conditions). Together with the
process of surface /erosion/, this is shows that *the atmosphere of a
planet is connected to its interior*.

* Atmospheric structure

** Stratification
Although evolving on geological and planetary timescales, the
atmosphere is globally stable: it is in hydrostatic equilibrium. The
weight of the gas on top of a column compresses the gas below, and
gravity from the underlying body holds it in place. The change in
pressure of the gas balances gravity out so the net force is zero and
there is a static equilibrium. Per unit volume we have:

#+BEGIN_latex
\begin{equation}\label{eq:HSE}
F_{g} = - \frac{GM\rho}{r^{2}} = \frac{dP}{dr} \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+END_latex

The balance between gravity and the pressure gradient sets the zeroth
order /structure/ of the atmosphere.

*N.B.:* Eq. \ref{eq:pscale} is an approximate solution for the
hydrostatic equilibrium /structure/.

*** Pressure
The pressure $P$ is the force (that is change in momentum by Newton's
second law) per unit area caused by the momentum exchange between the
molecules of the atmosphere colliding. The thermal velocity of these
molecules is of 100s of m/s at room temperature, each molecule moving
in a random direction and bumping into everything! Because the
direction of the thermal motion of the molecules is random and equally
likely in every direction, pressure is /isotropic/, meaning that it is
the same in every direction.


#+CAPTION: The pressure is caused by the momentum exchange in collisions between gas particles moving because of their finite non-zero temperature. Since this motion is random, pressure is isotropic.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/baloon_pressure.png]]

You don't /feel/ any pressure from the atmosphere because the stuff your
body is made of pushes outward with an equal pressure, and because
there is no /direction/ for pressure: it applies the same amount in
every direction! But you can feel pressure changes, for example on
take off and landing of a flight. This is called /adiabatic expansion
and cooling/ where "adiabatic" means that there is no exchange of heat
between the flow and the environment, and the cooling comes just from
the expansion.

Pressure is naturally related to temperature (which is what causes the
velocity of the molecules in the first place), but also density! For
instance you can feel as you exhale that the air is expanding and
cooling, which lowers its pressure as it exits your lungs.

Depending on the relationship between thermodynamical quantities
(pressure $P$, temperature $T$, density $\rho$), so-called /equation of
state/ (EOS), which in turn depends on the chemical composition, and
parameters such as humidity, we can solve for the structure of the
atmosphere of a planet.


#+CAPTION: Temperature on the x-axis with altitude for the Earth. The wiggly arrows show the depth of penetration of photons in the indicated wavelength ranges.
[[file:images/atm_struct_Earth.png]]

- The troposphere is the lowest layer, in which tempera-
ture drops with altitude (something you’ve probably
noticed if you’ve ever climbed a mountain).
- The stratosphere begins where the temperature stops
dropping and instead begins to rise with altitude. High
in the stratosphere, the temperature falls again.*
- The thermosphere begins where the temperature again
starts to rise at high altitude.
- The exosphere is the uppermost region, in which the
atmosphere gradually fades away into space.

** Energy balance

Atmospheres (together with [[file:notes-lecture-13-planetary-geology.org::*Magnetosphere][magnetospheres]]) /filter/ the radiation from
the host star, protecting the planets from some of the high energy
photons, and trapping some photons allowing to store some energy in
the form of thermal energy.

An atmosphere of a rotating and orbiting planet is constantly being
/forced/ on the day side by the irradiation of the star providing
energy. This means that an atmosphere, like any other system, will try
to balance out the energy input on one side, and reach some
equilibrium, but the constant and asymmetric driving constantly
perturbs them. Atmospheres of planetary systems are /dynamic/ systems!

The constant injection of energy has to be balanced out by the thermal
radiation losses of the planet. Recall that the thermal radiation
(black body) only depends on the temperature $T$. In a stably
stratified atmosphere, the temperature /structure/ as a function of
height $T(z)$ will depend on how easily energy can /flow/ through each
layer.

The processes to transport energy in the interior of a planet and in
its atmosphere are the same: radiative energy transport by photons,
conduction by electrons, and convection by bulk motion of matter if
the first two are insufficient. In an atmosphere (by definition
transparent to some radiation), the densities are typically low
($\lesssim 1\,\mathrm{kg\ m^{3}}$) and conduction by electrons is not
very efficient: radiation and convection are typically more important.

*N.B.:* In fact we have already seen an atmospheric example of
convection in cumulonimbus clouds!

The fact that the energy input and output of a planet need to match,
together with the processes that determine the energy losses of the
planet, determine its temperature structure. At any $T\neq0$, particles
(in this case molecules of the atmosphere) have a certain distribution
of random velocities with average value determined by $T$, and some
will be fast enough to escape the gravity of the body! Smaller (in
mass) bodies like Mercury or the Moon have weaker gravity and have
completely lost their atmospheres.

The injection of energy on one side of the planet causes constant
"churn" to transport and redistribute the energy: on a /local/ scale (in
space and time) this causes the /weather/. Increasing the scale
spatially and averaging over longer timescale we have the /climate/.

** Rotation

Adds a force (the centrifugal force) to the hydrostatic balance, which
corresponds to another term in Eq. \ref{eq:HSE}. On the rotation axis
one feels no centrifugal force, it only depends on the distance from
the rotation axis $r=R\sin(\theta)$, and it is maximum at the equator.
*N.B.:* This affect also the solid part of a planet if any!

This causes planets to deviate from a perfect sphere, and be "bloated"
at the equator! This is particularly relevant for the gas giants that
rotate very fast for their size.

* Greenhouse effect

#+CAPTION: Schematic representation of the "green house effect" caused by gas particularly opaque in the infrared.
[[file:images/greenhouse.png]]

Energy conservation in a planet requires that at equilibrium, what
goes in must come out.

In this context, what goes in is energy (in the form of radiation)
from the host star, part of which is reflected by the planet - this is
how we /see/ other planets in the solar system! - and part is absorbed.
But the planet temperature doesn't increase steadily over time: the
energy is re-emitted with the thermal spectrum of the planet, which
typically peaks in IR (as opposed to the hotter Sun peaking in the
visible range).

The re-emission of this energy occurs through radiative processes: the
composition of what is emitting (i.e. the planet) matters! In
particular, planets such as Venus rich in so-called "green house
gasses" have many molecules that contain a very large number of
electronic transitions corresponding to the energy of IR photons. This
makes it hard for photons of the thermal radiation to leave the
planet: instead of leaving, they keep on bumping into electrons of
these molecules and giving them their energy. The molecules will also
de-excite through emission of IR photons, but this process is random
in direction: an outward moving photon will on average be sent
backwards many times before making it out. The energy is effectively
/trapped/, and the temperature of the planet increases compared to if it
did not have a blanket of green-house gas molecules.

*N.B.:* This is a misnomer: greenhouses trap heat not by radiative
processes, but preventing hot air from rising and cooling as it
expands.

The Greenhouse effect is /not/ bad: without greenhouse gases on Earth, it would
be too cold for liquid water! The temperature reached by the planet is
exactly right to achieve a balance between the energy inputs and
outputs with the specific composition.

The current problem with global warming is that human industrial
activities are /increasing/ the number of greenhouse gases in the
atmosphere. This shifts the natural balance point to a hotter
atmosphere for thermal radiation (more trapped than before
industrialization) to balance out the (on average unchanged) solar
input. This make the Earth's chemical composition of the atmosphere
harder to cool down and literally traps thermal energy on the planet.
[[https://science.nasa.gov/resource/interactive-climate-time-machine/][This NASA animation]] shows the global warming of the atmosphere from
1884 to 2022.

*N.B.:* There is no ambiguity in the scientific community about the
occurrence of global warming. There is also no ambiguity and
overwhelming consensus that industrialization of human activities and
consequent release of large amounts of green house gases previously
stored inside the rocky portion of Earth are the driving cause. This
does not exclude minor contributions other than human activities (such
as solar activity, volcanic eruptions, etc.) that we do not control.
But the major source of green house gases that has changed in a
centuries timescale (compared to geological timescales) /is/ undoubtedly
industrial anthropic activity, and it is also the one we can
collectively control.

* Radiative properties

#+CAPTION: typical interaction between photons (increasing energy/frequency going up) with molecules in the atmosphere.
[[file:images/photon-atm.png]]

We have already discussed radiative properties for the green house
effect: it is all based on the opacity of certain molecules to
infra-red radiation.

In the visible range, the dominant source of light-matter interactions
is Rayleigh scattering, where molecules interact preferentially with
higher frequency photons. During the day, the Sun produces plenty of
photons of every wavelength following it's black body emission at
$T\simeq5800\,\mathrm{K}$, but the bluer (higher frequency) photons can
scattered around, creating the blue haze of any significant mass of
air (in any direction, think distant mountains).

#+CAPTION: Diagram of why the sky is blue during the day and red at sunrise/sunset.
[[file:images/bluesky.png]]
At sunrise or dusk, there is more air for sunlight to get through and
the blue frequencies are completely scattered away, becoming too faint
to see, and leaving the red portion visible.

On different planets, with different compositions (and around stars of
a different temperature), the radiative processes dominant and
determining the baseline color of the atmosphere may change!

** Cycles

Analogy maybe some of you know: the Nitrogen cycle in an aquarium.

There aren't many nuclear reactions going on on Earth: some
radioactive decays, some human reactors and bombs, and occasionally
some lightning can cause small nuclear reactions too. This means
overall the nuclei available are reused, and reassemble.

The combination of geological, biological, and atmospheric processes
creates cycles that particular chemical elements (whose properties are
determined by the electronic bonds across the nuclei in their
molecules) are constantly drifting.

Most over billions of years have reached some form of stable
equilibrium, but can be destabilized by exceptional events (collision
with minor body, development of an industrial species, etc...)

* What if there is no "surface"?


#+CAPTION: Gas giant planets of the solar system with Earth for scale.
[[file:images/gas_giants.png]]

So far you probably imagined surfaces as something roughly /solid/.
However, some planets do not have any solid surface at all: the /gas
giants/! However, we can think of a "surface of last photon
scattering", or in other words, the radius above which photons can
freely escape. This is just a statement on the photons propagation,
not the state of matter, which can be in any phase it wants!

Although gas giants have formed starting from solid planetesimal
cores, these may have since melted/vaporized under the extreme
pressure of all the gas they have accreted. This is usually still
debated, because it depends on the properties of matter that is under
extreme pressure (the weight of the huge planets), cold, and with the
specific chemical composition of the giant planets.

#+CAPTION: Comparison of models of the inner structure of Jupiter and the Earth.
[[file:images/jupiter_structure.png]]

Even if these planets are mostly or completely in gas phase, you
should not imagine them as a fluffy cloud that it is easy to go
through: when the /Galileo/ space-craft plunged into Jupiter, it dove
only down to 200km from the surface before being crushed by the
pressure of the gas.

As we will discuss in more detail when dealing with stellar structure,
what holds planets (and stars) up against their own weight and prevent
them from collapsing to a single point is the /pressure gradient/, that
is the change in pressure with depth. The gas at the bottom is
compressed, and has higher pressure than the gas above, and the
difference in pressure between two layers is exactly the balancing the
weight of that layer.

Another force that shapes the gas giants is the /centrifugal force/.
This is an apparent force in the /non-inertial/ frame co-rotating with
the planet (which corresponds in an inertial non-rotating frame to the
/centripetal/ force that keeps the velocity accelerating and changing
direction in the rotating motion). Gas giants typically spin fast
enough that they are visibly deformed from the spherical shape
expected for planets.
