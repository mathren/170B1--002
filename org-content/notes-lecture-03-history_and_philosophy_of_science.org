* History and philosophy of science

Scientific investigation is one of the key skills that allow human
communities to adapt their environments to their own
needs and adapt to the environment themselves.

Defining precisely what science is is not a trivial question, and an
entire field of philosophy concerns itself with this question
(/epistemology/, or the philosophy of /what is knowledge and how we
acquire it/). Surprisingly, a lot of professional scientists carry out
their research without concerning themselves too much with this.

In the broadest sense, science is the /collective process/ of organizing
known and quantitatively verifiable facts about natural phenomena in a
coherent framework that allows us to understand, explain, and predict
further quantitatively verifiable facts.

How this is done in detail throughout the history of humanity is
inextricably intertwined with technological developments that allow
the quantitative verification of factual information!

* A brief history of astronomy
As the record-keeping and description of the motion of celestial
objects (though the course of a night, a month, and years), astronomy
must be one of the oldest scientific endeavor of humanity. There are
at least two reasons:

1. the sky night was accessible to every culture (before light pollution was a thing)
2. knowledge of the sky night offered solutions to practical
   /time-keeping/ and /navigation/ issues: the regularities in the
   apparent motion of the sky provide a natural clock and compass!

Every community on Earth has observed the sky, and every community has
needed time-keeping or navigation. Knowing when cold or harvest season
are coming, or being able to navigate in the night are needs that can
be met with naked eye observations of the sky, and we can be sure that
every community in the history of humanity has developed some form of
astronomical understanding (and associated mythology to transmit this
knowledge across generations, see [[./notes-lecture-02-scale.org::*Constellations][Constellations]]).

As a "system of organizing knowledge", scientific thinking lived under
the broader umbrella of philosophy for most of human history. For
thousands of years, /astronomy/ and /physics/ remained distinct. Many
culture, especially "western" philosophy would typically distinguish
/astronomy/ as the description of /celestial/ (as in non-Earthly)
phenomena and /physics/ as the study of nature as it works on Earth.
These two were clearly distinguished and separated in the Aristotelian
framework of a sub-lunar (Earthly) world and the super-lunar ideal
world made of geometrically perfect circles. Seeking the same
explanations for both "classes" of phenomena was an absurd program in
context. assumed would be fundamentally different and require
different explanations. It took about 200 years, starting in the
1600s, for astronomy and physics to converge into /astrophysics/, that
is the application of the physical laws that apply on Earth and in
laboratories to explain the observed astronomical phenomena.

This occurred first with:
- 16-18^{th} century scientific revolution: unification of gravity on
  Earth and orbital motion of planets by Newton
- early 19^{th}: physics becomes concerned with entropy and
  irreversibility, which suggests that /luminous things/ need evolve to
  /keep up with their energy losses/, meaning things in the sky must
  change and evolve - the stars (and maybe the Universe) cannot be
  static and immutable.
- mid 19^{th} century technological advance: the ability to use spectra of
  light to determine the chemical composition of stars revealed the
  same materials in astronomical setting and on Earth.

Astrophysics allows to test a lot of our physical understanding of
natural processes in conditions that cannot be replicated in a
laboratory experiment (e.g., the highest energy particles ever
detected are cosmic rays, not at LHC or Fermilab, or it is impossible
to make experiments on amounts of mass larger than the mass of the
Earth on this planet, etc.), which have often probed and exposed the
limits of understanding at a given time, allowing for further
refinement and progress.

** Achievements of ancient astronomy
Most cultures have developed some investigation of astronomical
phenomena for both practical and religious or ceremonial purposes. All
these have as a pre-requisite careful observations and an organized
system to verify and pass-on knowledge, the roots of scientific
endeavors.

While all these culture live(d) on the same planet under the same sky
(modulo variations due to their position on Earth), not all focused on
and recorded the same phenomena.

For example, western culture strongly influenced by Aristotelian
thought, was mostly concerned in finding perfect regularities in the
"super lunar" world. This framework lead to a lot of recording of
repeating regular phenomena and hard to predict patterns (planet
motion, eclipse predictions, etc.).

Viceversa eastern-Asian cultures (e.g., in what today is China, Korea,
Japan) were interested in documenting the /changes/ in the sky, which
left much better record of unusual, non-repeating phenomena (e.g., the
appearance of "new sources", such as the explosion of a star becoming
visible from Earth), which although presumably visible in the western
sky too, were largely ignored.

The knowledge and use of astronomy by ancient cultures still reflects
on many aspects of language, commonly used units (degrees, weeks,
months, etc.), and archaeological evidence.

Let's first go over some of these achievements around the history and
the world.

*** Astronomical time-keeping
Within the day, shades cast by long objects can be calibrated to make
a sun-dial. This is one of the primary uses of, for example, Egyptian
obelisks. The division of day-time in 12h (i.e., a full day in 24h)
rather than 5 or 10h/day dates back to ancient Egyptians' choices
(mathematically justified by the large number of integer divisors of
12, allowing for easy sub-divisions.)


#+CAPTION: Obelisks from ancient Egypt were used as Sun dials.
[[file:images/obelisk.png]]

A common example of use of astronomy for timekeeping are the name of
the week days: in English, they are named after the Sun, Moon and the
5 naked-eye planets. By the classical definition, a planet
("wanderer") is anything moving in a "special way" in the sky
including the Sun and the Moon!

#+CAPTION: In various languages, the names of the week days are based on astronomical objects notable for their peculiar motion, namely, the "planets".
[[file:images/week_days.png]]

The word "month" itself is related to "Moon", as it is approximately
the amount of time it takes between a new and full moon (i.e., for the
satellite to go around the Earth, $P=29.5$ days).

On a longer timescale of several months, astronomy allows to predict
seasons and likely weather conditions - a great help for agricultural
planning. In central Africa, certain cultures used the orientation of
the illuminated part of the Moon to foresee the advent of the rainy
seasons:

#+Caption: the orientation of the illuminated side of the Moon depends primarily on the latitude on Earth. At a given latitude though, it depends on the time of the year, because of the relative inclination of the Moon-around-the-Earth and Earth-around-the-Sun orbital planes.
[[file:images/monsoon_season.png]]

Some cultures were so precise in their observations that they became

*** Calendars

Various people in antiquity tracked long-lasting astronomical changes
to create calendars, and keep track of time throughout a year, and as
the years pass.

The modern standard calendar is based on the motion of the Earth
around the Sun (/solar/ calendar), but calendars based on the motion of
the Moon around the Earth exist and are still in use (e.g., muslim or
jewish calendars).

Because the Moon's period is 29.5 days, 12 rounds of the Moon around
the Earth take 12\times 29.5 = 354 days, 11-less than it takes the Earth to
go around the Sun ($P=365.25$ days). This explains why /lunar/ calendars
drift by this amount every year w.r.t. the solar calendar, requiring
periodic "adjustments" to maintain certain astronomical events (e.g.,
solstice/equinoxes) in the same month.

#+CAPTION: Mayan Calendar.
[[./images/mayan_calendar.jpg]]


*N.B.:* The exact duration of a year (orbit of the Earth around the Sun)
is 365d 6h 9m 10s, slightly longer than 365d! This is why roughly
every 4 years we have a /leap year/ where we add a day in February: we
cut \sim 6h every year, and every four we add a day to compensate. The
remaining 9m 10s also need compensation but less often (leap seconds
and extra leap days occasionally).

The Mayan calendars also slowly drifted off-sync from the orbital
motion of the Earth, which is why they were occasionally "changing
calendar" to re-sync. This is what they had calculated would happen at
the end of 2012.

** Development of scientific ideas
Scientific ideas, developed based on quantitative and objective
observations and aiming at explaining them without any super-natural
element, have come with human societies all along their history.

We can follow a specific example, which is models of the Solar system
to see how they developed.

*** From Ancient Greece, through Hellenistic and Muslim world, to modern ideas

#+CAPTION: Ancient greek culture developed at the cross-road between Africa, Asia, and Europe, and was first to record investigating natural phenomena with a mathematical (at the time specifically geometric) description and without invoking super-natural causes.

By being at the intersection between Africa, Asia, and Europe, ancient
Greece was a place where ideas and cultures mixed which produced a lot
of innovative and foundational ideas.

Among these, also ideas on the structure of the Universe based on
mathematical (which at the time meant /geometrical/) models. These
models were spread through Asia and North Africa by Alexander the
great (who had been Aristotle's student), and refined and expanded

influences from Persian, Indian and eastern Asian's thought mixed in
and shaped the evolution of ideas on the cosmos, and through merchants
these ideas were slowly re-discovered in Western Europe. A lot of
mathematical concept where developed, expanded, and refined in this
time, explaining their names starting with /al/ (meaning /the/ in Arabic),

scholar, [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwarizmi][Al-Khwarizsmi]]). With the advent of more religious
conservative regimes in the Ottoman Empire ruling over Baghdad, a lot
of intellectuals fled towards Europe (especially through merchant
routes towards Italy). They brought with themselves translations in
modern languages of the ancient Greek texts and this was crucial to
seed the renaissance and the Scientific revolution of the 1600s often
ailed as the "birth of modern science".

* Kepler's laws

*N.B.:* In physics the use of the term /law/ is different than in the
common language. There is no legislative body prescribing a norm as a
/law/, in physics a law is a minimal and clear enunciation of
empirically sound facts, and they are usually (though not always)
named after the person who formulated this phrasing or with a
descriptive name (better! for example "Zero-th law of thermodynamics"
vs. "Clausius law", the former name gives immediately the context in
which this "law" exists, thermodynamics, the latter just names one
guy which does not say much).

[[./notes-lecture-02-scale.org][Last time]] we have briefly mentioned the achievement of Kepler based on
Tycho Brahe's long observational campaign of the position of Mars.
Kepler's laws are an /empirical/ description of the observations, which
does not explain /why/, but allows to make /quantitative/ predictions
/testable/ with further observations - and tested they were,
consistently outperforming any other available model at the time in
predicting orbits of planets

** 1^{st} Kepler law
The orbit of planets are /ellipses/ with the Sun occupying one of the foci.

Conceptually, this may be the most revolutionary contribution of
Kepler: it does away with the idea of "perfection" of the super-lunar
world held since Aristotle.

An ellipse is by definition the /locus of points for which the sum of
distance to each focus is constant/. It is characterized by a semimajor
axis $a$ and a semiminor axis $b$, or equivalently, by $a$ and the
eccentricity $e=\sqrt{1-b^{2}/a^{2}}$. An ellipse of $e=0$ ($\Rightarrow a=b$) is a
special case of a circle.

#+CAPTION: Planet's orbit are elliptical, with the Sun in one focus
[[file:images/ellipse.png]]

** 3^{rd} Kepler law
Kepler also worked out a mathematical relation between the orbital frequency
of a planet $\omega=2\pi/P$ (where $P$ is the period, $P=1$ year for Earth),
and the semimajor axis of the planet's orbit.


#+BEGIN_LATEX
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:3rd_Kepler}
\omega^{2} a^{3} = G(m_{1}+m_{2}) \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+END_LATEX

where $a$ is the semimajor axis of the elliptical orbit,
is the orbital frequency, and $m_{1}(\gg m_{ 2})$ are the
masses of the star and planet respectively.

Using the definition of $\omega$, this can be re-written as:
#+BEGIN_LATEX
\begin{equation}
\frac{a^{3}}{P^{2}} = \frac{G(m_{1}+m_{2})}{4\pi^{2}} \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+END_LATEX
and for the Solar system, where $m_{1}=M_{\odot} \gg m_{2}$ is the mass of the Sun
which is much larger than any other mass, we can use $m_{1}+m_{2}\simeq m_{1}$. The
right hand side is now a constant that depends only on the mass of the
Sun $m_{1}=M_{\odot}$, a physical constant ($G$) and a mathematical constant
$\pi$, so it's the same for every body in the Solar system. To get that
constant (which does have physical dimensions!) we can use the values
on the left-hand side that we know for Earth: if $a$ is measured in
astronomical units and $P$ in years, then $a=1\,\mathrm{au}$ and
$P=1\,\mathrm{yr}$, so the equation becomes:
#+BEGIN_LATEX
\begin{equation}
\frac{a^{3}}{P^{2}} = \frac{G(m_{1}+m_{2})}{4\pi^{2}} \equiv \frac{1 \mathrm{au}^{3}}{1 \mathrm{yr^{2}}} = 1 \frac{\mathrm{au}^{3}}{\mathrm{yr^{}2}} \ \ .
\end{equation}
#+END_LATEX

:Question:
- how many km is 1 au?
:end:

*N.B.*: While the formula on the book focuses your attention on the
important scaling $\omega \propto a^{3/2}$, it is definitely /wrong/ because if
equates a period (indicated with $p$ on the book) squared, which has
the units of [t^{2}] to a semimajor axis ($a$) cubed, which has the
dimensions [L^{3}]. This is /not/ physically sound, it's like saying you
are 1h tall! What the book is doing is glossing over important
physical constants, in this case G \times a mass which fixes the dimensions.

* Scientific models

To achieve this level of predictions of astronomical phenomena and
competency in using them (for navigation, time-keeping, and cerimonial
effect), all these cultures engaged in some form of scientific
investigation.

Fundamentally, science requires making objective observations (e.g.,
where the Sun rises on a given day), and formulating a hypothesis that
may explain said observation without resorting to super-natural
explanations. From the hypothesis, testable consequences should
follow. This is [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper][Karl Popper]]'s /falsificationism/: an hypothesis need to
lead to testable predictions to be scientific, something typically
missing in anything trying to "predict the future". The hypothesis can
thus be tested on its predictions of observations/experiments, and
either refined or confirmed.

#+Caption: Simplified scheme of the scientific method (Popper's vision). In the grander scheme, diverse observations also need to fit together in a coherent picture, and there may be some observations that do not fit that are temporarily ignored or addressed as "special cases" (e.g., the motion of planets for geocentrism)
[[file:images/scientific_method.png]]

However, Popper's falsificationism is only a part of the story: many
scientific theories have remained fashionable and dominant for
centuries while observations falsifying them were available.

The most notable example is perhaps the /precession of Mercury/: the
small deformation in Mercury's orbit shifts position every year at a
rate incompatible with Newton's law of Gravity, a fact that was well
known and waved away for centuries before Einstein's theory of general
relativity could reconcile the understanding of gravity with the
observations.

Therefore, while the basic process of falsificationism governs the
day-to-day performing of experiments, there is an overarching
scientific /culture/ which determines on which problems to focus and
which problems can be set aside and/or explained away as "special
cases" during /normal science/. Only when technological and/or
theoretical advances allow a new point of view on these normally
ignored problems they become of interest and this can lead to a
/scientific revolution/ (these are the ideas of [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn][Thomas Kuhn]], [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imre_Lakatos][Imre
Lakatos]], etc.).


#+CAPTION: Characteristics of valid science
[[file:images/hallmarks.png]]


Note how every culture has applied consistently seeds of these method
in various settings, suggesting maybe that the successfulness of this
approach will naturally be recognized by human cultures, which will

** Occam Razor
Another key tenant of scientific investigation is to strive for
/simplicity/. This doesn't mean shying away from complex math (often
physicists have had to develop or rediscover new forms of math for
their needs), but it means that a good scientific /theory/ has to make
the smallest possible number of assumptions to explain the largest
number of phenomena.

This is often referred to as "Occam's razor", because the simplicity
criterion allows to shave off most explanation and prefer always the
simplest one.

#+CAPTION: Occam was a monk who proposed that all else being equal, the simplest explanation should be preferred.
[[file:images/occam.png]]


For example: in ancient Greece most thinkers favored a geocentric
model of the Universe. This was the simplest at the time: it did not
require to explain the lack of parallax observed for stars, despite
requiring adjustments (in the form of epicycles) for 5 special objects
(the planets), the Sun and the Moon.

But with Copernicus calculations and Kepler's laws, the simpler model
became a heliocentric one, where no object required any special
explanation (planet motion is perfectly explained), as the main
argument against geocentrism became progressively weaker with the
realization that stars are much farther than originally thought.

* My favorite definition of science
I really like the definition of science by [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mertonian_norms][Robert Merton]], who was a
sociologist, not a philosopher, and in 1942 enunciated a set of
"ethical standards" for the scientific community, summarized by an
acronym, CUDOS:
- *Communism* (sometimes re-phrased as "communalism" to avoid the
  political connotation): science should be done for the benefit of
  everyone, and ideas do not belong to any specific scientist, but to
  the community as a whole
- *Universalism*: scientific validity is independent of location,
  institutions, status
- *Disinterestedness*: scientific research should be driven by
  curiosity, not profit or desired outcome (Note how applied research
  in a company or for defense purposes does not count as /science/ by
  this definition!)
- *Organized Skepticism*: the scientific community, collectively, tests
  ideas and claims through an /organized/ process that can/should/does
  question all hypothesis and steps, but in an organized and
  methodological way that allows for progress.

What I particularly like of this framing is that it is /not/
prescriptive, and it leaves the agency with the scientific community,
allowing for progress and evolution of the scientific method, while at
the same time it gives a responsibility to the community as a whole.

Of course, science is a /human/ enterprise, therefore the "actors" are
flawed human beings with their own personal motivations. The "Science"
with a capital "S" doesn't belong to any specific individual, but to
the whole community which with enough time and testing and
verification tends to catch, identify, and shun frauds and unethical
behaviors (e.g., [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancet_MMR_vaccine-autism_report][Vaccines cause autism fraudolent paper]], [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber_bar][Weber's
resonant bar gravitational wave detectors]], various "cold fusion"
claims, etc.).

In other words: one single paper from one single author may be as
wrong as anything. But the community consensus on a specific
scientific issue is much slower to move, and harder to manipulate,
which guarantees the /collective/ and /self-correcting/ mechanism of
science.
