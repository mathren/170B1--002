The star around which every other star seems to turn around is
Polaris, the north star. It actually only coincidentally is close to
the direction pointed at by Earth's rotation rate! This star is not
even particularly bright, but identifying it helps finding the
direction of North, which is useful for navigation.

It can be found identifying this /asterism/ (which is a "piece of a
constellation") in the sky

#+CAPTION: Big dipper asterism, the last two stars define a visual "pointer": going in that direction for about \sim 5\times the angular distance between the two stars one finds the North star (aligned with the Earth's rotation axis, so all other stars appear to go around it each night).
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 100%
[[./images/big_dipper_north.png]]

This constellation was also used as a "test" to see if your eyesight
was sufficiently good to be an astronomer in hellenistic times
(pre-telescopes!). The test was to /find among the 7 stars which is a
visual binary/, i.e., two separate distinguishable sources.

You can try to do this tonight -- it should be possible only with
perfect vision.

:Answer:
The second star in the handle is actually two stars, Mizar and Alcor
(the latter from the Arabic meaning "hard one to see"). Nowadays, with
telescopes we see that Mizar is actually a "binary of binaries", i.e.
four stars organized as two orbiting pairs, in turn orbiting each
other, and Alcor may be a triple. This puts up to 7 individual stars
in what most see only as one dot of light.

** Constellations
We have already referred indirectly to the concept of a
"constellation". Many different cultures have created narratives about
figures apparently drawn in the sky by stars projected on the
celestial sphere. These have been useful story-telling devices, and
mnemonic tools, but the figures they are based on are just apparent
and temporary.

In fact brighter sources can be seen farther, and in 3D, members of
the same constellation may be extremely far from each other! Moreover,
stars in the Galaxy move around (orbiting the galaxy and because of
their local peculiar velocity), with speeds that depend on their
position. Given enough time (the relevant scale is 100s of millions of
years, short compared to the age of the Sun!), the drawings will
stretch, deform and disappear.

Moreover, our own point of view drifts in time: the Sun and solar
system move. But while this takes millions of years, there are other
smaller motion that happen faster that influence how the sky appears
to us. The Earth's rotation "wobbles" (precess) with a period of 10s
of thousands of years: what was at certain celestial coordinates 2000
years ago is not the same as today! The orbit of the Earth around the
Sun also "wobbles", changing the plane of the ecliptic (i.e., the
plane of the apparent motion of the Sun on the celestial sphere).
