** Celestial sphere

The celestial sphere is an idealized surface of a sphere of infinite
radius centered on Earth over which the images of stars are projected.

#+CAPTION: Depiction of the celestial sphere with constellations and reference points. The celestial sphere is also represented in the Vermeer painting on the [[./index.org][home page]].
#+DOWNLOADED: screenshot @ 2026-08-17 11:18:03
[[file:images/celestial_sphere.png]]

- North and south poles are projection of the Earth's rotation axis on
  the celestial sphere
- The celestial equator is the intersection between the Earth
  rotational equator and the celestial sphere
- zenith is the point right above on the celestial sphere, varies with
  the position of the observer (zenith at the north pole = north star)
- nadir is the opposite point, never visible.


[[https://stellarium-web.org/][Stellarium web]] allows one to see the celestial sphere from different
points of views (on the Surface of Earth, the non-online open-source
application allows also from other solar system bodies!), which can be
used to plan observations. The button in the bottom left allows to
change location of the observer, and the button in the bottom right
allows to move the sky forward/backward in time.

The plane of the ecliptic is the plane traced by the orbit of the Sun
as seen from Earth, or in other words, the plane of the orbit of the
Earth around the Sun. You can see it on Stellarium web using the
checkboxes under "View settings" on the top left.


** Angular distances and size
Because stars are at different distances along the radial direction of
the celestial sphere, it is hard to tell their distance in 3D. But in
projection on the celestial sphere, we can tell the angle between
them. This is a measure of their *apparent* distance in projection, but
for example, it needs to be sufficiently large for two stars to be
distinguishable!

#+CAPTION: We measure angles in degrees, each degree is divided in 60 minutes, and each minute in 60 seconds. Using a base-60 system dates back to Babylonians, which use this number because it has a lot of divisors (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60) allowing for many integer sub-divisions.
#+DOWNLOADED: screenshot @ 2026-08-17 11:24:59
[[file:images/angles_measurements.png]]

Roughly, at arm's length, the angle of sky covered
- by one finger \sim 1 degree
- by one fist \sim 10 degrees
- by a stretched hand \sim 20 degrees

This can be used, for example, to measure the time to sun set: the Sun
goes around \sim 180 degrees every day (half a full circle, the other
half happens during the night), which lasts about 12 hours, so it
moves about \sim 180/ 12 = 15 degrees/hour. When the Sun is about 1.5 fists
height above the horizon, there is still about one hour before it sets
(adjustments due to local topography, e.g., presence of a mountain,
time of the year and latitude are needed to be precise).

We can also define the angular size: a disk of radius $D$ at a
distance $s$ will have an angular size of $\theta = \frac{D}{s}\frac{\mathrm{360
degrees}}{2\pi}$. If the angular size of an object is smaller than
the smallest angle we can measure, typically the diffraction limit,
then it will appear to us like a point-source.

*N.B.:* Angles are by definition the ratio between the length of an arc
along a circumference and its radius, so they are the ratio between
two lengths, which makes them a dimensionless number.

This is true for all stars but the Sun as seen by the naked eye
(because despite their large radii, recall $D\sim R_{\odot}\sim 10^{10}$ cm, their
distances are even larger, $s\sim$ 1-10^{4} ly = 1-10^{4}\times 10^{18} cm) .


** Constellations
We have already referred indirectly to the concept of a
"constellation". Many different cultures have created narratives about
figures apparently drawn in the sky by stars projected on the
celestial sphere. Humans are biologically good at finding patterns,
and patterns among the stars have been useful story-telling devices,
and mnemonic tools.

#+CAPTION: Orion constellation, visible in the winter in the Northern Emisphere.
[[./images/orion_marked.jpeg]]

However, the figures they are based on are just apparent and
temporary. In fact brighter stars can be seen farther, and in 3D,
members of the same constellation may be extremely far from each
other! Moreover, stars in the Galaxy move around (orbiting the galaxy
and because of their local peculiar velocity), with speeds that depend
on their position. Given enough time (the relevant scale is 100s of
millions of years, short compared to the age of the Sun!), the
drawings will stretch, deform and disappear.

#+HTML: <iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/ia0s1G8cdv0?si=HGu_MjUpLIeTv3v8&amp;start=6" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ia0s1G8cdv0&t=6s


Moreover, our own point of view drifts in time: the Sun and solar
system move. But while this takes millions of years, there are other
smaller motion that happen faster that influence how the sky appears
to us. The Earth's rotation "wobbles" (precess) with a period of 10s
of thousands of years: what was at certain celestial coordinates 2000
years ago is not the same as today! The orbit of the Earth around the
Sun also "wobbles", changing the plane of the ecliptic (i.e., the
plane of the apparent motion of the Sun on the celestial sphere).

#+CAPTION: Precession of the Earth's rotational axis.
[[file:images/precession.png]]


Modern astronomy still uses constellations as a useful reference
point, for example many stars visible by naked eye can be referred to
as =<greek letter indicating brightness>= + =<greek name of
constellation>=. The letters are assigned in order of brightness (e.g.,
\alpha Orionis = Betelgeuse is the brightest star in the constellation
Orionis, \zeta Ophiuchi is the 26^{th} brightest in the constellation
Ophiuchus). But each constellation is a specific patch of the
celestial sphere defined as a polygon on it: astronomers /tassellate/
the celestial sphere with arbitrarily defined "constellations" that
roughly match historical constellations.

The zodiacal constellations are those that at a given point in the
year are "hidden behind the Sun", that is the ones along the ecliptic
plane.

#+CAPTION: Zodiacal constellation as those along the ecliptic plane.
[[file:images/zodiac_constellations.png]]


*Which constellations one sees varies with latitude* on Earth (i.e.,
how far north/south one is), *but not longitude* (how far east/west one
is). This is because Earth's rotation is in the West-to-East
direction: different points at different longitudes will see the same
patch of the sky at the zenith at different times (i.e., different
phases of the Earth's rotation). Conventionally, longitude is measured
starting from the Greenwich meridian (from the British empire).

Viceversa, the latitude determines what part of the sky is above the
horizon at any given rotation. The simple example is: could you see
Polaris from the South pole?

* The reason for the seasons

*Q*: What is the reason for seasonal variations in weather patterns?

:Answer:
It is often misunderstood to be an effect of the small deformation
(eccentricity) of the orbit of the Earth around the Sun which brings
it closer/farther, but that is /incorrect/. If it were so, there would
not be an anti-synchrony between Northern and Southern emisphere.

In fact this year, Earth has reached aphelion - the farthest point
from the sun in its elliptical orbit - on July 6, 2026, at 17:31 UTC
(1:31 p.m. EDT). Meaning it was closer to the Sun during Tucson's
winter than during the summer!

The correct reason is *because of the inclination of Earth's rotation
axis with respect to the ecliptic plane*.


#+CAPTION: The inclination of the rotation axis of the Eath w.r.t. the perpendicular to its orbital plane around the Sun.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 75%
[[file:images/incl_angle.png]]
:end:

Since the Earth's rotation axis maintains it's inclination of \sim 23
degrees w.r.t. the direction perpendicular to the plane of its orbit
around the Sun (i.e., the ecliptic plane), depending on whether an
emisphere is pointing "towards" the Sun or "away" from it, it will
receive Sun light at a different angle and for a different amount of
time.


#+CAPTION: The apparent height of the Sun at a given time in the day depends on the time of the year, because the rotation axis of Earth always points in the same direction from each side of its orbit.
[[file:images/equinoxes.png]]


#+CAPTION: Depending on the angle between the Sun and the surface of the Earth, only a fraction of the energy can be harvested by the Earth. Specifically, the cosine of the inclination angle enters in the calculation of the energy flux (amount of energy incoming per unit surface area and time).
[[file:images/sun_irradiance.png]]

The change in daily energy input  from the Sun (due to inclination) and duration of
such input (due to the length of the path on the sky) causes the atmosphere
and Earth below it to be "forced" differently, creating different
climate conditions along the year.

** Equinoxes and Solstices
\sim21 March and \sim 21 September approximately mark the day of the Spring
and Fall equinoxes, which are the days in which the Sun shines for the
same amount of time on both hemispheres. In these days the Sun rises
and sets exactly at East and West, respectively.

\sim 21 June and \sim 21 December are instead the Summer and Winter
solstices, the days where in the northern hemisphere the sunlight is
longest and shortest respectively.

These approximate dates, or more precisely, the astronomical events
occurring on these dates have influenced religions all over the world,
with every culture having some form of winter solstice celebration
(amount of daylight bouncing back and increasing again), equinoxes,
etc.


* The moon

#+CAPTION: The moon is Earth's satellite, which is quite big w.r.t. to Earth itself.
[[./images/moon_detailed.png]]

The moon is a /satellite/, a major astronomical body of the Solar system
that does /not/ orbit the Sun, but a planet instead, Earth. It likely
formed from the impact between Earth and a Mars-sized planet in the
early evolution of the Solar system, which took some mass away from
both bodies and sent it in an orbit around the planet.

While the Moon is still slowly drifting away from Earth, it is still
very close (and very big relative to Earth compared to other moons in
the Solar system), with the Moon-Earth distance being only 64\times the
radius of the Earth.

#+CAPTION: Dimensions of the Earth-Moon-Sun system.
[[./images/earth_moon_sun_dimensions.png]]

The orbit of the moon around the Earth takes about a month (there is a
common root between the word "Moon" and "month"!), which is also its
/rotational/ period: the moon orbits around the Earth and rotates around
its axis at the same rate.

*N.B.:* This is the configuration that minimizes the energy!

This means that seen from Earth, the moon always shows the same
"face". The backside, not visible, is referred to as "dark side", but
it is not dark: it receives as much sunlight as the face towards the
Earth! It is dark to radio waves from Earth, because it is in the
shade of the moon itself as seen from Earth.

** Eclipses

Since the diameter of the moon is about \sim 1/400 of the diameter of the
Sun, but it is about 400\times farther, the apparent angular size of Moon
and Sun is the same. This is a cosmic coincidence, true now and only
in the Earth-Moon-Sun system.

Therefore, when the Moon passes in front of the Sun, or the Earth
passes in front of the Sun as seen from the moon, we have an /eclipse/.
Eclipses on other planets can be impossible (no Moon, misaligned moon
orbit), only anular (smaller angular diameter of Moon cf. Sun) or
partial.

*** Why not every month?

The orbit of the Moon around the Earth and the ecliptic plane (of the
orbit of the Earth around the Sun) are not perfectly aligned: most
months the Moon is just too high/low to pass exactly in front of the
Sun.

* The puzzle of planet motion

** What is a planet?
The word comes from greek "wanderer" because, unlike all other stars,
their motion on the celestial sphere as observed through the years
from Earth seems irregular, relative to background stars.

#+CAPTION: example of retrograde portion of the orbit of Mars, each picture taken at 5-9 days of interval at the same time. Note the planet is brighter at the bottom of the loop, when it is closest to Earth!
[[file:images/mars_retrograde.png]]


In fact, planets exibit /retrograde/ periods of motion, where instead of
going East-to-west, they appear to move in the opposite side. This
always posed a challenge for cosmological models putting the Earth at
the center of the Universe and everything rotating around it -- this
is why epicycles and other modifications to geocentric theories were
always needed to reconcile them with the observations of planets.

*N.B.:* This is the motion as the year progresses, that is as the
planets move around the Sun. Their nightly motion apparent due to
Earths' rotation is always rising at the East and Setting at the west.

***  A faster way to find planets
To use this to identify a planet, one needs several nights of
observations. However, planets have also another visible feature that
one can discern quickly by naked eye: /unlike stars, planets don't
twinkle/.

The reason is that while planets are smaller (recall for the Earth $R\sim
6000$ km) than stars, they are also much closer (distances on the
scale 1-10 au, instead of 1-1000 light years). This means their
*apparent size* (see above) is larger. To our eyes, a planet appears as
a marginally extended source (if your eyes were digital cameras,
imagine they would be 4 pixels instead of 1), unlike stars that are
true point-like sources (size less than a pixel).

When the light (from the Sun bouncing off a planet, or another star)
crosses the atmosphere, it will interact with the gas in Earth's
atmosphere. This has a finite temperature (meaning each molecule of
gas moves around a bit) and constant small turbulent motion. If the
light only goes through one patch of the atmosphere moving coherently,
as the patch changes, the light changes, causing the /twinkling/ of the
stars. But for a planet, it doesn't go through one patch only, but a
little bundle of patches. The "wiggling" of the air in the atmosphere
in front of a planet averages to zero and the planet doesn't twinkle.
You can test this tonight!

** An heliocentric perspective on retrograde planet motion
Several people had proposed heliocentric models of the cosmos
throughout history, and it was known it would simplify explaining the
motion of planets. However, accepting an anomaly for a handful of
astronomical object seemed like a good cognitive trade off compared to
other problems heliocentric models produced at the time (see below).

In a heliocentric perspective, the planets (including Earth) go around
the Sun, and the inner planets orbit faster than the outer ones (this
is one of Kepler's laws). Whenever a planet *inside* the orbit of
another (e.g., the Earth's orbit inside Mars'), it will take over, and
at the moment it takes over, relative to the inner planet, the outer
will appear to move backwards.

#+CAPTION: Heliocentric explanation of the retrograde apparent motion of planets.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/planet_take_over.png]]

** Why hold on to a geocentric perspective until the 1600s?
The biggest counter argument to a heliocentric theory in ancient
Greece was not religious, but empirical: if the Earth goes around the
Sun (once a year), it should be on opposite sides every \sim 6 months.

Therefore the stars as seen from Earth should have an apparent motion
(reflecting the motion of the Earth around the Sun), with yearly
timescale, which is called /parallax/.


#+DOWNLOADED: screenshot @ 2026-08-17 14:25:01
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/parallax.png]]

The parallax is just the "apparent motion" of nearby objects (w.r.t.
objects much farther away) due to the change in point of view. Your
brain uses this to reconstruct depth: each of your eyes are at a
slightly different position, so nearby objects have different
positions on your retina in each eye (while distant objects remain
closer to themselves in each eye). Our brains detect this and perceive
it as distance.

A changing of point of view can be used to measure distance by other
animals too (like a pigeon "bobbing" its head or a lizard doing "push
ups").

This effect could /not/ be detected by naked eye in ancient times, hence
the preference for a geocentric system that made it hard to explain
planet motions, but didn't require to explain away the absence of
parallax. Now-a-days, with more precise tools, we do detect and use
this effect. For example 1 pc is the distance Sun-star for a star
showing 1 arcsecond of yearly parallax!

With the detection of astronomical parallax, the problems of a
heliocentric system disappear, leaving the geocentric system just with
the complications of explaining planet motions without any pay off.
