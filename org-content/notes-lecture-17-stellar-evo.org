* Stellar evolution

The solution to the great-debate on the age of the Sun/Earth was that
there needs to be an energy /source/ to compensate for the energy losses
in the form of the luminosity $L$. /Stars don't shine because they
burn! They burn because they shine!/

Since $L$ constantly causes removal of energy, some must be /extracted/
from somewhere to compensate if the system is in equilibrium. But that
means something is /consumed/: the stars must /evolve/!

It turns out the energy source is the nuclear energy that binds
neutrons and protons in nuclei in the core of the star, but that
evolution would be needed became clear even before nuclear fusion
became the apparent energy source. This has profound implication:
stars must be /born/ and they can /die/ (cf. millennia of human history
elevating the Sun to god-like status!).

*Stellar evolution is a constant battle between the gas and gravity.*
Gravitational contraction releases energy and heats up the gas. If in
the battle the gas heats up sufficiently to release its own energy, it
can momentarily delay the gravitational collapse at the expenses of
the energy source, and we get a star. See [[https://lavinia.as.arizona.edu/~mrenzo/courses/545/in-class-evol-wrap-up.html][for a more in depth
description from ASTR545]].

** How can we study stellar evolution

Clearly, stars are overall pretty stable: they don't change on a
nightly basis. So their evolution must be very slow compared to human
lifetimes! How can we study something too slow to observe, and which
is determined by processes happening in the interior of the star that
we cannot see?

1. *Populations*: the slower the evolutionary phase, the more stars we
   see in that phase (like a bottleneck: most cars are where traffic is
   slow). Astrophysics compensates lack of /control over the experiment/
   with very large sample that allow to build /statistical samples/.
   #+CAPTION: Stellar populations can be selected based on location (e.g., a whole Galaxy), or observational properties (e.g., all the stars bluer than some threshold). Controlling /selection biases/ (as in "what fraction of what is out there can your experiment detect?") is often crucial to analyze the data.
   [[./images/Gaia.jpg]]

2. *Simulations*: we can tell a computer the /physics/ that governs
   certain phenomena and obtain a /numerical model/ of, for example, a
   star. Through computer simulations we can access (a representation
   of) the internal /structure/ of a star, and /fast-forward/ time to
   study its /evolution/.
   #+CAPTION: Example of graphical output with many plots showing the interior and the past evolution of a stellar model computed with [[https://mesastar.org/][MESA]].
   [[./images/Grid.png]]

3. In selected cases: neutrinos come straight from the core!

4. Asteroseismology: if we can detect fluctuation in the light of a
   star due to star quakes, we can infer directly something about its
   interior.


Option 2 is what I do, through a variety of numerical tools. These
require combining models of several physical processes (atomic
physics, radiative processes, nuclear reactions, hydrodynamics), and
are therefore very simplified. Currently and for the foreseeable
future simulations of stars will be spherically symmetric: anything
else is way too expensive!

:Quote:
/All simulations are wrong, but some are useful/ - G. Box
:end:

Because simulations are based on a /numerical approximation/ of a
/physical model/ of what nature does, they are necessarily "wrong" at
some level (Nature does not need to write down the equation and teach
a computer how to do things!). By comparing observations with
simulations, we can learn about things we cannot directly see.

This is why the color-magnitude diagram/Hertzsprung-Russell diagram is
a powerful tool: we can /simulate/ the evolution of a star (in terms of
its $L$ and $T_{\rm eff}$ as a function of time), and compare of
populations of stars we can /observe/. This particular diagram allows to
combine and compare empirical data with theoretical predictions!

*** Initial mass function

In general, a galaxy will form stars with a certain distribution in
initial mass and at a certain (possibly variable) rate in time: there
is a star formation rate function $SFR\equiv SFR(M, t)$. Edwin Salpeter in
the 1950s proposed a /factorization/ of this function:

#+BEGIN_latex
\begin{equation}\label{eq:salpeter}
 SFR(M, t) = IMF(M)\times SFH(t) \ \ ,
\end{equation}
#+END_latex

that is write the star formation rate as the product of a function
only of the mass (the "initial mass function" or IMF) and the star
formation history. This is a /big/ and a priori physically unjustified
hypothesis that greatly simplifies the math (no cross terms!). It
stays that the history of how you form stars does not depend on which
stars you have formed first, while in reality stars with their light,
stellar winds, and as we will see, violent deaths, do shape
significantly their environments. However, this /heuristic/ hypothesis
(which means: just a guess to see where it goes) has proven extremely
successful.

Wherever we look, the $IMF(M)$ seems almost *universal*, and the reason
is still unclear and depends likely on the complex physics of large
quantities of plasma falling on itself, and how the energy this
liberates feeds back on the flow (the /star formation/ problem).

#+CAPTION: Schematic representation of a typical $IMF(M)$ showing the number of stars formed in a population as a function of their initial stellar mass. Note that it's a log-log plot: straight lines correspond to powerlaws, and the number of low mass stars outnumbers the high mass stars by much! See e.g., [[https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.322..231K/abstract][Kroupa 2001]] for a commonly used $IMF(M)$
[[file:images/imf.png]]

*** Lowest mass limit

The lowest mass limit for a star is $\sim0.08M_{\odot}$, determined by when the
gas can win over gravity without turning on any energy generation by
nuclear fusion: this never works with a classical gas that heats up as
it is compressed by gravity. Something must change the relationship
between $P$ and $T$ for the gas, and this something is a quantum
mechanical effect: electron degeneracy.

We have encountered electrons already, but there is a property we have
not discussed yet. Electrons are fermions, this means they are
particles that cannot occupy the same energy level: like two people
don't sit on the same chair normally, two electrons cannot occupy the
same energy spot.

As gas is compressed to very high densities, this effects start to
matter: electrons start providing more pressure than if they were a
classical gas, and if the star is not too massive, it's core is cold
enough for this effect to kick in and win over gravity.

At the lowest boundary between stars and objects where
electron-degeneracy pressure is sufficient to stop gravity we find
Brown Dwarfs: these are just big enough to do some secondary burning
(e.g., of deuterium) but not to ignite hydrogen burning reactions and
become a star.

*** Highest mass limit: is there one?
You will find on many textbooks that for sufficiently large mass
$M\ge100-150\,M_{\odot}$ the luminosity of the star $L\propto M^{x}$ with $x\ge1$ becomes
so large that the photon pressure on the gas tears them apart: gas not
only would win over gravity by preventing collapse but even reverting
it!

This however is an oversimplified picture: in the Universe we see
stars /more massive/ than this! While these may be stellar mergers in
binaries, so not at their "original" mass, there are many radiative
phenomena that can prevent the photons from destroying a star, first
and foremost "asymmetries".

#+CAPTION: The most massive stars known are in the core of the cluster R136 in the LMC, and exceed $200-250\,M_{\odot}$. The diagonal lines show the orientation of the spectroscopic slit (the diffraction element) in /Hubble Space Telescope/ WFC3 instrument, and the colors mark estimated masses for the stars, from [[http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.11080][Brands et al. 2022]].
[[file:images/R136_core.png]]


Gravitational wave detections of very massive black holes exceeding
$100\,M_{\odot}$ also suggests the upper limit for stellar mass is higher
than a naive calculation may suggest. This limit thus remains debated.

** Main sequence evolution

#+CAPTION: We have seen that 90% of the stars along the "Main Sequence".
[[./images/Gaia_DR2_HRD_Gaia.png]]

The majority of stars follow a line on a color-magnitude
diagram. We can now interpret this as a group picture of stars: most
are in the /longest and slowest/ evolutionary phase.

Let's do an anti-historical step (I recommend "[[https://openlibrary.org/books/OL26743479M/The_Life_of_Stars_The_Controversial_Inception_and_Emergence_of_the_Theory_of_Stellar_Structure][The life of Stars]]" by
G. Shaviv for the history of the development of these ideas) and let's
assume we /already/ know the energy source of stars. This is not too
bad: the paper proposing nuclear fusion as the energy source in the
Sun is [[https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRev.55.434][Bethe 1939]], and much of the evolution that we are going to
discuss became clear only through the advent of computational sciences
and the collection of large populations later on. The description of
stellar evolution we will discuss is mostly empirically validated by
collecting multiple independent observational constraints that have to
fit together in a /concordance/ picture. Moreover, the detection of
solar neutrinos uncontroversially proves directly that fusion
reactions do occur in the Sun.

This must mean that on the main sequence stars must be burning the
best fuel, that can be burned at a slower rate for compensating the
given luminosity $L$.

#+CAPTION: Nuclear energy per unit nucleon (proton or neutron) as a function of nuclear mass $A$. The structure of this plot is determined by the /strong/ (or nuclear) force.
[[./images/nuclear_binding.png]]

*During the main sequence stars burn hydrogen into helium*. Not only
this gives the best yield per proton/neutron invested in a reaction
(largest $\Delta y$ on the plot above), but stars are made mostly (\sim70%) of
hydrogen, as we know thanks to the insight of Cecilia Payne-Gaposhkin
that spectral line strength correlates with electron population and
not mass abundance. *This explains why 90% of stars are found in this
phase*, which lasts 90% of a stars life!

Nuclear reactions however are not throughout the entire star: the
there is a /structure/ (pressure gradient) needed to support the star
against gravity. For a plasma the pressure $P\equiv P(T,\rho)$ is a function
of the temperature and density! To a pressure gradient corresponds a
temperature gradient: only the core is sufficiently hot to burn.

#+CAPTION: Only the core region of a star is hot enough to sustain a significant rate of nuclear fusion.
[./images/TRho.png]

The Sun is roughly half-way through its hydrogen fuel, at the age of
$\sim 4.5$ billion years. It is in this sense a "middle-age star"

** Mass determines the structure and thus the evolution

We know through the study of stellar binaries (two stars orbiting each
other according to Kepler's laws) that the mass $M$ of a star
determines /where/ on the main sequence it sits, specifically there is a
mass-luminosity empirical relation. It is maybe not surprising that
for an object that is self-gravitating mass is important.

More massive stars are more luminous: therefore they need to burn to
produce a higher amount of energy $L_{\rm nuc}=L\propto M^{x}$ with $x>0$.

This is because /burning is a consequence of the energy losses/. The
energy losses through $L$ are inevitable whenever $T\neq0$ (thermal
radiation), and stars have the $T\equiv T(r)$ /structure/ they have to
support themselves. In a /star/, $T(r=0)$ in the center is hot enough
that nuclear reactions can occur (and $T=T_{\rm eff}=T(r\simeq R_{\star})$ shines in
the optical spectrum). Therefore the nuclear burning is
[[./notes-lecture-15-sun.org::*Stars don't shine because they burn, Stars burn because they
 shine!][self-regulated]], and the star compresses just enough to produce just
$L_{\rm nuc}=L$, which is determined by its mass $M$, and not the nuclear
processes in the core.

Because $x>1$, even if massive stars have more fuel, they burn through
it faster: we only see them young re are very few /old/ massive stars.
The fact that they are /short-lived/ (for stellar standards, we are
still talking 10-100s of millions of years) also explains partially
why they are /rare/. The other part of the explanation is that stars are
so messy as proto-stars accreting from the proto-stellar disk that
it's very rare for a star to become very massive.

As the energy source runs out, gravity takes over again in the fight
with gas. The stellar core slowly contracts, which can cause further
energy sources to turn on as the gas re-arranges. The specifics are
governed by the mass of a star, which has already been setting its
luminosity and burning rate, and thus sets how much and where the
exhausted nuclear fuel (helium rich gas) is.

#+CAPTION: Internal structure as a function of initial mass: gray is /convective/, white is radiative. The red lines show the mass-coordinate within which 50% and 90% of the nuclear burning occurs, while the blue mark the radius containing 25% and 50% of the mass. Note the logarithmic scale on the x axis.
[[./images/conv_ZAMS.png]]

Very low mass stars $\lessim 0.4M_{\odot}$ are low $L$ and /cold/ (cf. CMD
diagram). They still retain gas in molecular form, and it's hard for
thermal radiation to come out (matter is opaque): *very low mass stars
are fully convective*.


#+CAPTION: Mass $M$ determines $L$, thus the gravity, thus the gas structure. Burning is always in the center, but how it connects to the surface depends on $M$.
[[file:images/structure.png]]

Recall that convection is heat transport through bulk motion of
matter: if the whole star is convective, the burning region is
constantly refilled! They have low $L$, so they burn slowly, and
convection refills them (plus they are easy to form): low mass stars
are numerous!

Low mass stars similar to the Sun instead become hot enough that
convection retreats in the outermost (colder and opaque) layers: the
core becomes /radiative/ and thermal energy is transported by photons.
*Sun-like stars have radiative cores and convective enveloped*.

*N.B.:* This does not mean it's /transparent/. It may take \sim 10^{6} years for
a single photon to make its way from the center of the Sun to the
surface (neglecting the fact it's likely going to be absorbed and
re-emitted, making the "single photon" idea questionable)

#+CAPTION: Photons are constantly scattered by free electrons in the plasma, energy flows, but photons are impeded.
[[file:images/photon-path.png]]


As mass increases, so does $L$, so does the weight to support and thus
the steepness of the $P$ gradient needed. All these things results in
hotter central temperatures where hydrogen fusion occurs differently
than in the Sun: the gas finds the most efficient way to generate
energy to compensate $L$ and support itself against gravity!

#+CAPTION: Schematic representation of the CNO cycle active in massive stars, compare the steps with the [[file:notes-lecture-15-sun.org::*Nuclear reactions][pp chain]] dominant in the Sun-like a and low mass stars.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[./images/CNO.png]]


The trick is to use elements other than hydrogen and helium to
/catalyze/ the nuclear reaction. The overall result is still
$4\mathrm{H}\rightarrow^{4}\mathrm{He}+2e^{+}+2\nu_{e}$, but the intermediate steps bypass
the bottlenecks of the pp-chain we have seen by sticking protons onto
carbon and oxygen (temporarily making nitrogen), and ultimately spit
back out the origina CNO elements and helium (CNO cycle). This results
in a /larger/ energy generation: the radiation in the core becomes again
insufficient to carry the energy and convection occurs. Meanwhile,
their position on the color magnitude diagram makes them hot enough to
be rather low opacity. *Massive stars have convective cores and
radiative envelopes*.

The subsequent evolution continues to be determined by the fight
between the gas (producing energy as best it can to slow down the
collapse refueling against $L$) and gravity.

Before we start unpacking the post-main-sequence evolution of stars,
note how stellar evolution is driven by the fight between gravity and
gas pressure gradient, which /causes/ the nuclear burning that has two
main effects:

- delay the collapse by releasing just the right amount of energy to
  compensate for the surface losses
- change the chemical composition.

The famous sentence by Carl Sagan that we are /made of star stuff/ means
this: all the nuclei in the atoms and molecules of your body that are
/not/ hydrogen or helium (made at the big bang) have been /produced/ in
stars (or in their explosions for stuff heavier than the most bound
nuclei, iron and nickel)!

Stars act as *nature's thermonuclear gravitational confinement reactors*
that process light nuclei into heavier nuclei before releasing them in
the host galaxy!

#+CAPTION: Stars cycle (parts of the) gas into heavier elements before returning it to the host galaxy, progressively enriching the galaxies with heavy nuclei.
[[./images/star_recycling.png]]

* Very low mass star evolution
For $M\lesssim 0.4M_{\odot}$, fully convective low-$L$ stars take longer than
the current age of the Universe to run out of H: their evolution is
/only/ main sequence evolution, none of these have exhausted their fuel.

As they evolve, the slow change in chemical composition reflects in
changes in their structure, which causes their $L$ to slowly increase.

** Main sequence
Throughout the main sequence, the luminosity of the Sun increases: /the
Sun used to be fainter!/ This for example implies that the /habitable
zone/ determined by the stellar flux on a planet surface has moved:
when the Sun was fainter early in the evolution, it was further in.

The increase in $L$ is caused by the fact that the composition of the
star is changing (specifically, the average weight of a particle is
changing, since the mass is roughly constant, neglecting solar wind
and nuclear-mass-energy converted into thermal energy by the burning).

** Red giants: degenerate He core, H shell, expanded envelope

At the end of the main sequence, the Sun will have depleted H only in
the very center (recall Sun-like stars have radiative cores).

#+CAPTION: Hydrogen mass fraction $X$ as a function of mass coordinate $m$ for a single, non-rotating, $1M_{\odot}$, $Z=0.02$ =MESA= model across its main sequence evolution. The color go from dark (\sim ZAMS) to light (\sim TAMS).
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 100%
[[./images/1Msun_X_M.png]]

Because the mass is relatively low, the core temperature is also
relatively low: $e^{-}$ degeneracy pressure matters! The He-rich core
will be temporarily sustained by it, while the first layer where H is
available burns in a /shell/ that only sustains the envelope.

Because the shell position is determined by the composition structure
(set by the main sequence structure, set by the $L\equiv L(M)$), that is by
the availability of fuel, its burning is /not/ self-regulated like the
core burning during the main sequence. The nuclear processes occur at
whatever rate is set by the temperature ($\Rightarrow$ thermal motion of nuclei
in the plasma) at the location where fuel is dictated by the stellar
structure. The envelope responds to this by expanding: the star
becomes a /red giant/.


#+CAPTION: Red giant structure.
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 50%
[[file:images/RG_struct.png]]


During the red-giant phase the luminosity is mostly due to:
- slow gravitational contraction of the inert core
- $L_{\rm nuc}$ from the H shell, set by the $T$ at the outer edge of the
  core, also dependent on the core structure.

In other words, the core determines the luminosity (while in the main
sequence the structure and the luminosity where set by the ease of
escape of thermal energy in the form of radiation, so mostly in the
envelope).

Therefore, as a Sun-like star ends the main sequence, it will smoothly
transition to burn H in a shell, with an inert core sustained by
degeneracy pressure.

The empirical validation of this prediction can be found in the
color-magnitude diagrams of clusters, where very bright stars are
always redder (besides being at most \sim 10% of the cluster population).

#+CAPTION: Color-magnitude diagram converted to Herzsprung-Russell diagram for the cluster M4.
[[file:images/M4CMD.png]]


During this phase the Sun will expand by $\sim 100\times$ and the photosphere
will almost (but not quite) reach the Earth's orbit, Mercury and Venus
will likely be swallowed. Don't worry, we still have \sim 5.5 billion
years of fuel left in the Sun.

** Next fuel is hard to start: He Flash

*** 3\alpha reaction
Based on nuclear physics and particle physics experiments, we know
that there aren't many stable nuclei between helium and $A\ge8$: He is
hard to burn because there aren't many /catalyst/ the star can leverage
as intermediate steps to bypass the electromagnetic repulsion between
nuclei.

*N.B.:* This is often referred to as $3\alpha$ channel, because 3 helium
nuclei (aka $\alpha$ particles) meet through the intermediary of making a
temporary $^{8}\mathrm{Be}$ to form carbon.

The next nuclear fuel has to be He:
- the core is now mostly made of it (70% of H was converted to He, and
  \sim25% already was)
- it's the next best fuel in terms of energy release per nucleon

*N.B.:* These will be true regardless of the mass of the star
considered.

This was a problem for a long time: [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Hoyle][Fred Hoyle]] used the existence of
/evolved/ (post-main-sequence) stars to /predict/ the existence of nuclear
energy levels in nuclei to /enable/ He burning. The idea is that you
typically do $^{4}\mathrm{He}+^{4}\mathrm{He}\rightarrow^{8}\mathrm{Be}$, but the latter
immediately decays with a very short half-life. But if $T$ is high
enough, before $^{8}\mathrm{Be}$ can decay it will encounter another
$^{4}\mathrm{He}$ and make an excited nucleus of $^{12}\mathrm{C}$ which
then decays into stable $^{12}\mathrm{C}$. Hoyle used stars to deduct the
out-of-equilibrium nuclear structure of carbon nuclei!

*** Tip of the red giant branch

The movie below shows a calculation by [[https://www.stellarphysics.org/][M. Cantiello]] of this phase: in
the early stellar evolution calculations this was *the* hardest problem,
and held the community back for about a decade.

#+HTML: <iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/2_Km4RTdkPw?si=ZkacE_zcP7g67kIN" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>
# https://www.youtube.com/embed/2_Km4RTdkPw?si=ZkacE_zcP7g67kIN


The structure of a red giant described above can only be temporary:
the He core is only sustained by degeneracy pressure, but the H shell
is adding its hashes to its mass. As the core mass grows, it's
temperature grows. As temperature grows, the electrons in the plasma
of the core can more easily jump to higher energy levels, and the
Pauli exclusion principle doesn't apply anymore (because more energy
levels are available: it's like there are more seats in the venue and
electrons don't need to fight each other for a spot!).

This will lead to a big problem: as the electrons degeneracy pressure
decreases the core will be out of hydrostatic balance, and an
explosion, called He flash will ensue.

Because the breaking of hydrostatic balance always occurs at a
specific $T$, corresponding to a specific mass of the core, and as we
saw above, the core determines the $L$ of a red giant.

Therefore, /all red giants ignite He at the same core mass meaning same
luminosity/. This is the luminosity at the tip of the red giant branch.
The fact that it's a standard value allows for use of this luminosity
as distance indicator for cosmology applications: measure the
brightness $B$, know $L$, infer the distance $r$.

#+CAPTION: Color magnitude diagram in absolute magnitude (corrected for distance) of four globular clusters, the red horizontal line marks the inferred tip of the red giant branch. From [[http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03833][Straniero et al. 2020]].
[[file:images/tipRGB.png]]

*** Horizontal branch
But if all red giants at the ignition of the next fuel have the same
core, and they had different initial masses, this means at the
ignition of He, they have different envelopes!

The less envelope is left by the time the He core has reached its
threshold mass, the larger fraction of the star is turned into core
material, the hotter the star is. But the luminosity, post-He flash,
now comes primarily from the energy generation in the core, and the
core mass is the same, so the luminosity is the same: in a population
of roughly co-eval stars such as a cluster, we find a line at constant
luminosity (set by the core mass) and varying $T_{\rm eff}$ (the lower
the envelope mass the higher $T_{\rm eff}$), see the color-magnitude
diagram of M4 above, or \Omega Centauri in [[file:notes-lecture-16-stars.org::*The Color-Magnitude diagram][the last lecture.]]

We now have a stellar structure with two energy sources (for one
energy loss, the luminosity $L$ at the surface). Moving from the center out:
- He core burning
- Inert He-rich layer too cold to burn (non-burning portion of the core)
- H shell burning
- H-rich envelope (whose properties are set by the core/envelope
  boundary location)

This remains the structure for about 9.something % of the remaining
lifetime (going from \sim90% to 99% of the total life). This is
/consistent/ with the energy release by Helium burning based on nuclear
structure.

*** Red clump
We have seen that the mass of the star sets $L$, thus the burning rate
$L_{\rm nuc}$, and the H burning main sequence covers 90% of the
lifetime. In a co-eval cluster, most stars evolved past the main
sequence have roughly the same mass: they will all sit on the same
spot along the horizontal branch, and form the so called "red clump".

Red clump stars and red supergiants can be distinguished by their
asteroseismic properties (they are both red and big, but their
structure is different, so they support different wave propagation
modes).

** Asymptotic Giant branch (AGB)

Once He runs out in the core, we can reason by analogy with the end of
the main sequence. The composition of the core is mostly carbon (and
some oxygen produced by He burning processes other than $3\alpha$), and if
the mass of the star is not too large, this CO core will be supported
by electron degeneracy pressure.

Above it, we have a He layer, and possibly still the H rich envelope.
This is an asymptotic giant branch star, where the energy to
compensate the losses by $L$ comes from:
- slow gravitational contraction of the core
- H burning shell (set by He core properties)
- occasionally He shell

*** Thermal pulses
The He shell is in fact typically /not/ on: the He that didn't burn
during the red clump/horizontal branch phase was too cold to burn, and
this is still mostly true. Therefore most of the time an AGB star has
an inert CO core, surrounded by an inert He shell (both degenerate)
and a H-rich shell. As the shell adds ashes, like in the He flash
described above, the He layer can ignite in a flash.

This explosive energy release drives the star temporarily out of
equilibrium, pushes out the envelope and turns off both the H and He
shell. With no energy sources, the star then re-collapses on a slow $\sim
E/L$ timescale before the H shell re-ignites first and we return to
the previous structure. What I just described is a "thermal pulse".

AGB stars tend to be a bit larger than RGB stars: the Sun will likely
extend beyond the Earth's orbit in this phase, and swallow the planet.

During this interplay of the thermal pulses, matter can mix from the
intra-shell layer to the envelope, which slowly takes carbon (produced
by He burning) into the envelope: composition can be used to
distinguish AGB from RGB (both are red and luminous).



*** Planetary nebulae

These thermal pulses are also accompanied by dust formation in the
outer layers and copious mass loss: AGB stars are surrounded by their
messy spill overs!

The repetition of thermal pulses divided by thermal timescales
ultimately removes most of the envelope, forming beautiful nebulae
around old stars, called "planetary nebulae" (historical name that has
nothing to do with planets).
