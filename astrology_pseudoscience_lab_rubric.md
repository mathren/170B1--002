## Detailed criteria

### 1. Initial reflection — science vs. pseudoscience (2 pts)
*Prompt: What is science vs. pseudoscience, and why does the distinction matter?*
- **Full (2):** Accurately distinguishes science (testable, falsifiable, built on evidence) from pseudoscience (claims scientific legitimacy without following the method); gives a genuine, specific reason the distinction matters (e.g., avoiding being misled on health, money, or policy decisions).
- **Partial (1):** Distinguishes the terms but vaguely, or defines only one clearly; the reason it matters is generic.
- **No/Minimal (0):** Blank, off-topic, or shows no real grasp of the distinction.

### 2. Hypothesis & prediction, Steps 2–3 (1 pt)
*Prompt: Select "does" or "does not" [influence personality]; circle your sign; predict the experiment's outcome.*
- **Full (1):** Hypothesis selected, sign circled, and the prediction logically follows from the chosen hypothesis (e.g., "does work" → predicts correctly matching most/all 3 horoscopes; "does not work" → predicts near-random matching).
- **Partial (0.5):** Selections made but the prediction doesn't clearly follow from the stated hypothesis.
- **No (0):** Hypothesis or prediction missing.

### 3. Experimental data, Step 4 (2 pts)
*Prompt: Record horoscope picks per set, the revealed signs, personal tally, and the class-wide poll results.*
- **Full (2):** All fields filled in — 3 horoscope picks, 3 revealed signs, a personal correct-count, and the class-wide percentages for 0/1/2/3 correct.
- **Partial (1):** Personal results complete but class-wide data missing (or vice versa).
- **No (0):** Largely blank, or the self-reported tally doesn't match the picks/signs given.

### 4. Identifying *n* and *p* (2 pts)
*Prompt: What are n and p in this experiment?*
- **Full (2):** Correctly identifies **n = 3** (three independent horoscope-matching trials) and **p = 1/12** (one correct sign out of twelve possible). See the answer key below.
- **Partial (1):** One value correct, or the right idea with a small slip (e.g., gets p close but not exact).
- **No (0):** Both wrong, missing, or n/p are conceptually confused with something else (e.g., number of students in the class).

### 5. Binomial probability calculations (3 pts)
*Prompt: Compute P(0), P(1), P(2), and P(3) correct by chance.*
- **Full (3):** All four probabilities correctly computed from the binomial formula using their n and p (or correct method with only a trivial arithmetic slip). Bonus sanity check: the four values should sum to ~1.
- **Good (2):** Correct formula/setup for most values, with a couple of computational errors.
- **Partial (1):** Formula partially understood (e.g., missing the combinatorial term, or mixing up p and 1−p) but real effort to apply the method is visible.
- **No (0):** Missing, or no coherent attempt at the formula.

### 6. Comparing class results to the binomial prediction (2 pts)
*Prompt: Do your classmates' actual results follow the binomial distribution you calculated?*
- **Full (2):** Explicitly compares the class's real percentages to the calculated probabilities (numbers referenced, not just a vibe), with a reasonable read on the fit — noting that a small class will show some sampling noise is a sign of good statistical thinking, not a flaw.
- **Partial (1):** A comparison is attempted but stays vague ("pretty close") without citing either set of numbers.
- **No (0):** No comparison made, or the conclusion contradicts the student's own reported numbers.

### 7. Conclusion & hypothesis evaluation (2 pts)
*Prompt: What do you conclude about the efficacy of horoscopes? Does this support your original hypothesis?*
- **Full (2):** Conclusion is grounded in the actual data — typically, that results are consistent with random chance, so the test found no evidence horoscopes predict anything. Explicitly loops back to the Step 2 hypothesis and revises it if the data didn't support what was originally selected.
- **Partial (1):** States a conclusion but doesn't clearly connect it to the student's own numbers, or skips revisiting the hypothesis when the data called for it.
- **No (0):** No conclusion, or one that disregards the data entirely.

### 8. "Just a theory" misconception (2 pts)
*Prompt: Give a modern example of a scientific theory wrongly dismissed as "just a theory."*
- **Full (2):** Names a real, relevant example (evolution, climate change, germ theory, the Big Bang, etc.) and correctly explains that "theory" in science means a rigorously tested explanatory framework, not a hunch.
- **Partial (1):** Names an example, but the explanation of *why* the "just a theory" framing is a misconception is thin or missing.
- **No (0):** No example, or the answer itself treats "theory" as synonymous with "guess."

### 9. Classifying astrology (2 pts)
*Prompt: How easy is it to compare horoscope predictions to your lived experience? Is astrology science or pseudoscience?*
- **Full (2):** Notes that horoscopes are worded vaguely/generically enough to fit almost anyone's day, making them hard to falsify — and uses that, plus the lab's own results, to classify astrology as pseudoscience.
- **Partial (1):** Reaches the correct classification but the reasoning is thin, or the reasoning is solid but the final classification doesn't match it.
- **No (0):** Missing, or calls astrology a science without engaging the falsifiability/vagueness problem.

### 10. Two more pseudoscience examples (2 pts)
*Prompt: Name two more examples of pseudoscience and explain why each qualifies.*
- **Full (2):** Two valid, distinct examples (e.g., homeopathy, numerology, crystal healing, tarot reading, ancient-astronaut theory, flat-Earth claims) each with accurate reasoning tied to a lack of testability or evidence.
- **Partial (1):** Only one valid example, or the "why" is missing/weak for both.
- **No (0):** Missing, or the examples given aren't actually pseudoscientific (e.g., naming an actual, well-supported scientific theory).

## Answer key for the quantitative section (Step 5)

Standard version of this exercise — 3 independent horoscope sets, 12 possible signs each:

- **n = 3, p = 1/12 ≈ 0.083**
- P(0 correct) = (11/12)³ ≈ **77.0%**
- P(1 correct) = 3(1/12)(11/12)² ≈ **21.0%**
- P(2 correct) = 3(1/12)²(11/12) ≈ **1.9%**
- P(3 correct) = (1/12)³ ≈ **0.06%**

These sum to 100% — a good check to have students run themselves. If a section actually presented a different number of horoscopes per set, p becomes 1/(number of options); grade the student's *method* against whatever n and p they were given in class, not against these exact figures.

This is a classic classroom demonstration, and real class data almost always tracks this prediction closely — most students land in the "0 correct" bucket at close to the predicted ~77% rate. That's the expected, correct takeaway for criterion 7: the experiment found no evidence of predictive power beyond chance.

## General grading notes

- **Group work, individual grade:** identical numbers within a group are normal, not a red flag — look for individual voice in the written-response sections instead.
- **Line limits** ("max 3–4 lines") exist to encourage concision; don't penalize a student for running slightly over if the content is on target.
- **Arithmetic vs. conceptual errors:** a wrong final number from a correctly-applied binomial formula (item 5) should lose at most a point, not the whole category — the concept, not the arithmetic, is the learning objective.
