Naked eye observations

Every night, you can see in a long-exposure image, the apparent motion due to the Earth rotation:

The star around which every other star seems to turn around is Polaris, the north star. It actually only coincidentally is close to the direction pointed at by Earth's rotation rate! This star is not even particularly bright, but identifying it helps finding the direction of North, which is useful for navigation.

It can be found identifying this asterism (which is a "piece of a constellation") in the sky

This constellation was also used as a "test" to see if your eyesight was sufficiently good to be an astronomer in hellenistic times (pre-telescopes!). The test was to find among the 7 stars which is a visual binary, i.e., two separate distinguishable sources.

You can try to do this tonight – it should be possible only with perfect vision.

The second star in the handle is actually two stars, Mizar and Alcor (the latter from the Arabic meaning "hard one to see"). Nowadays, with telescopes we see that Mizar is actually a "binary of binaries", i.e. four stars organized as two orbiting pairs, in turn orbiting each other, and Alcor may be a triple. This puts up to 7 individual stars in what most see only as one dot of light.

Each star rises towards the East and sets towards the West because of the West-to-East rotation of Earth, dragging you and your point of view around with it.

Celestial sphere

The celestial sphere is an idealized surface of a sphere of infinite radius centered on Earth over which the images of stars is projected.

stellarium web

The plane of the ecliptic is the plane traced by the orbit of the Sun as seen from Earth, or in other words, the plane of the orbit of the Earth around the Sun.

Angular distances

Because stars are at different distances along the radial direction of the celestial sphere, it is hard to tell their distance in 3D. But in projection on the celestial sphere, we can tell the angle between them. This is a measure of their apparent distance, but for example, it needs to be sufficiently large for two stars to be distinguishable!

Roughly, at arm's length, the angle of sky covered

  • by one finger ∼ 1 degree
  • by one fist ∼ 10 degrees
  • by a stretched hand ∼ 20 degrees

This can be used, for example, to measure the time to sun set: the Sun goes around ∼ 180 degrees every day (half a full circle, the other half happens during the night), which lasts about 12 hours, so it moves about ∼ 180/ 12 = 15 degrees/hour. When the Sun is about 3 fists height above the horizon, there is still about one hour before it sets (adjustments due to local topography, e.g., presence of a mountain, time of the year and latitude are needed to be precise).

Constellations

We have already referred indirectly to the concept of a "constellation". Many different cultures have created narratives about figures apparently drawn in the sky by stars projected on the celestial sphere.

Humans are biologically good at finding patterns, and patterns among the stars have been useful story-telling devices, and mnemonic tools.

However, the figures they are based on are just apparent and temporary. In fact brighter sources can be seen farther, and in 3D, members of the same constellation may be extremely far from each other! Moreover, stars in the Galaxy move around (orbiting the galaxy and because of their local peculiar velocity), with speeds that depend on their position. Given enough time (the relevant scale is 100s of millions of years, short compared to the age of the Sun!), the drawings will stretch, deform and disappear.

Moreover, our own point of view drifts in time: the Sun and solar system move. But while this takes millions of years.

But there are other smaller motion that happen faster that influence how the sky appears to us. The Earth's rotation "wobbles" (precess) with a period of 10s of thousands of years: what was at certain celestial coordinates 2000 years ago is not the same as today! The orbit of the Earth around the Sun also "wobbles", changing the plane of the ecliptic (i.e., the plane of the apparent motion of the Sun on the celestial sphere).

Modern astronomy still uses constellations as a useful reference point, for example many stars visible by naked eye can be referred to as <greek letter indicating brightness> + <greek name of constellation>. The letters are assigned in order of brightness (e.g., α Orionis = Betelgeuse is the brightest star in the constellation Orionis, ζ Ophiuchi is the 26th brightest in the constellation Ophiuchus). But each constellation is a specific patch of the celestial sphere defined as a polygon on it.

The zodiacal constellations are those that at a given point in the year are "hidden behind the Sun", that is the ones along the ecliptic plane.

Which constellations one sees varies with latitude on Earth (i.e., how far north/south one is), but not longitude (how far east/west one is). This is because Earth's rotation is in the West-to-East direction: different points at different longitudes will see the same patch of the sky at the zenith at different times (i.e., different phases of the Earth's rotation). Conventionally, longitude is measured starting from the Greenwich meridian (from the British empire).

Viceversa, the latitude determines what part of the sky is above the horizon at any given rotation. The simple example is: could you see Polaris from the South pole?

No: it is always below your feet at any time of the day! Instead in the Southern emisphere there is another notable asterism, the "Southern Cross" that acts as a "pointer" to a star close to the South pole.

As one moves along the surface of the Earth southward, what stays above the horizon changes.

  • North and south poles are projection of the Earth's rotation axis on the celestial sphere
  • The celestial equator is the intersection between the Earth rotational equator and the celestial sphere
  • zenith is the point right above on the celestial sphere, varies with the position of the observer (zenith at the north pole = north star)
  • nadir is the opposite point, never visible.

Seasons

Q: What is the reason for seasonal variations in weather patterns?

It is often misunderstood to be an effect of the small deformation (eccentricity) of the orbit of the Earth around the Sun which brings it closer/farther, but that is incorrect. If it were so, there would not be an anti-synchrony between Northern and Southern emisphere.

The correct reason is because of the inclination of Earth's rotation axis with respect to the ecliptic plane.

Since the Earth's rotation axis maintains it's inclination of ∼ 23 degrees w.r.t. the direction perpendicular to the plane of its orbit around the Sun, depending on whether an emisphere is pointing "towards" the Sun or "away" from it, it will receive Sun light at a different angle and for a different amount of time.

The change in daily energy input from the Sun causes the atmosphere and Earth below it to be "forced" differently, creating different climate conditions along the year.
