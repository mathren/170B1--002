Fermi problem: how many stars in the Milky way?

Enrico Fermi was a physicist in the 20th century known for being the last great theorist and experimentalist. One of the things he excelled in and that is now named after him are so-called "Fermi problems": these are questions to which it is possible to get to a reasonable answer (say within a factor of ∼10×) with only few assumptions. The key is to break down the question into manageable components.

The size of the Milkyway is $R$∼10000pc=10 kpc=104 pc, so it has a volume \(V\sim R^{3} \sim (10^{4})^{3}=10^{12}\) pc3. Consider the distance from the Sun to the nearest star, $d$∼4 ly∼1 pc. So on average we can estimate a star occupies about \(d^{3}\) of volume in the Galaxy, and the number of star to be the Volume of the Galaxy divided the volume for each star, \(N\simeq V/ d^{3} = 10^{12} pc^{3}^{}/(1 pc)^{3} = 10^{12}\).

There are about 1012 = 1 000 000 000 000 = 1 Trillion stars in the Milky way Galaxy, which is roughly the correct answer!

N.B.: Chose the right units (pc = parsecs) to keep the calculations simple.

Not all these stars are visible: many are too faint, hidden behind clouds of interstellar dust, in too crowded areas to stand out, etc.

Observations as a time-machine

This is a consequence of the finite speed of light c≅3×1010 cm s-1 This is experimentally constant in vacuum under all conditions, and assuming this to be true, we can construct a theory of space-time, and gravity, called relativity (special and general).

You may not realize it, but every time you use the GPS on your phone, you are using relativistic corrections for the communication (at the speed of light) between your phone and satellites. Without these corrections, your GPS would be too inaccurate to be usable.
