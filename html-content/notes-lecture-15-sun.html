Composition

Cecilia Payne-Gaposhkin's PhD thesis in 1925 (only about 100 years ago) realized that the bulk of the mass of stars is made of Hydrogen and Helium. The key insight was that it doesn't take much of an element with many lines for it to imprint a strong signal in the spectrum: the most prominent lines do not indicate the elements that are most present in mass, but just those that have more transitions available in the visible spectrum. Instead, most of the mass is in light elements, unlike the rocky planets.

At the time, initially, famous astrophysicists (e.g., Russell!) rejected this idea: dominant paradigm was that there is no particular composition difference between the Earth and the Sun (something that reads absurd now after discussing solar system formation).

Mass

Measuring masses in space is difficult, much more than measuring time! We can leverage Kepler's laws to measure the mass of the Sun from the measure of the year and the knowledge of the Sun-to-Earth distance!

Because the Earth is much less massive than the Sun, \(M_{Earth}+M_{☉}\simeq M_{☉}\), the 3rd Kepler law gives:

\begin{equation} M☉ = \frac{4π2}{P2}\frac{a3}{G} \ \ ,

where except for the constants, we only need to know the Earth's period and the semimajor axis \(a\). We can even refine this estimate using more than one planet.

N.B.: Measuring time is always easier than measuring distances in astrophysics! Methods that allow to convert time measurements into distance measurements are important tools in every sub-field of astrophysics.

The result is \(M_{☉}\simeq 2\times 10^{33}\,\mathrm{g}\), which is often used as a unit of mass (instead of kg or pounds) for astrophysical objects.
