Reading: Chapter 9, 11, 12

Planetary science is the study of planets, moons, and minor-bodies in the Solar system (including Earth) and/or exoplanets (and exomoons!). These studies allow us to understand the physical processes that shape a planet (formation and evolution) and probing them in conditions different than the Earth, for example:

  • different chemistry
  • different irradiation from the Sun/host star
  • different mass and thus thermodynamic conditions (pressure, temperature, etc)

By performing comparative studies, planetary science can teach us a lot about our own planet that we cannot know otherwise.

Planetary geology

  • TOP HAT Q1

We have discussed how the Solar system formed, and how the architecture of the planets, the smaller bodies, and their orbits supports the "disk-mediated bottom-up" process. But this means that the planets themselves contain clues about their formation!

Much like we can learn a lot about what an object is made of from its shape, we can learn something about the interior of planets from their surfaces!

Even on Earth, the deepest hole ever dug to date is the Kola superdeep borehole, at an impressive 12 km depth. This is out of a radius of the planet of ∼ 6000 km! We literally only have scratched the surface, and we can learn a lot about our own planet comparing with other bodies!

The aim of today is to discuss the processes that shape the surface of planets. The physics is the same everywhere, although the specific conditions (size of the planet, composition, etc.) determine the astounding variation of landscapes in the Solar system described in more detail in the book.

Sources of energy for a planet

Planets receive on their "day-side" energy in the form of radiation from the Sun (or their host star for exoplanets). However, depending on the properties of the planet, this energy does not penetrate very deep (maybe 100s of km at best). While it is crucial in driving both weather (you know it's colder at night!) on the surface, irradiation from the host star is not the main source of energy in the inside of a planet. Nevertheless, on Earth geological activity (volcanoes, earthquakes, etc) is direct evidence that there is energy (the ability to "move things") stored there!

1. Residual thermal energy from formation

As planets accreted matter from the protoplanetary disk, typically through a disk because matter interacts with itself as it falls and conserves the total angular momentum, it also accretes thermal energy (the source is the gravitational potential energy of the infalling matter). Part of this residual accretion heat is still trapped in planets and acts as an energy source.

2. Differentiation

Imagine to pour oil in a glass of water, in a messy way, and then let it sit for a long time. The oil would progressively rise at the top, because it is less dense – there is a buoyancy pushing it up. This process releases some energy (it was effectively excess gravitational potential energy of the water temporarily above the oil).

Similar things can occur in a planet (or a star!), although the time to wait is literally geological (100s of millions to billions of years, as old as the Solar system!). This process of rearrangement is called differentiation, and is also a source of energy in a planet

3. Radioactive decay

Add the radioactive decay of material the planet is made of (tapping into the nuclear energy stored in the structure of those nuclei), and you have all three major sources of heat in a planets interior.

Energy transport

N.B.: The concept we introduce here to discuss the structure of planets are exactly the same to discuss the structure of stars.

The total energy in the interior of a planet discussed above ultimately produces the thermal emission of the planets, typically peaking in the infrared (the details depend on the temperature of the last layer of the planet from where photons can escape).

This means planets lose energy (or cool) from the surface: energy must be moving from the interior outwards. Roughly speaking, energy in a blob of matter can move in three main ways:

Conduction (electrons carry the energy)

  • TOP HAT Q2

Matter, especially if solid, doesn't have many moving parts to carry energy. The most movable are the electrons in the outskirts of molecules/atoms bound in crystals.

Energy transport by motion of electrons is called conduction. Metals are good at conducing heat because their electrons are typically quite "loose" and this is why they heat up evenly even if only one part is left in the Sun.

Radiation (photons carry the energy)

In a medium in which light can pass (remember that light has many wavelengths!), energy can be carried by photons. This is typically more important in stars and outer layers of planets than their interior.

Convection (bulk flow of matter carries the energy)

This is when energy is carried by bulk motion of matter: convection implies there s some stuff going up and some stuff going down, with one of the two sides carrying more (thermal) energy even if the mass cancels out. Bulk motion of matter, no net mass flux, but with an energy flux.

This is an instability meaning convection kicks in only if the other possible energy transport are insufficient.

A common misconception is that water boiling is exhibiting convection: that is not exactly correct. Water boiling is by definition a phase transition from liquid to gas, however, shortly before the phase transition occurs, it is true that conduction in the water is typically insufficient to carry the energy release at the bottom, and convective motion can be spotted as a small simmering of the water breaking down into small waves at the surface.

It's maybe easy to picture convection in gas (atmosphere) and liquid (water "simmering" just before it starts boiling with large bubbles), but in planetary interiors, even solid can participate in convection! This is a very slow motion (compared to for example the thermal velocities), but unstoppable!

Geological timescales are comparable to the age of the planets, that is the age of the Solar system, up to ∼4.5 Billion years. Many processes can take hundreds of millions of years to shape a planet.

The fact that even rock over long timescales behaves like a fluid! We can thus study the evolution of the surface of a planet to learn about their formation, evolution, and outer conditions!

Why are planets spheres?

If we wait geological times, in a planet, which by definition is self gravitating, fluid rock will shape itself to minimize the gravitational potential it has. In other words it will "fall on itself at the lowest possible height".

Gravity is a central force, only depends on \(r\)

\begin{equation} \mathbf{F_{1}} = \frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\mathbf{\hat{r}} \ \ , \end{equation}

so the shape that minimizes the energy is a sphere, the region of space equidistant from a point, the center.

For non self-gravitating bodies, e.g., small asteroids, the shape is determined by the detailed properties of the matter they are made of.

Small, non-self-gravitating, asteroid are effectively "piles of rubble" loosely held together by gravity and shaped by how the parts lock into each other, without gravity crushing down the parts into a nice spherical shape.

Different planets → different structures → different surfaces

Since heat storing scales with volume, but heat losses scale with surface:

\begin{equation} \mathrm{Energy\ loss} \propto \mathrm{Surface} = 4 \pi r^{2}\\ \end{equation} \begin{equation} \mathrm{Amount\ of\ energy \propto Volume} = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3} \ \ , \end{equation}

The larger r the smaller the Surface/Volume ∝ \(r^{-1}\) is, therefore larger planets hold on to more of their internal energy.

Small planets also have smaller mass and thus gravity: it is harder to hold on to their ever-evaporating atmosphere for long!

For each star, there is a range of distances defined as habitable if liquid water is possible at the surface of the planet. Too close and the surface will be too hot, too far from the star it would be too cold. In the Solar system, Earth, Mars and Venus are all in this region, and yet because of their surface evolution, only Earth does have liquid water at the surface presently. Geological and atmospheric process compound with the distance from the host star to determine the chemical and physical conditions at the surface!

Earth's structure

The Earth is a medium size rocky planet, with a fast rotation rate (once every 23h56m∼24h). It has an atmosphere (decaying exponentially, conventionally defined to be 100 km thick), and a surface (70% covered by water), a solid crust, a thick mantle liquid below some depth, and a core partially liquid.

Shaping the surfaces

Tectonics

Tectonic plates are slabs of solid rock "floating" over the mantle of a planet, and slowly drifting, bumping in each other, sinking, and rising. This is driven from below by convection in the mantle!

The boundaries between the plates are often areas of relative instability, with outflows in the form of volcanoes (great pacific ring of fire), earthquakes (notoriously, California or Nepal).

Whether tectonics occurs on bodies other than Earth can be studied in the Solar System. For instance, Moon missions have left radio detectors, which allow to measure its size very precisely and detect its shaking: the Moon being small, it has no tectonic (anymore) because it has already lost most of its formation residual energy.

Volcanism

When liquid pockets of magma are squeezed upwards, or just rise through rocks and pierce through the crust, a volcano forms. The example below is an eruption from mount Etna in Sicily (Italy) this year:

Depending on the pressure and composition of the magma (which becomes lava onces it is out in the surface), there are different morphologies of volcanoes even on a single planet like Earth (think of the linear volcanoes in Iceland versus monutains like Mount Vesuvius).

Volcanism is not unique to Earth, in their history other planets and even moons have had/have volcanic activity!

Note that Io is a moon with radius ∼ half the Earth's radius: it's fairly small! Thus, as discussed above, it should have lost most of its formation heat – what does power its volcanic activity then? The key is how close it is to Jupiter: tides slosh the likely liquid magma ocean under its crust and give it energy (so called tidal heating)!

Erosion

By water (including in solid form: ice glaciers!) and other atmospheric agents. It of course requires a planet to have (or at least have had) an atmosphere for this to occur.

Impact cratering

We have seen how gravitational dynamics between N>3 bodies plays a chaotic role in the solar system evolution. Minor bodies in the solar system are often perturbed and knocked off their long-held mildly elliptical orbit, to become space projectiles on very eccentric ellipses, or even parabolae/hyperbolae. Occasionally these can hit something else.

Earth's atmosphere acts as a shield: most of these objects heat up due to friction with the air, and consume themselves becoming a "shooting star". Some hit the ground as meteors. When this occurs this can leave a crater!

  • TOP HAT Q3

The Moon is small so it managed to lose its internal heat, and let its atmosphere escape, so more craters form there and they don't weather down very much. The same argument applies to Mercury!

From the way craters degrade in time, we can age date them, and looking at areas of the moon that are rarely hit (dominated by old-looking craters) we can determine when most of these were formed.

It turns out this is likely roughly when the gas was cleared from the protoplanetary disk by the Sun, resulting in a decrease in the mass of the disk, and a re-arrangement of many orbits, causing also migration of the gas giants and including many comets from the Oort cloud. The decreased total mass and changes in position of the biggest objects (excluding the Sun) cause a re-arrangement of the orbits of the minor bodies, and put many of those in route towards the inner solar system. And we know from looking at the distribution of age of craters on the Moon!

Major impacts are rare today, the solar system is mature and most of the violent orbital perturbations have ceased (because they mostly ran out of stuff to push in the Sun or out of the solar system). A recent spectacular example was the comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 impact with Jupiter in 1992:

Despite the spectacular impact, because Jupiter is a gas giant, its "surface" is not solid (it's defined by the maximum depth at which photons can penetrate, but there is no solid material there, the gas blocks the photons), so we don't expect it to have formed craters.

The role of collisions in Earth's history

Earth has also been subject to "bombardment" from minor bodies earlier in the Solar system evolution. We know of craters on Earth (one of the most recent one is in Arizona!), and we have already seen some consequences of the bombardment for the history of Earth:

  • major collision with Mars-sized body → creation of the Moon, peculiarly large, moving outwards, and with a ∼ half-earth-like and ∼ half non-earth-like composition
  • how most of the water arrived on Earth: it condensed far from the snow line in the outer solar system, and was brought to us by comets "panicked" by the change in the disk and thrown into orbits crossing the inner solar system.
  • extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs

The last one however is often misunderstood in popular culture. About 66 million years ago, at the transition between Cretaceous and Paleogene, about 75% of life forms on Earth went rapidly extinct. But rapidly has to be understood on ecological and geological terms: it is unlikely that this means minutes/hours/days! In fact, the hypothesis that this mass-extinction event comes from an impact was first suggested based on finding a layer of minerals (iridium) all over the surface of the world. This mineral forms often in violent impacts between rocks (which provide the energy to reshuffle the crystal structures at molecular/atomic level), and the fact we find it all over the surface of the Earth suggests that the hashes from such collision spread globally on the surface of the Earth.

This suggests that the impact lifted so much material to cause a change in the global climate, resulting in an overall cooling of the Earth, massive death of plants, which disrupted the entire food chain leading to the mass extinction especially of large animals.

N.B.: Remember the resonances in the orbit of Mars or Jupiter and the asteroids (small integer ratio of the orbital periods)? These also act as barriers preventing asteroids from crossing them and "protecting" the inner solar system from further bombardments. Only few rare asteroids manage to cross and reach us!

Magnetosphere

What about magnetic-fields? This question is almost a running joke among astrophysicists, because magnetic fields are ubiquitous when discussing ionized gas (i.e., freely moving charged particles), but typically complicate significantly the dynamics of the system.

Much like velocity is defined in a reference frame, and depending on the observer's relative velocity to it an object can be moving or static, magnetic fields and electric fields are related like motion and not moving depending on the observer. The bottom line is that magnetic fields in planetary context are generated by electric currents.

Thus, for a planet to have a magnetic field it requires a source of energy, and a mechanism to generate currents, for example fast rotation! This is not enough, there should be something that can carry the current, like liquid metal.

As we have seen the residual heat from formation (gravitational collapse), and to a smaller degree the radioactive decay of trapped heavy unstable elements act as source of heat in planets. This causes convection (transport of heat by macroscopic motion). If rotation is fast and the planet is not "too stiff", meaning there is a liquid layer, rotation "twists" the convection generating currents which make a magnetic field.

The Earth meets these conditions, its magnetic field roughly has a North-South direction (that's because Earth's rotation is involved in generating it!), so it can be used to navigate by compass.

On a planetary scale, it protects the Earth from the solar wind: this is a stream of high energy protons investing us. Protons are charged and a moving electric charge is deflected by magnetic fiels (Lorentz force). This visualization shows the pressure around the Earth as a function of the impinging wind (mostly protons) constantly escaping from the Sun.

N.B.: We can literally see what was happening at the surface of the Sun 8 light-minutes ago, because that is the time it takes a photon traveling at the speed of light to cover the Sun-Earth distance (1 au). The protons of the solar wind are more slower than photons, so we can use this to help predicting some of the solar wind variability.

N.B.: Mars, slightly smaller than Earth, has lost enough of its formation heat and has no molten core anymore: this means it has lost the ability of carrying currents and generating a significant magnetic field. This makes its surface more exposed to stellar winds, and probably contributed to the evaporation of its atmosphere.

The magnetic field of a planet can accelerate particles from the solar wind (typically protons) and make them smash into molecules in their atmosphere, causing aurorae. This website shows forecast predictions on the probability of aurorae based on the Sun's activity.

Any planet with a magnetic field can have aurorae! In Jupiter case, the aurorae interact with the (close by) Moon Io, which is volcanic and its volcanoes can eject charged particles which can be channeled by Jupiter's magnetic field towards the magnetic poles where aurorae most likely form.

What if there is no "surface"?

So far you probably imagined surfaces as something roughly solid. However, some planets do not have any solid surface at all: the gas giants! However, we can think of a "surface of last photon scattering", or in other words, the radius above which photons can freely escape. This is just a statement on the photons propagation, not the state of matter, which can be in any phase it wants!

Although gas giants have formed starting from solid planetesimal cores, these may have since melted/vaporized under the extreme pressure of all the gas they have accreted. This is usually still debated, because it depends on the properties of matter that is under extreme pressure (the weight of the huge planets), cold, and with the specific chemical composition of the giant planets.

Even if these planets are mostly or completely in gas phase, you should not imagine them as a fluffy cloud that it is easy to go through: when the Galileo space-craft plunged into Jupiter, it dove only down to 200km from the surface before being crushed by the pressure of the gas.

As we will discuss in more detail when dealing with stellar structure, what holds planets (and stars) up against their own weight and prevent them from collapsing to a single point is the pressure gradient, that is the change in pressure with depth. The gas at the bottom is compressed, and has higher pressure than the gas above, and the difference in pressure between two layers is exactly the balancing the weight of that layer.

Another force that shapes the gas giants is the centrifugal force. This is an apparent force in the non-inertial frame co-rotating with the planet (which corresponds in an inertial non-rotating frame to the centripetal force that keeps the velocity accelerating and changing direction in the rotating motion). Gas giants typically spin fast enough that they are visibly deformed from the spherical shape expected for planets.
